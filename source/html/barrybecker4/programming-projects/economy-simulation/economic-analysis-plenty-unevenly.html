A field report from an artificial economy · Extreme AI

Plenty, Unevenly

Assume the strong case: AI that can do almost every task, adopted within a few years, with compute that keeps getting cheaper. What happens to well-being, wealth and inequality? 1,760 simulated 20-year histories, thousands of simulated households each.

Extreme AI as the base case The bottom 40% The top 1% Dividends, taxes, ownership
Built on Barry Becker’s agent-based economy simulator · experiments and write-up by an AI analyst · October 2026

The question is not whether we get richer

Under the simulator’s Extreme AI preset, real output grows about 125-fold in 20 years, against 1.4× with no AI. Jobs fade into the background: by year 20 about half of households have no job and wages are under 1% of household income (0.4%). So this report doesn’t ask about employment. It asks who owns the abundance, whether the typical and the poorest households share in it, and which rules decide that.

Where the numbers come from

  • Runs 20 seeds per setting, 500 households, 50 firms, 3 banks, 240 months, ordinary central-bank money. Figures are medians across seeds; ranges give the 10th–90th percentile. “Year 20” is the last 12 months.
  • Spot check Key settings rerun at the app’s full scale (4,000 households, 200 firms, 4 banks), 4 seeds each.
  • Live app buttons open the deployed simulator with the same settings and 10 seeds. Press Run.

How to read “×”

  • Consumption and wealth are in real terms, as multiples of today’s median household (month 0 of the same run). “28×” means 28 times what the typical household consumes today.
  • Household-level figures (bottom 40%, top 1%, the three households) come from yearly snapshots of every simulated household, averaged over 12 months.

02
Inequality

How unequal, how fast

Wealth concentrates quickly once adoption takes off. The wealth Gini starts at 0.57, reaches 0.71 by year 5 and 0.91 by year 20. The top 1% go from about 8% of household wealth to 75%. The bottom 40% hold 2.6%.

Adoption speed changes the timing, not the destination. With the adoption midpoint at year 15 instead of year 3, the Gini passes 0.8 in year 14 instead of year 7, and still ends at 0.93.

Ranks stop meaning much below the top. Without AI, a household’s wealth rank in year 1 predicts its rank in year 20 almost perfectly (rank correlation 0.99). Under Extreme AI it predicts nothing (0.01). Everyone outside the top few lives mostly on the same dividend, so their wealth sits close together and small differences reshuffle the order.

Takeaway

Inequality under Extreme AI is a step change that arrives within about seven years of the adoption midpoint. Slowing adoption delays it but doesn’t prevent it.

03
Who gets rich

The winners own skill-linked capital, not agents

In the simulator, firm profits and equity are shared out by skill, and the tilt toward skill steepens as AI raises firm capacity. The “AI ownership concentration” slider sets how fast it steepens. Under Extreme AI, profits become almost all of household income, so the tilt decides nearly everything. The 1% most-skilled households end up holding 65% of all household wealth, against 7% with no AI.

Owning the AI agents themselves barely matters. Agent accounts hold a negligible share of wealth in these runs; the money flows through firm profits instead. Whether 10% or 95% of households own agents, and whether each owner has 2 or 50, the top 1% hold 74–75% of wealth.

05
Policy for the bottom 40%

The dividend decides, up to a cliff

Two dials matter most. The AI dividend is the share of the AI-produced slice of GDP paid out equally to every household (the app’s “UBI share”, default 25%). The ownership tilt sets how strongly profits skew toward high-skill households as AI grows (default 0.8). We crossed both under Extreme AI.

With no dividend, the bottom 40% consume about 1.1× today’s median after 20 years, against 0.6× in year 1, while output grows 118-fold. The default 25% dividend lifts them to 28×, and 50% to 47×. Prices stay calm up to a 50% dividend (3.3% a year at the default tilt). Between 50% and 75% there is a cliff:

The best mix for the bottom 40% at 5% inflation or less is ownership tilt 0.1, dividend 50%: the bottom 40% consume 57× today’s median (range 56–58×), the median household 74×, at 2.8% inflation. The top 1% hold 18% of wealth instead of 75%, and the median household’s well-being index is 4.01 vs 3.22. Pushing the dividend to 100% lifts the bottom 40% further, to 77×, but at 52% inflation a year.

Takeaway

Pay households about half of the AI slice and keep profit claims broad. That gives the bottom 40% 2.0 times what the defaults give them, at low inflation. Beyond about 60%, the extra money mostly turns into higher prices.

06
Taxes, budgets, pie and slices

Size and fairness are set by different dials

The simulator’s income tax is flat, and any budget surplus is rebated in proportion to income. So raising the income tax doesn’t move money down the ladder. At a 35% rate, the top 1% end up with more of the wealth (84% vs 75%), and the bottom 40%’s consumption barely moves (26× vs 28×). More public spending helps a little: with the government buying 35% of output, the bottom 40% get 32×. Central-bank settings change little on their own.

Is there a trade-off between total wealth and fairness? Not in output. Across every policy mix under Extreme AI, real output ends at 106–137× start, and the mixes with large dividends sit at the top of that range. Only shrinking the state costs much output: 106× for the small-state budget, 115× with government buying 10%. Average real household wealth tells a similar story: 37× start under the best mix and 38× under the defaults. It is lower in the high-dividend mixes where prices rise fast (24× at a 75% dividend, 31% inflation) and under high flat taxes (15× at 50%), which are less fair, not more.

Takeaway

Technology sets the size of the pie; the dividend and ownership rules set the slices. In these runs, fairer slices didn’t shrink the pie.

07
Technology levers

Smarter agents grow every slice; cheaper ones already are

The capability ceiling caps how much bought compute can raise a firm’s capacity (1 = it can double a firm). It scales everyone’s outcome almost in proportion. The bottom 40% go from 14× with a ceiling of 0 (compute adds nothing) to 28× at 1 and 68× at 4. The wealth Gini hardly moves (0.93 to 0.90). The singularity mix (ceiling 4, faster capability growth, 12-month cost half-life) adds the dividend story on top: with a broad tilt and 75% dividend the bottom 40% reach 167×, but at 30% inflation.

Agent prices. Under Extreme AI the price of agents falls fast enough that the pace hardly matters. With a cost half-life anywhere from 6 to 240 months, the bottom 40% get 27.6–28.0×. Price matters only when agents start expensive (half a wage) and fall slowly with no energy breakthrough: then the bottom 40% get 15×. The month-48 energy step (a fusion-like cut in compute cost) restores the full result (28×).

Recessions. With large, frequent shocks (30% chance a year, size 0.2), the median household’s worst 12-month consumption drop grows by 3.0 points under Extreme AI against 9.2 points with no AI (paired by seed). With jobs no longer the main income source, shocks barely reach household consumption.

Takeaway

Falling agent prices and rising capability make the pie bigger for everyone, but they don’t change who gets which slice.
