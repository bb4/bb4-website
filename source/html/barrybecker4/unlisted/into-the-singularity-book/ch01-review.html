Chapter 1

The call came at 6:48 on a Tuesday, while Daniel was explaining to a Slack channel why the overnight deploy had eaten itself at 2 a.m. He’d been up since five. He was, for what it was worth, good at this particular part of the job — the actual diagnosis, tracing cause through six services and a message queue until you found the one bad assumption everything else had trusted — and there was a small, private satisfaction in that goodness that had nothing to do with anyone noticing it. He had the incident doc open in one window and a coffee going cold in the other hand. When his phone lit up with MARA on the lock screen he almost let it go to voicemail. Mara calling before seven on a weekday was never about anything good, and he did not, at that exact moment, have the bandwidth for whatever it was.

He answered anyway. Some habits outlast their reasons.

“It’s Dad,” Mara said, and then didn’t say anything else for a second, which was so unlike her that he knew before she got the rest of the sentence out.

He asked the wrong first question — when instead of how — and she answered it anyway, flat, like she was reading off a form. Sometime before six that morning. On the shop floor, by the mill he still ran himself even though he had three guys who could run it for him. Ray Delgado found him when he came in to open up and called it in. The paramedics had come, and there had been nothing for them to do. The county would want to talk to Daniel and Mara both about arrangements. Mara had already booked a flight into Columbus for Thursday and wanted to know if he wanted her to see if there was a seat on the same one.

“Yeah,” Daniel said. “Yeah, text me the flight.”

“You okay?”

“I don’t know yet,” he said, which was the only honest thing available to him, and they both let it sit there for a second before she said she had to go make more calls and hung up.

He put the phone down on the desk, next to the mouse, and looked at the incident doc, which was still open, cursor blinking in a field where he’d been about to write root cause: race condition in the batch scheduler, mitigated, postmortem to follow. The words meant nothing to him now in a way they hadn’t ten seconds earlier. He closed the laptop. He did not remember deciding to do that; his hand just did it, the way it silenced his phone during meetings without asking him first.

Outside his window the Austin morning was already going white and hot at the edges, sprinklers ticking across the courtyard, somebody’s dog barking at somebody’s delivery. None of it adjusted itself to account for what he’d just been told. He found that obscurely offensive, and then found it obscurely comforting, and then didn’t examine either reaction too closely, because examining things too closely was what he did with code, not with this.

His father’s name was Warren, and he had run Kessler Machine out of the same building on Route 9 for thirty-one years. Daniel had not been inside it, he realized while booking a rental car, in something like fourteen months. He’d told himself it was distance and work and the general gravity failure that pulled adult children away from the towns their parents stayed in. That was true as far as it went. It also wasn’t the whole of it, and he knew that too, in the way you know a fact you’ve decided not to spend time with.

His parents had split up when he was eighteen, quietly and without much drama, the way two people do once they’ve spent a few years failing to explain themselves to each other and finally stop trying. His mother had moved to Cincinnati not long after; Daniel called her more than he called his father, which had never felt like a decision so much as a fact he’d noticed after it was already true. His father had gone back to school once, decades before any of that — a graduate program in mathematics, cryptography specifically, back when that meant RSA and key exchange and not currency — and dropped out in his mid-twenties to come home and keep Kessler Machine running for his own father. He’d never once called it a sacrifice, and never once, in Daniel’s hearing, brought it up again. It was just a fact about him, filed away with the other facts nobody in the family had ever gotten a straight answer about.

Bitcoin, they’d all gathered eventually, had gotten under his skin around 2013 — an old friend from a machinists’ union local who’d gotten him started, back when it was still cheap enough, thirty dollars or so a coin, that a machinist’s spare cash could buy a few dozen at a time. Most men who bought in that year forgot about it or panic-sold within eighteen months. Warren hadn’t been most men; he’d actually understood what he was looking at, Daniel suspected without ever quite being told, in a way that traced straight back to the graduate program he’d walked away from. He got quieter about it as the years went on, not louder — longer hours at the shop, evenings on a laptop in the back office doing something he called “keeping an eye on things” that nobody in the family, including apparently his own wife, ever got a straight answer about. Daniel had never been sure how much of the marriage’s slow unraveling was that, specifically, versus everything else two people accumulate over twenty-some years, but he’d never fully believed it was nothing. Warren didn’t explain himself, then or later. He’d just, every so often, hand Daniel an envelope at Christmas with a printed sheet of numbers in it and say don’t do anything with this, just hold it, like he was passing along a family recipe nobody had asked for.

Daniel had held it. He’d never asked what it added up to. That felt, this morning, like one more conversation he’d let slide past him on the theory that there would obviously be time later.

The last time he’d actually stood on that shop floor — not driven past it, not seen it in a photo Mara forwarded from the Route 9 Facebook group, but stood on the oil-dark concrete and felt the mill’s vibration come up through his shoes — was fourteen months ago, over a long weekend he’d almost canceled twice. His father had wanted to show him a boring-and-facing machine he’d bought used off a shop going under two counties over, proud of the price the way another man might be proud of a car. Daniel had nodded in the right places and asked one real question, and his father’s face had opened up for exactly as long as it took to answer it, then closed again when Daniel’s phone buzzed with a work message he checked without meaning to. His father hadn’t said anything about that. He’d just gone quiet in the specific way he’d been quiet since 2013, and they’d gone to the diner on Fourth Street for dinner and talked about nothing, and Daniel had flown home Sunday night telling himself there’d be a longer visit at Christmas, a better one, a real one. There hadn’t been. Christmas had turned into a phone call and a padded envelope.

He called his manager to say he’d be out the rest of the week, family emergency, and Nadia was kind about it in the brisk, slightly relieved way of someone who has a sprint review in twenty minutes and one less thing to worry about now that this is handled. She told him to take whatever he needed. He believed she meant it. He also noticed, in a part of his mind that apparently did not know how to stop noticing things even now, that she said whatever you need and not however long you need, and that the difference was probably nothing.

He flew out Thursday morning as planned. The funeral was set for Saturday — the county coroner’s office moved on its own schedule, and there were things Mara called “logistics” that Daniel understood to mean the funeral home needed a deposit before it would commit to a date. He spent Wednesday night making lists he didn’t need to make, because making lists was a thing he could do with his hands and his attention that produced a small, false sense of progress. Suit, dry-cleaned, hanging on the closet door already, so that wasn’t even a real item. Flight, booked. Out-of-office reply, set. He stood in his kitchen at eleven p.m. rewriting the out-of-office message four times because none of the wording seemed right — limited availability felt like a lie, bereavement leave felt like something he was performing for an audience of nobody — before he picked one and stopped looking at it.

Somewhere around midnight he thought about who else he should call — not to tell them anything, just to have said it out loud to somebody who wasn’t already inside the week with him — and came up mostly empty. There was Elena, except that felt like reopening something he’d spent two years carefully not reopening, at exactly the wrong moment to test whether he still meant it. There were a handful of names from grad school he texted maybe twice a year, birthday-shaped contact that didn’t survive contact with an actual crisis. There was Wei, technically, if the time difference and Wei’s increasingly strange radio silence over the last few months hadn’t made the last two calls feel like leaving messages in a bottle. Mostly there was work, which had become the shape of most of his days without his ever deciding that it should, and now the work was the thing calling an HR name he didn’t recognize into a Friday morning meeting. He didn’t want to follow that thought any further than he had to. He went to bed instead. He did not sleep especially well.

Thursday afternoon, an hour after he landed, sitting in the rental car in the airport garage with the AC running before he’d even pulled out of the space, his phone buzzed with a calendar invite: Team Update — Required. Ten a.m. his time Friday. No agenda. Nadia wasn’t on the invite. A name from HR he didn’t recognize was.

He knew what it was before Friday came, the way you know the shape of a message from the outline of the envelope. He’d watched it happen to three people on an adjacent team in the spring — Sanjay, who’d been there four years longer than Daniel had; a contractor named Beth who never even got a laptop-closing moment out of it, just found out over email between two other emails; a third guy whose name Daniel was faintly ashamed to realize he’d already half forgotten — each of them getting an identically worded invite with an identically blank agenda field. He’d told himself at the time that his team was different, that his numbers were good, that Meridian’s routing models needed a human in the loop for the messy exception cases the system couldn’t confidently resolve on its own — needed him, specifically, because he’d built half of that exception-handling logic himself. He believed that on Thursday night the way you believe things you have a strong incentive to believe.

The drive from the airport took an hour and forty minutes, most of it on a two-lane state route that went from strip malls to soybean fields to more strip malls, the kind of unbroken suburban geometry that made it hard to say exactly when Ohio started. Somewhere past the county line the fields gave way to a downtown built for three times its current population — a shuttered movie theater missing half its marquee letters, a hardware store, a diner Daniel recognized without wanting to, a bank branch that had become a vape shop that had become, per a taped-up paper sign, available for lease. He drove past Kessler Machine without meaning to slow down, and did anyway. The lot was empty except for Ray Delgado’s truck. The bay doors were shut. Someone — Ray, probably — had already taken down whatever had needed taking down, so there was nothing marking the building as different from how it had looked the last time Daniel had seen it, which felt like its own kind of wrong. A building should look different, he thought, after something like that happens inside it. This one just looked closed.

Friday morning he sat in his father’s kitchen in Millbrook — Mara had gotten in the night before and taken the guest room without asking, which was fine, he hadn’t wanted it — and joined the call from his laptop at the same table where he’d eaten breakfast as a kid, a hundred miles and one full day away from walking into a room with his father’s casket in it.

There were three people on the call: Nadia, silent, camera off; the HR name from the invite, a woman named Talia Brandt who spoke in the unhurried, over-enunciated cadence of someone who does this every week and has learned to make each instance sound like the first; and a slide, screen-shared, titled Q3 Organizational Efficiency Update.

She used the phrase twice. Efficiency gains. Once early, framing — “as part of a broader set of efficiency gains enabled by our platform investments” — and once at the end, softer, almost kind, like she thought repeating it might make it land as something other than what it was: “We know this is difficult, and we want you to know this decision reflects efficiency gains at the org level, not anything about your individual performance.”

Daniel had spent four years writing systems that told warehouse floor managers, in exactly that register, that a shift could run with two fewer people and the numbers would hold. He recognized the sentence structure the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else’s signature. Somewhere upstream of Talia Brandt, a dashboard had flagged his role — senior, expensive, redundant against a model that had gotten quietly better at the exception cases over the last two quarters. A decision that had once required a person to make it had instead required a person to deliver it. He almost said this out loud. He didn’t see the point.

“Do you have any questions?” Talia asked, in the tone of someone who has been trained to ask this and trained not to expect much of an answer.

“No,” Daniel said. His voice came out steadier than he felt entitled to. “I think I understand.”

The severance details would follow in an email, she said. Eight weeks, plus accrued PTO, plus a package that assumed — reasonably, correctly, in a way that made the whole thing feel even more like being handled by a system that had already modeled him — that he would not need it explained to him twice, that he was the kind of person who read the fine print himself.

Eight weeks. He did the math without meaning to, the way he did math on everything: rent in Austin, the lease he’d signed in the spring when the offer letter had made it feel safe to sign a longer one, the health insurance he’d need to replace before eight weeks ran out if he wanted to keep seeing his actual doctor instead of whichever one a marketplace plan assigned him. It would be fine. He had savings. He had, technically, whatever his father’s envelopes had turned into over the years, unopened, unspent, a number he’d never let himself compute, because computing it had always felt like betting on something he didn’t want to need. It would be fine. He made himself believe that with about the same effort it had taken to believe Meridian needed him specifically — enough to get through the next five minutes, and no guarantee further than that.

He closed the laptop. It made the exact same sound it had made three days earlier in Austin, plastic meeting plastic, a small definitive click, and for a second the two mornings sat on top of each other so precisely that he had to put both hands flat on the table to remember which one he was in.

Mara found him twenty minutes later still sitting there, laptop shut, coffee he hadn’t touched going cold in one of their mother’s old mugs, the one with the chip on the handle that none of them had ever thrown out.

“You look like you got hit by a truck,” she said, not unkindly. She had her own phone in her hand, thumb moving, some market thing she was tracking even here, even today, and he didn’t have it in him to resent her for it. It was just how she metabolized things. Everybody had a version of the laptop-closing habit.

“Lost my job this morning,” he said.

She stopped scrolling. “This morning morning?”

“Ten a.m. call. ‘Efficiency gains.’” He heard himself say it in Talia Brandt’s exact cadence and didn’t try to stop.

Mara set the phone face-down on the table, which from her was a significant gesture, and for once didn’t lead with a plan or a number or a piece of advice about what he should do next. She just pulled out the chair across from him and sat, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the first thing all week that had felt like the right amount of words.

“Tomorrow’s going to be bad,” she said eventually.

“Today wasn’t great either.”

“No.” She almost smiled. “It really wasn’t.”

Outside, the sky had gone the flat gray Ohio got before rain that never quite arrived on schedule. Somewhere in the house a pipe knocked once and went still, an old-house sound their mother used to explain away and their father never had, and neither of them moved to investigate it now. They just sat there a while longer, in a kitchen built for four that had held one for the last eleven years, letting the coffee finish going cold.

Upstairs, in a closet Daniel hadn’t opened yet, hung the suit he’d need in the morning, still creased from the garment bag. Down the hall, his father’s back office sat locked and dark, a laptop in a drawer that no one had turned on, a life’s worth of quiet, unexplained arithmetic waiting in it that nobody had asked about while there was still someone to ask. None of that was tonight’s problem. Tonight’s problem was smaller and larger at once: getting through the hours between now and a church at eleven a.m., two losses sitting side by side in him with no clear boundary between where one ended and the other began, and no system, however efficient, that had a model for what came after.
