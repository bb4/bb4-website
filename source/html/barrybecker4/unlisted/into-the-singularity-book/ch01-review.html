Chapter 1: The Double Blow

The call came at 6:48 a.m. on a Tuesday, while Daniel was explaining to a Slack instant message channel why the overnight deploy had eaten itself at 2 a.m. He’d been up since five. He was good at this part of the job — tracing cause through six services and a message queue until you found the one bad assumption everything else had trusted.

He had the incident document open and a coffee going cold. When Mara’s name lit up on an incoming call he almost let it go to voicemail. Mara calling before seven on a weekday was never about anything good.

He answered anyway.

“It’s Dad,” Mara said, and then didn’t say anything else for a second, which was so unlike her that he knew before she got the rest of the sentence out.

He asked the wrong first question — when instead of how — and she answered it anyway, flat. Sometime before six that morning. On the shop floor, by the mill he still ran himself. Ray Delgado found him when he came in to open up. The paramedics had come, and there had been nothing for them to do.

She’d booked a flight into Columbus for Thursday and wanted to know if he could get something that landed around the same time.

“Yeah. Text me the flight.”

“You okay?”

“I don’t know yet.”

He put the phone down and looked at the incident doc, cursor blinking where he’d been about to write root cause: race condition in the batch scheduler, mitigated, postmortem to follow. The words meant nothing now. He closed the laptop.

His father’s name was Warren, and he had run Kessler Machine out of the same building on Route 9 in Millbrook, Ohio for thirty-one years.

His parents had split up when he was eighteen, quietly. His mother, Joan, had moved to Cincinnati not long after. Daniel called her more than he called his father, and that had never felt like a decision. He’d just noticed, one day, that it was already true.

His father had gone back to school once, decades before any of that — a graduate program in cryptography. He’d dropped out in his mid-twenties to come home and keep Kessler Machine running for his own father.

Bitcoin had gotten under his skin around 2013. An old friend from a machinists’ union local had gotten him started, when it was still thirty dollars or so a coin. Most men who bought in that year forgot about it or panic-sold within eighteen months. Warren hadn’t. Daniel suspected he’d understood what he was looking at because of the graduate program he’d walked away from.

He got quieter about it as the years went on. Longer hours at the shop, evenings on a laptop in the back office. Warren didn’t explain himself. He’d just, every so often, hand Daniel an envelope at Christmas with a printed sheet of numbers in it and say don’t do anything with this, just hold it.

Daniel had held it. He’d never asked what it added up to.

The last time he’d stood on that shop floor was fourteen months ago. His father had wanted to show him a boring-and-facing machine he’d bought used, proud of the price the way another man might be proud of a car. Daniel had nodded in the right places and asked one real question, and his father’s face had opened while he answered. Then it closed again when Daniel’s phone buzzed with a work message he checked without thinking.

He’d flown home telling himself there’d be a longer visit at Christmas. There hadn’t been. Christmas had turned into a phone call and a padded envelope.

He called his manager to say he’d be out the rest of the week. Nadia told him to take whatever he needed. He noticed she said whatever you need and not however long you need.

The funeral was set for Saturday. He spent Wednesday night making lists he didn’t need, because lists gave his hands something to do.

Around midnight he thought about who else to call and came up mostly empty. There was Elena, except that meant reopening something he’d spent two years carefully not reopening. There was Wei, if the time difference and Wei’s increasingly strange radio silence hadn’t made the last two calls feel like talking into an empty line.

Thursday afternoon, sitting in the rental car in the airport garage with the AC running, his phone buzzed with a calendar invite: Team Update — Required. Ten a.m. his time Friday. No agenda. A name from HR he didn’t recognize was on it.

He knew what it was. The blank agenda was the tell.

He’d watched it happen to three people on an adjacent team in the spring. Sanjay, who’d been there four years longer than Daniel. A contractor named Beth who found out over email. He’d told himself his team was different, that his numbers were good, that Meridian’s routing models needed a human in the loop for the messy exception cases.

The drive from the airport took an hour and forty minutes. Somewhere past the county line the fields gave way to a downtown built for three times its current population. A shuttered movie theater. A hardware store. A diner Daniel recognized without wanting to.

He drove past Kessler Machine without meaning to slow down, and did anyway. The lot was empty except for Ray’s truck. After something like that, a building should look different. This one just looked closed.

Friday morning he sat in his father’s kitchen in Millbrook. A hundred miles and one full day away from walking into a room with his father’s casket in it.

There were three people on the call. Nadia, silent, camera off. The HR woman, Talia Brandt. And a slide titled Q3 Organizational Efficiency Update.

She used the phrase twice. Efficiency gains. Once early, framing. And once at the end, softer: “We want you to know this decision reflects efficiency gains at the org level, not anything about your individual performance.”

Somewhere upstream of Talia Brandt, a dashboard had flagged his role: senior, expensive, redundant against a model that had gotten quietly better at the exception cases over the last two quarters.

“Do you have any questions?”

“No. I think I understand.”

Eight weeks severance. He did the math: Austin rent, the lease he’d just signed, health insurance. He had savings. He had, technically, whatever his father’s Christmas envelopes had turned into over the years, unopened, unspent.

He closed the laptop. For a second the two mornings sat on top of each other so precisely that he had to put both hands flat on the table to remember which one he was in.

Mara found him twenty minutes later still sitting there, coffee going cold in one of their mother’s old mugs.

“You look like you got hit by a truck.”

“Lost my job this morning.”

She stopped scrolling. “This morning morning?”

“Ten a.m. call. ‘Efficiency gains.’”

Mara set the phone face-down on the table, and for once didn’t lead with a plan or a number. She just pulled out the chair across from him and sat, and for a while neither of them said anything.

“Tomorrow’s going to be bad.”

“Today wasn’t great either.”

“No. It really wasn’t.”

Outside, the sky had gone that flat Ohio gray that promised rain that never quite arrived. They sat there a while longer, in a kitchen built for four that had held one for the last eleven years, letting the coffee finish going cold.

Upstairs hung the suit he’d need in the morning. Down the hall, his father’s back office sat locked and dark, a laptop in a drawer that no one had turned on. None of that was tonight’s problem. Tonight he just had to get from here to a church at eleven a.m., with two losses sitting in him and no clear line between where one ended and the other began.
