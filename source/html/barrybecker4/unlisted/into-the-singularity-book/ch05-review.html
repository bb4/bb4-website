Chapter 5: Almost in Passing

It had started months earlier, easy enough to miss. A coffee mug that slipped half an inch in her left hand. A stretch of keyboard where her fingers landed a beat behind where she meant to put them. A pen she’d dropped twice in one product review.

She noticed the pattern the way she noticed everything: thoroughly, and late.

The neurologist had a way of saying “consistent with” that Elena had already learned to hear as a euphemism doing quiet, heavy lifting. Consistent with, rather than is, the same way an incident report says a system behaved unexpectedly instead of broke.

Dr. Alvarez said it twice this time, once about the EMG — a nerve test that measured how her muscles answered — and once about the reflex testing.

“Consistent with early ALS,” Dr. Alvarez said eventually — a disease that slowly takes away the body’s ability to move. “I want to be honest that there’s still a confirmation process. But I’d rather you hear the working diagnosis from me now than watch me dance around it for another six weeks of tests.”

“Thank you,” Elena said. She appreciated competence. She had built a career on it.

It turned out that didn’t stop being true just because the competence in question was now aimed at the wiring in her own left hand — which had spent four months forgetting, in small increments, how to hold a coffee mug without help from the right one.

She asked the questions she’d prepared, and wrote the answers in the notes app she used for everything else in her life — grocery lists, one-line performance reviews. Compartmentalizing a diagnosis into the same interface as buy oat milk was either extremely healthy or a warning sign.

She told three people that week, in descending order of how much she trusted them not to immediately treat her like something breakable: her sister, her closest friend from grad school, her manager.

Her sister cried for exactly as long as it took Elena to say “please don’t do the thing where you fall apart before I do,” and then, admirably, didn’t. She asked the practical question underneath all the others: “what do you need from me, actually, not the movie version.”

Such a precise thing to ask. I don’t know yet, Elena said. I’ll tell you when I do.

Then, on a Thursday night with a glass of wine, she told a fourth person who didn’t count as a person so much as an inbox: a recruiter from the Bridge Program, whose unexpected email she had ignored twice already.

The Bridge Program was research on a device that read signals from the brain, so people with ALS — and others who could still think after the body stopped cooperating — could still talk and write. The first part of the study tried to preserve that. Elena understood the goal and found it almost comfortingly modest.

What she noticed, reading further, was a second consent form buried in an extra section at the end. It described another part of the study, something about “continuity of recorded cognitive state beyond the interface’s communicative function.”

She read that sentence four times. Put in ordinary words, it sounded like they wanted to save a copy of a person’s mind, not just help them talk. She did not know if that was even possible. She suspected she would eventually be the kind of person this was written for.

She said yes to the first part by return email on Friday. She did not sign the second form. She put a note in her phone — ask about Appendix C again in three months — right underneath buy oat milk.

She found out about Warren on a Wednesday, a few days after her own diagnosis, from a LinkedIn post. A mutual former coworker’s thinking of Daniel and Mara this week with a photo of a machine shop Elena had never seen.

She held off texting him for most of a day, long enough to make sure it wasn’t just reflex.

They’d met at a routing startup, four years into her career of writing documentation nobody read until something broke. Daniel had been the engineer whose commit messages she’d spent a year turning into sentences an actual customer support rep could use. They had been almost something, once. Not the vague kind. A night a deploy ran until midnight and they ate cold noodles out of the same carton in the hallway by the fire door, and he almost asked her not to go home, and he asked if she wanted the rest of the carton instead.

She had. Fourteen days later she took the job in Boston.

I don’t have the right sentence for this, she wrote finally, so I’m just going to say I’m thinking about you and I’m here if you want to talk.

He called forty minutes later.

“I wanted to hear an actual voice today instead of another notification.” He sounded tired in a way she recognized — not sleep-tired, depth-tired. “How are you? It’s been a while.”

“I’m okay. Work’s been strange lately — I’m actually starting a research study thing, neurological stuff, nothing dramatic.” She heard herself say nothing dramatic and marveled at her own capacity for technically true sentences. “How are you holding up? Actually holding up.”

He laughed, once, without much air behind it. “Ask me in a month.”

“Consider it asked. I’m setting a reminder.”

“Do it,” he said. “I might actually need someone to hold me to that.”

They talked for twenty minutes about his father, the shop, the estate still to be sorted. Elena listened the way she used to listen to him explain a routing algorithm at 1 a.m. — completely, without waiting for her turn. She did not bring up the Bridge Program again, and did not correct nothing dramatic. There would be a better week for that conversation.

She believed that when she said it. She was aware, turning off her phone, that believing it and being right about it were not guaranteed to turn out to be the same thing.

The intake appointment she’d kept for the second Wednesday after the funeral turned out to be less dramatic than its extra consent form.

The program occupied a converted floor of a medical building. Its waiting room had the beige calm of every waiting room she’d ever sat in. A coordinator named Priya walked her through the main study’s consent form sentence by sentence.

“Most people skim it,” Priya said.

“I write documentation for a living. Skimming feels like malpractice.”

Near the bottom of the intake folder, in the same unremarkable font: Appendix C materials available upon request; no obligation to review at this time.

A second sheet, clipped behind the first, was a fertility-preservation referral. Freezing eggs, scheduled at the hospital next door, listed with the bloodwork and the BCI screening as if it were the same kind of item. Elena almost deleted the booking. Deleting it felt like a decision she hadn’t earned. She kept the slot.

Elena signed where she was supposed to sign. She did not ask for Appendix C. Not yet, she told herself, which she knew was a different thing from never, and which she also knew was what a person said on the way to never.

Her left hand, closing around her phone that night, took a half-second longer than it used to. She noticed. She always noticed now.
