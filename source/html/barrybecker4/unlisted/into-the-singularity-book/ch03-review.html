Chapter 3: Slow Stations

The Monday after the funeral, Kessler Machine opened at 6 a.m., same as it had every weekday for thirty-one years. Denise was there at 5:50, because the coffee didn’t make itself and somebody had to disarm the alarm panel. It still ran on his ex-wife’s birthday — nobody had ever bothered to change it.

She had the shop to herself for the first ten minutes most days, and she liked it that way. The smell of it before the machines woke up — cutting oil and cold metal and the particular mustiness of a building that had been doing the same job since before she was born.

She walked her station the way she always did, hands first: calipers where she’d left them, the boring bar she’d been fighting with all week, the job traveler clipped to the fixture. She did not think about the number.

She had been not thinking about the number for six days, and it had gotten easier, like a toothache right up until you bite down wrong.

A hundred and twenty thousand dollars, in something she didn’t fully understand, sitting in an app her nephew had walked her through installing. Tied to a string of words on an index card she’d hidden in a coffee can in her closet.

She hadn’t told her sister. She hadn’t told Ray. A shop this size ran on gossip the way an engine ran on oil. She had exactly no interest in being the woman everyone was suddenly being extra nice to.

In recognition of eleven years at the shop and one favor she is under no obligation to remember. She remembered the favor well enough. She just didn’t see any reason the whole county needed to.

Ray called the floor meeting at 6:45, early enough that half the crew was still holding their thermoses.

Denise counted five people including herself. Tyler, who’d been there eight months. Purnell, sixty-one, who ran the old Bridgeport like it owed him money. And Marisol, who did deburring and inspection at the end of the line.

“I’m not going to make this a whole thing,” Ray said. “The trust’s accountant wants to see the shop running lean before the valuation review next year. So we’re piloting some automated inspection at the deburr station. Vision system, checks parts against spec faster than a person can with calipers, flags anything out of tolerance.”

He paused. “It’s not touching the mills. It’s not touching the lathes. It’s one station, and it’s the slowest one we’ve got. The bottleneck on every job we’ve run behind schedule since March.”

“So Marisol,” Purnell said, not unkindly.

“Marisol’s not going anywhere,” Ray said, too fast. “There’s plenty else on the floor that needs hands. This is about the one station.”

Marisol didn’t say anything. She had a face built for not saying anything.

“When’s it going in,” Denise asked.

“Vendor’s coming Thursday to install. Runs alongside Marisol for a couple weeks while it learns the parts, then we see.”

Denise lingered after the others scattered. Ray had the look he’d been wearing since the reading. The attorney’s word, option, was supposed to sound like a door opening. Instead it seemed to weigh on him like five more years added to a sentence.

“You doing okay with all this?” she asked.

“Which part.”

“Any of it.”

“I’ve run this floor eleven years and never once had to explain to anybody why a machine could do their job faster than they could.” He shut his notebook. “I’ll let you know when I’ve figured out how I feel about it.”

Denise watched, without exactly meaning to, the vendor’s crew set the camera rig up Thursday morning. A boxy enclosure on an arm, lights inside that came on in a cold white flicker, a laptop running something that produced a satisfied chime every time a part passed.

Marisol kept working through it, handing parts under the camera the way she’d always handed them to her own eye. The chime went off ninety-some times that morning without missing once. By lunch Marisol had stopped watching the screen.

“It’s not faster than me,” Marisol said at lunch, unwrapping a sandwich. “It’s just — awake all the time. I have to go to the bathroom eventually.”

Nobody had a good response to that.

Denise thought about Warren’s laptop, the one nobody could get into. There was probably something almost funny about the fact that the man who’d spent fourteen years quietly betting the financial system was heading somewhere strange had also willed the exact machine that would start doing Marisol’s job into a fund that would pay to install it.

She didn’t say that out loud.

She’d started the bitcoin thing three years back, twenty dollars here, forty there, whenever the check cleared and the number in her regular account looked like it could stand to lose a little without her feeling it by Friday.

Warren had never sat her down and explained it. But she’d watched him for years — the way he’d stand at the back office door some evenings with a look on his face like a man doing long division in his head. Whatever he was carrying around, she’d figured, it wasn’t nothing.

He’d told her once — four, five years ago, while she was closing up and he was locking the office — hold onto something you don’t need yet.

She hadn’t asked what for. It hadn’t occurred to her until this week, sitting with an index card in a coffee can, that he might have meant it more literally than that.

She pulled out her phone on her break, thumbed into the app, and moved thirty dollars over, same as she had most weeks for three years.

Not the hundred and twenty thousand — that sat where it sat, untouched. Just the thirty dollars, the ordinary kind, the kind that came out of her own check. Habit. Maybe a little spite aimed at the vision system, the trust’s accountant, a world that kept saying not to worry about the one station — Marisol’s — that would obviously become more than one station.

And underneath both, something closer to faith. That a dead man who’d never once been wrong about anything that actually mattered to her had probably not started being wrong about this.

She put the phone away and went back to her mill.

By the end of the shift the vision system had passed four hundred and eleven parts and flagged three, all three correctly, and Marisol had gone home early with a headache she didn’t attribute to anything.

Denise drove home in the truck that had outlived one engine already.

Her sister Angie called around eight, mostly to talk about a landlord who wouldn’t fix a water heater and an orthodontist bill that kept finding new ways to get bigger. Denise let her talk and didn’t say a word about the coffee can. There wasn’t a version of that conversation that ended with Angie not needing something from it by Wednesday.

After she hung up she got the can down anyway, and looked at the index card without taking it out.

A hundred and twenty thousand dollars could fix the roof and the truck and probably Angie’s water heater, and still leave more sitting there than Denise had ever had in one place. Holding the can, she understood that it could also just as easily do nothing at all — sit there being a number, the way Warren’s own numbers had sat being a number for fourteen years.

She thought about the phrase again, hold onto something you don’t need yet, and thought that maybe the hard part was believing, on every ordinary day nothing happened, that not yet and not ever weren’t quietly turning into the same sentence.

She put the can back and went to bed. She figured the truck had a few years left in it yet. And so, probably, did everything else she wasn’t ready to think about tonight.

Nobody at Kessler Machine was worried. That was, everyone would agree later, looking back, more or less the whole problem with the week it started.
