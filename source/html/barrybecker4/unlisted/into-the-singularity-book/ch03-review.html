Chapter 3: Slow Stations

The Monday after the will was read, Kessler Machine opened at 6 a.m., same as it had every weekday for thirty-one years. Janelle was there at 5:50, because the coffee didn’t make itself and somebody had to disarm the alarm panel. It still ran on Warren’s ex-wife’s birthday — nobody had ever bothered to change it.

She had the shop to herself for the first ten minutes most days, and she liked it that way. The smell of it before the machines woke up — cutting oil and cold metal and the particular mustiness of a building that had been doing the same job since before she was born.

She walked her station the way she always did, putting a hand on each thing in turn: calipers where she’d left them, the boring bar she’d been fighting with all week, the job traveler — the work sheet that rode with each part — clipped to the fixture. She did not think about the number.

She had been not thinking about the number for six days, and it had gotten easier, like a toothache right up until you bite down wrong.

A hundred and twenty thousand dollars, in something she didn’t fully understand — bitcoin, same as Warren’s — sitting in an app her nephew had walked her through installing. Tied to a string of words on an index card she’d hidden in a coffee can in her closet.

She hadn’t told her sister. She hadn’t told Ray. A shop this size ran on gossip the way an engine ran on oil. She had exactly no interest in being the woman everyone was suddenly being extra nice to.

In recognition of eleven years at the shop and one favor she is under no obligation to remember. She knew which favor Warren meant. She had never thought it mattered this much, and she saw no reason the whole county needed to know about it.

Ray called the floor meeting at 6:45, early enough that people were still holding their thermoses.

Janelle counted four people including herself. Tyler, who’d been there eight months. Purnell, sixty-one, who ran the old Bridgeport — a manual mill you still steered by hand — like it owed him money. And Marisol, who did the finishing work at the end of the line, smoothing rough edges and checking parts against the drawing.

“I’m not going to make this a whole thing,” Ray said. “The trust’s accountant wants to see the shop running lean before they price it next year. So we’re piloting some automated inspection at Marisol’s station. A camera system that checks parts against the drawing faster than a person can with measuring tools, and flags anything the wrong size.”

He paused. “It’s not touching the mills. It’s not touching the lathes. It’s one station, and it’s the slowest one we’ve got. The bottleneck on every job we’ve run behind schedule since last March.”

“So Marisol,” Purnell said, not unkindly.

“Marisol’s not going anywhere,” Ray said, too fast. “There’s plenty else on the floor that needs hands. This is about the one station.”

Marisol didn’t say anything. She had a face built for not saying anything.

“When’s it going in?” Janelle asked.

“Vendor’s coming Thursday to install. Runs alongside Marisol for a couple weeks while it learns the parts, then we see.”

Janelle lingered after the others scattered. Ray had looked wrung out since the reading. The word going around the floor was option, and everybody had decided it was a gift. What Ray had actually been handed was five years of payments on a building he’d have to keep full, at a price set low enough that turning it down would look like cowardice.

“You doing okay with all this?” she asked.

“Which part?”

“Any of it.”

“I’ve run this floor eleven years and never once had to explain to anybody why a machine could do their job faster than they could.” He shut his notebook. “I’ll let you know when I’ve figured out how I feel about it.”

Janelle watched the vendor’s crew set up the camera rig Thursday morning. It was a box on an arm, with lights inside that came on in a cold white flicker and software that produced a satisfied chime every time a part passed.

Marisol kept working through it, handing parts under the camera the way she’d always handed them to her own eye. The chime went off ninety-some times that morning without missing once. By lunch Marisol had stopped watching the screen.

“It’s not faster than me,” Marisol said at lunch, unwrapping a sandwich. “It’s just — awake all the time. I have to go to the bathroom eventually.”

Nobody had a good response to that.

Janelle thought about Warren’s laptop, the one nobody could get into. Fourteen years he’d spent quietly betting that the money everybody used was going to break. The will that revealed that bet had also put his shop into a trust. The trust had an accountant, and the accountant wanted Marisol’s station cheaper by spring. One way of looking at it, Warren had bought the camera himself.

She didn’t say that out loud.

She’d started buying bitcoin three years earlier, twenty dollars here, forty there, whenever her paycheck cleared and her bank account could spare a little without leaving her short by Friday.

Warren had never sat her down and explained it. But she’d watched him for years. He’d stand in the back office doorway some evenings with the face of a man doing long division in his head. She’d figured he knew something about money he wasn’t going to explain to anybody, and that she’d rather be a little bit in than all the way out.

He’d told her once — four, five years ago, while she was closing up and he was locking the office — hold onto something you don’t need yet.

She hadn’t asked what for. It hadn’t occurred to her until this week, sitting with an index card in a coffee can, that he might have meant it more literally than that.

She pulled out her phone on her break, thumbed into the app, and moved thirty dollars over, same as she had most weeks for three years.

Not the hundred and twenty thousand — that sat where it sat, untouched. Just thirty dollars from her own paycheck. Habit. Maybe a little spite aimed at the vision system, the trust’s accountant, and a world that kept telling her not to worry because it was only one station. Every job on that floor was one station.

And underneath both, something closer to faith. That a dead man who’d never once been wrong about anything that actually mattered to her had probably not started being wrong about this.

She put the phone away and went back to her lathe.

By the end of the shift the vision system had passed four hundred and eleven parts and flagged three, all three correctly, and Marisol had gone home early with a headache she didn’t attribute to anything.

Janelle drove home in the truck that had outlived one engine already.

Her sister Angie called around eight, mostly to talk about a landlord who wouldn’t fix a water heater and an orthodontist bill that kept finding new ways to get bigger. Janelle let her talk and didn’t say a word about the coffee can. There wasn’t a version of that conversation that ended with Angie not needing something from it by Wednesday.

After she hung up she got the can down anyway, and looked at the index card without taking it out.

A hundred and twenty thousand dollars could fix the roof and the truck and probably Angie’s water heater, and still leave more than Janelle had ever had in one place. Or it could sit untouched for fourteen years, the way Warren’s had, until one afternoon in a law office above a dentist when a woman read aloud what it had become.

Hold onto something you don’t need yet. The holding wasn’t the hard part. The hard part was believing, on the ordinary days when nothing happened, that the day she’d need it was ever going to come at all.

She put the can back and went to bed. She figured the truck had a few good years left in it. She figured she did too.

Nobody at Kessler Machine was worried. Later, everyone would agree that was the problem: the week the cameras started doing people’s jobs, nobody thought they had anything to worry about.
