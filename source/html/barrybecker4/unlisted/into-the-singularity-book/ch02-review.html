Chapter 2: The Reading

The funeral itself Daniel would remember afterward mostly in pieces — not the whole of it, just the moments where something snagged.

The organ, playing something nobody had picked because his father hadn’t left instructions and nobody had wanted to be the one who did. Ray Delgado in a suit that didn’t fit him, sitting in the third row because he’d insisted the front two belonged to family. Mara’s hand finding his during the part of the service where the minister, who had clearly never met Warren, described him as “a man who loved his work” in a tone that made it sound like a diagnosis.

And his mother, Joan, three pews back, in a gray coat Daniel didn’t recognize. She’d driven up from Cincinnati that morning and would drive back that night. She’d said, gently, that she didn’t think she belonged at the house afterward, and nobody had argued with her about it.

She’d hugged him at the door of the church, quick and real, and said “I’m sorry, baby” in the voice she’d used for skinned knees thirty years ago. For one brief second he’d been eight again, so he let her hold on a second longer than either of them probably meant to.

Then she’d let go and asked after his job, the way people ask when they assume the answer is still fine. He’d said it’s fine, Mom, don’t worry about it, and she had, visibly, worried about it anyway. The same way she’d worried about things that were none of her business anymore for eleven years and hadn’t managed to stop.

She and his father hadn’t spoken in that church. They hadn’t needed to. Whatever they’d had left to say to each other had already gotten said, years ago, in rooms he hadn’t been in. What remained was two people standing on opposite sides of a box neither of them had asked to be standing near, being adult about it. He was getting fairly well acquainted with that kind of quiet this month.

The reading was the following Tuesday, in a law office on the second floor of a building on Main Street that also housed a dentist and, per the directory in the lobby, an outfit called Millbrook Tax & Notary that Daniel suspected was one guy.

Warren’s attorney was a woman named Carol Weiss, somewhere in her sixties, with a cardigan and a manner that suggested she had done this exact thing more times than she’d have liked. Sat across a table from grieving adult children and told them what their parent had actually thought of them, in the only legally binding format the man had ever bothered to use.

She had known Warren, she said, since 1998, when he’d come in to have a supply contract reviewed and stayed forty minutes past the appointment asking her, apropos of nothing, whether a living trust could hold something that wasn’t, strictly speaking, a physical asset. “I didn’t know what he meant,” she said. “For about a decade I didn’t know what he meant.”

Denise was already in the waiting room when Daniel and Mara arrived — Denise Marsh, who’d worked the lathe at Kessler Machine since before Daniel moved away, sitting very straight in a chair that didn’t look built for straightness. She wore a coat Daniel recognized from the funeral, one of maybe two he’d ever seen her in, both times at things involving his father.

She stood when they came in, said she was sorry for their loss in the flat, over-used cadence of someone who has said it to too many people this week to make it sound new. Then she didn’t sit back down until Mara did, like she wasn’t sure whose room this was.

“I didn’t invite her,” Mara murmured to Daniel, low, while Carol got settled behind the desk. It wasn’t unkind. It was just Mara, running an audit.

“I didn’t either.”

“So who did?”

Carol answered that one without being asked. She opened a folder and said, in the same unhurried voice she’d used for everything else that morning, that Denise Marsh was named in the instrument and had been sent notice by the office, same as they had. She’d now begin, if everyone was ready, because there was a decent amount to get through and she had a two o’clock.

Most of it was ordinary — boilerplate, the kind Daniel associated with terms of service he’d clicked through a thousand times without reading. The house, in a trust, to be sold, proceeds split between Daniel and Mara.

Kessler Machine itself — the building and the equipment and the accounts — was held in the same trust. Ray Delgado had an option to buy in over five years at a price set low enough that Carol read it twice to make sure they’d heard the number correctly the first time.

Until then, the shop would keep running exactly as it had. “Your father’s language here, and I’m quoting him directly, is that a business is a living thing and you don’t turn one off because the man who fed it died on a Tuesday.”

Daniel felt something move in his chest at a Tuesday — his father hadn’t meant it as an echo, couldn’t have, but there it was anyway.

Then Carol turned a page and said, “The rest of it is in Schedule C,” and the register of the room changed the way it does right before a deploy that everyone on the call already privately suspects is going to break something.

“Your father held a quantity of bitcoin,” she said, “accumulated over approximately fourteen years, which he documented — meticulously, I want to say, because it made my job considerably easier than it usually is with this kind of asset — in a private ledger cross-referenced against a series of storage devices and paper backups.”

She continued. “Some of which are in this office and some of which, per his instructions, are not, and are described in a letter I’ll read you in a moment.”

She looked up. “As of the appraisal date, six days after his death, the total was independently valued at just under eleven million dollars.”

She paused. “I want to be honest with you both that this number is already wrong, in one direction or the other, and will keep being wrong every day from here forward. There isn’t a version of this conversation where I can tell you which direction it’ll be wrong in next.”

Daniel heard himself say “okay” in a voice that sounded like it belonged to someone watching this happen to a coworker.

He did the math without meaning to — not the big number, which refused to resolve into anything he could hold, just sat there being eleven million dollars, meaning nothing the way the incident doc’s root-cause field had ten days ago — but the small math, the legible math. Eight weeks of severance. A lease he’d signed in the spring. A health plan he’d need to replace.

All of that had been the entire scope of his worry for a week and a half. All of it had just stopped mattering, in a room that smelled like toner and the dentist’s waiting room through the wall.

He didn’t feel relieved. He felt unmoored. The week-and-a-half of worry had been holding something up, and now it was gone and he couldn’t tell what.

Mara hadn’t said anything. She had gone very still — the stillness that always came right before she moved fast — and when Daniel glanced at her she was already somewhere else, running numbers behind her eyes that had nothing to do with grief.

“Split evenly between the two of you,” Carol continued, “per his direction, along with a letter he asked me to read into the record rather than simply hand over — his words, not mine: ‘so nobody can pretend later they didn’t hear it.’”

She unfolded a single page, and read it in her own voice, not an impression of his, which Daniel was grateful for.

To Daniel and Mara, in equal shares: everything in Schedule C is yours, accumulated a little at a time starting in 2013, and I’m not going to explain why I did it that way except to say that neither of you ever once asked me what the envelopes added up to, and I noticed that, and I appreciated it more than I probably ever said. Use it or don’t. It was never a test. The passwords are with the rest of it, wherever “the rest of it” turns out to be, which I expect will take you longer to find than you’d like. That’s on purpose too.

Daniel thought, with a jolt that arrived a full ten seconds late, of every Christmas envelope, the padded ones especially. He’d always assumed the weight was just a folded card stock or two.

He understood now, sitting in a dead man’s attorney’s office, what some of those envelopes must have held — a piece of a private key printed on a metal card, or a small hardware wallet wrapped in enough bubble mailer to survive a mail carrier’s indifference. He had signed for none of it, understood none of it, and simply held it, because he’d been told to.

“There’s a laptop,” Carol said, “in a locked drawer in the back office at the house, that isn’t part of this inventory because I don’t have a password for it and neither, as far as I know, does anyone else currently living. Your father’s letter says you’ll figure it out. I’d start there when you’re ready, not before.”

Then she turned to the last page, and said, “Denise, this part concerns you,” and the room’s attention swung toward her. Daniel watched it land on her face as something closer to dread than curiosity.

“To Denise Marsh,” Carol read, “in recognition of eleven years at the shop and one favor she is under no obligation to remember, the sum described in Schedule D.” She named a figure, small against the number that had just reorganized Daniel’s entire understanding of his own life, but not small in any other sense — enough to matter to a person who’d spent a decade buying bitcoin ten and twenty dollars at a time out of a machinist’s paycheck.

“To be transferred outright, no trust, no conditions, because,” and here Carol’s voice did something careful, reading a sentence she’d clearly read to herself first and decided to deliver exactly as written, “‘she never once asked me for anything, which is more than I can say for people who had a legal right to.’”

Nobody looked at Mara when that sentence came out, which was its own kind of loud.

Denise didn’t say anything for a long moment. When she did, it was to Carol, not to either of them: “What favor.”

“He didn’t say. I asked him the same thing, years ago, when he first added the clause, and he told me it wasn’t mine to tell.” Carol closed the folder. “I’m sorry. I know that’s not an answer.”

“No,” Denise said. “It’s the answer he’d give.” She said it like she’d known him well enough to recognize a door being deliberately left shut.

That told Daniel more clearly than anything else in the last twenty minutes that there was a whole relationship here he’d never once thought to wonder about. His father and a woman who’d run a lathe on the same floor Daniel had grown up walking through, close enough that Warren had rewritten his will around a debt he refused to name.

Outside, on the sidewalk, in the flat gray light Ohio kept producing this month, Mara had her phone out before the office door had fully shut behind them.

“I need to call Devon,” she said — a business-school friend who ran a small crypto shop she’d mentioned once or twice. “There’s a window right now, structurally, that closes if I wait even a week. I am not going to sit on half of eleven million dollars because it feels rude to think about money the day we heard the word ‘bitcoin’ out loud at our father’s lawyer’s office.”

She caught herself, softened. “You okay?”

“I don’t know yet,” Daniel said. Same answer he’d given her on the phone ten days ago. It was still the only true one.

Denise came out a minute behind them, coat buttoned, and stopped like she was deciding whether the conversation she was about to have was worth having. “I didn’t ask for that,” she said, to both of them and neither. “I want that on the record too, since we’re apparently doing everything on the record today.”

“We know,” Daniel said, and meant it, though he understood as he said it that we know wasn’t the same thing as we’re fine with it, and that Denise, watching his face, understood that too.

“He was good to me,” she said, carefully, like she was choosing which true thing to hand over and which to keep. “I don’t know what you want me to do with that.”

“Nothing,” Mara said, not unkindly, already half-turned back toward her phone. “You don’t owe us an explanation for what our own father did.”

It was, Daniel thought, the most generous thing he’d heard his sister say all week. It still sounded like a door closing. Denise seemed to hear it the same way — she nodded once, said she had to get back, the shop didn’t run itself, whatever the will said about who technically owned it now — and walked off toward a truck that looked, up close, like it had outlived at least one previous engine.

Daniel stood on the sidewalk a while after she’d gone, doing the math again — the real math this time, the kind with no clean denominator.

A father he’d seen fourteen months before he died. A mother who’d driven two hours each way to stand near a man she hadn’t spoken to in eleven years. A woman on his father’s shop floor who apparently knew something about Warren that neither of his own children did. And a number that wasn’t in any bank — a number in a ledger somewhere — that had just changed what was possible for him without changing anything about what he actually knew.

Somewhere behind a locked drawer in his father’s house was a laptop that might explain some of it. He wasn’t ready for that yet. He got back in the rental car instead, and sat for a minute before starting it. His father had managed, in death, to say more to him in one read-aloud letter than he had in the last decade of phone calls. He should have found a way to fix that while there’d still been time. He hadn’t. Same theory as always: there would obviously be more of it later.
