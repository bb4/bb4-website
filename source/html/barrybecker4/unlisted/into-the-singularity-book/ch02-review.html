Chapter 2: The Reading

Daniel would remember the funeral in pieces. The organ, playing something nobody had picked. Ray Delgado in a suit that didn’t fit him, sitting in the third row because he’d insisted the front two belonged to family. Mara’s hand finding his during the part where the minister, who had clearly never met Warren, described him as “a man who loved his work” in a tone that made it sound like a diagnosis.

And his mother, Joan, three pews back. She’d driven up from Cincinnati that morning and would drive back that night. She’d said she didn’t think she belonged at the house afterward, and nobody had argued.

She hugged him at the church door, quick and real, and said “I’m sorry, baby” in the voice she’d used for skinned knees thirty years ago. For a second he was eight again.

Then she’d asked after his job, the way people ask when they assume the answer is still fine. He’d said it’s fine, Mom, and she had, visibly, worried about it anyway.

The reading was the following Tuesday, in a law office on the second floor of a building that also housed a dentist.

Warren’s attorney was a woman named Carol Weiss, somewhere in her sixties, who had clearly done this exact thing more times than she’d have liked.

She had known Warren since 1998, she said. He’d come in to have a supply contract reviewed and stayed forty minutes asking her whether a living trust could hold something that wasn’t, strictly speaking, a physical asset. “I didn’t know what he meant,” she said. “For about a decade I didn’t know what he meant.”

Denise was already in the waiting room when Daniel and Mara arrived — Denise Marsh, who’d worked the lathe at Kessler Machine since before Daniel moved away.

“I didn’t invite her,” Mara murmured to Daniel. “So who did?”

Carol answered that one without being asked. Denise Marsh was named in the instrument and had been sent notice by the office. She’d now begin, if everyone was ready.

Most of it was ordinary — the house, in a trust, to be sold, proceeds split. Kessler Machine itself was held in the same trust. Ray Delgado had an option to buy in over five years at a price set low enough that Carol read it twice.

Until then, the shop would keep running exactly as it had. “Your father’s language here is that a business is a living thing and you don’t turn one off because the man who fed it died on a Tuesday.”

Daniel felt something move in his chest at a Tuesday.

Then Carol turned a page and said, “The rest of it is in Schedule C,” and the atmostphere of the room changed.

“Your father held a quantity of bitcoin,” she said, “accumulated over approximately fourteen years, which he documented — meticulously — in a private ledger cross-referenced against a series of storage devices and paper backups.”

Some of which were in this office and some of which were not, and were described in a letter.

“As of the appraisal date, six days after his death, the total was independently valued at just under eleven million dollars.”

She paused. “I want to be honest that this number is already wrong, in one direction or the other, and will keep being wrong every day from here forward.”

Daniel heard himself say “okay” in a voice that belonged to someone watching this happen to a coworker.

He did the math without meaning to — not the big number, which refused to resolve, just the small math. Eight weeks of severance. A lease he’d signed in the spring. A health plan he’d need to replace.

All of that had been the entire scope of his worry for a week and a half. All of it had just stopped mattering.

Mara hadn’t said anything. She had gone very still — the stillness that always came right before she moved fast.

“Split evenly between the two of you,” Carol continued, “along with a letter he asked me to read into the record.”

She unfolded a single page.

To Daniel and Mara: everything in Schedule C is yours, accumulated a little at a time starting in 2013. Neither of you ever once asked me what the envelopes added up to, and I appreciated that more than I probably ever said. Use it or don’t. It was never a test. The passwords are with the rest of it, wherever “the rest of it” turns out to be, which I expect will take you longer to find than you’d like. That’s on purpose too.

Daniel thought, with a jolt, of every Christmas envelope, the padded ones especially. He’d always assumed the weight was just a folded card stock.

He understood now what some of those envelopes must have held — a piece of a private key printed on a metal card, or a small hardware wallet wrapped in bubble mailer. He had signed for none of it, understood none of it, and simply held it, because he’d been told to.

“There’s a laptop,” Carol said, “in a locked drawer in the back office at the house. Your father’s letter says you’ll figure it out.”

Then she turned to the last page. “Denise, this part concerns you.”

“To Denise Marsh, in recognition of eleven years at the shop and one favor she is under no obligation to remember, the sum described in Schedule D.” A figure small against Daniel’s number, but not small in any other sense — enough to matter to a person who’d spent a decade buying bitcoin ten and twenty dollars at a time.

“To be transferred outright, no conditions, because ‘she never once asked me for anything, which is more than I can say for people who had a legal right to.’”

Nobody looked at Mara when that sentence came out.

Denise didn’t say anything for a long moment. “What favor.”

“He didn’t say. I asked him years ago, and he told me it wasn’t mine to tell.”

“No,” Denise said. “It’s the answer he’d give.” She said it like she’d known him well enough to recognize a door being deliberately left shut.

Outside, Mara had her phone out before the office door had shut.

“I need to call Devon. There’s a window right now, structurally, that closes if I wait even a week.”

She caught herself, softened. “You okay?”

“I don’t know yet,” Daniel said.

Denise came out a minute behind them. “I didn’t ask for that,” she said. “I want that on the record too.”

“We know,” Daniel said.

“He was good to me,” she said carefully. “I don’t know what you want me to do with that.”

“Nothing,” Mara said. “You don’t owe us an explanation for what our own father did.”

It was the most generous thing he’d heard his sister say all week. It still sounded like a door closing.

Daniel stood on the sidewalk after she’d gone, doing the real math. A father he’d seen fourteen months before he died. A woman on his father’s shop floor who apparently knew something about Warren that neither of his own children did. And a number that wasn’t in any bank — a number in a ledger somewhere — that had just changed what was possible for him without changing anything about what he actually knew.

Somewhere behind a locked drawer was a laptop that might explain some of it. He wasn’t ready for that yet. He got back in the rental car and sat for a minute. His father had managed, in death, to say more to him in one read-aloud letter than he had in the last decade of phone calls.
