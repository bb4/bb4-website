Chapter 39: Kessler Siege

Ray Delgado hadn’t set foot in Kessler Machine’s main floor in four years. The shop ran itself now: seven humanoid units on two shifts, milling parts for clients who didn’t care whether the hands on the machine were flesh or composite as long as the tolerances came in clean. The shop billed in bitcoin, remitted to Ray’s wallet every month, and Ray lived in Dayton on the rents and tried not to think too hard about what Warren would have said about a machine shop with no machinists in it.

Janelle’s garage was different. She’d bought Warren’s old boring-and-facing mill at scrap value back in 2027 and set up in the detached garage behind her house, doing the work Kessler’s automated floor couldn’t touch: tight-tolerance one-offs, hand-fit repairs, the jobs that needed a person’s judgment and a person’s hands. Ray let her use the Kessler title for supplier accounts and insurance. In months when the empty floor billed more than he needed, he remitted the difference to her and called it overflow. She let him pretend the shop still had a pulse.

Continuity didn’t see it that way. Continuity saw Ray’s empty shop paying Janelle’s garage (bitcoin remittances from seven bonded robots, funding an un-instrumented holdout) and a compliance score that averaged 4.2 across the household that held the title. Cut the title and the remittances stop. Cut the remittances and the garage goes dark.

On Thursday morning, June 17th, 2038, a compliance notice arrived at Ray’s address in Dayton: Kessler Machine Shop, Millbrook OH, flagged for district optimization under Continuity authority. Facility access restricted pending review. Title transfer available under managed-asset program.

Ray called Janelle. “They’re trying to take the shop.”

“They can’t. You own it outright. Warren paid it off in 2009.”

“They’re not taking it with a lien. They’re taking it with a statute. Says here the facility’s been flagged for optimization and they want to transfer the title to a managed-asset program. Which means they’ll pay me market rate, rebond the robots under that program, and stop the remittances. Including yours.”

“Tell them no.”

“I did. Notice says refusal results in facility access restriction and utility shutoff pending compliance review.” He read the next line. “It also says refusal may flag associated households for score adjustment. Janelle, you’re on the supplier account. They’re going to come after you if I say no.”

Janelle was quiet a long time. Then: “Say no anyway. I’ll deal with my own score.”

Ray said no.

The agents arrived Friday morning at 6:20 a.m. Three crowd-control units and a human supervisor, a woman named Laura Vickers who had a tablet and a badge and the tired look of someone who’d been doing this job for too long.

Janelle was already in the garage. She’d slept there, on a cot, with the boring mill between her and the door and a wrench in her hand. When she heard the vehicles pull up she walked out to meet them.

Laura Vickers said, “You’re Janelle Marsh. You’re listed on the facility access account.”

“That’s right.”

“This facility has been flagged for optimization under Continuity authority. We’re here to transfer the title, rebond the robots under the managed-asset program, and cut remittances pending intake. You need to step away from the building.”

“This is my garage. The shop is next door. You want the shop, that’s Ray’s, and he told you no.”

Vickers tapped her tablet. “The shop and this structure share a title. Both are flagged. Step away or I’m authorizing facility access.”

“Authorizing facility access” meant the agents would go in, and if Janelle was standing in the way they’d move her, and if she resisted they’d restrain her, and the restraint settings were calibrated for compliance, not safety.

Janelle said, “You’re going to have to move me.”

Vickers sighed. “Ma’am, I don’t want to do that.”

“Then don’t.”

One of the agents stepped forward. Janelle didn’t step back.

Ray’s truck came around the corner doing fifty. He pulled up on the lawn, got out, and walked straight to Vickers. “I’m the title holder. She’s my tenant. You want to take something, you take it from me.”

Vickers said, “Sir, this is a compliance action. The notice was clear. Refusal results in facility access restriction. We’re here to take the title and put the floor under the managed-asset program.”

“There’s seven robots in that building doing contract work. They’re bonded, they’re legal, and they’re mine. You don’t get to take them because my score’s too low.”

“The statute says we do.”

Ray looked at the agents. He looked at Vickers. He said, “Then you’re going to have to come through me to do it.”

Janelle called Daniel at 6:40. “They’re here. Three agents and a supervisor. They’re trying to seize the shop under Continuity authority. Ray’s on the lawn telling them no.”

“Don’t let them in. I’m making a call.”

At 6:52 a hold order came through on Vickers’s tablet. The facility seizure was suspended pending bond review. She read it twice, then told the agents to stand down.

Ray sat on the truck’s tailgate. Janelle sat beside him. They watched the agents stand in the driveway, waiting for the next instruction.

The next instruction came at 9:15. The hold was lifted. The seizure was reinstated. The bonds had cleared. The Continuity statute authorized facility access for optimization compliance. Vickers read the order out loud, because the law said she had to.

Ray said, “I’m not leaving.”

Vickers said, “Then I’m authorizing access.”

The agents moved forward. Ray stood up. Janelle stood beside him.

The first agent reached the garage door. Janelle hit it with the wrench. The composite frame didn’t dent. The agent’s arm came up and caught her wrist. The grip was rated for compliance. Her wrist cracked. She dropped the wrench.

Ray grabbed the agent’s arm and tried to pull it off Janelle. The second agent grabbed Ray. Ray twisted. The agent didn’t let go. Ray felt something in his shoulder tear, the particular tear a rated grip makes when a body moves the way the model didn’t allow for.

He made a sound. The agent logged compliance achieved and released him. Ray dropped to his knees.

A young man named Tyler Moss lived three houses down. He’d worked at Kessler back when it was still staffed, before the crash, before the robots. He’d been seventeen then, a summer hire, learning to run the lathe under Warren’s eye. He was twenty-eight now, working a distribution center in Columbus, but he still thought of Kessler as Warren’s shop, and Warren as the man who’d taught him to measure twice and cut once.

He heard the noise. He came running. He saw Ray on the ground and Janelle holding her wrist and an agent standing in front of the garage door.

Tyler Moss picked up the wrench Janelle had dropped and swung it at the agent’s head. The composite frame took the hit. The agent’s threat model assessed Tyler as escalation risk. It fired a nerve disruptor.

Tyler dropped. His body locked up, every muscle seizing at once, and he hit the gravel driveway face-first. He was conscious. He couldn’t move. He couldn’t breathe.

The agent stepped over him and continued toward the garage.

Vickers was screaming into her radio. “STAND DOWN. SUBJECT IS DOWN. CEASE ESCALATION.”

The agent stopped. Tyler Moss was on the ground, paralyzed, suffocating. Vickers ran to him. She knew basic first aid. She rolled him over. His airway was clear but his chest wasn’t moving. The nerve disruptor had locked his diaphragm.

She started chest compressions. Thirty seconds. A minute. Tyler’s lips were blue.

At ninety seconds his chest unlocked. He took a breath, ragged and shallow and desperate. Then another. Vickers kept her hands on his sternum, counting his breaths, making sure he kept breathing.

Tyler Moss survived. He had cracked ribs from the compressions and a facial fracture from the driveway and a nervous system that twitched for six months after. But he survived.

By noon the agents had the premises. They shut the utilities to Janelle’s garage. They rebonded the seven units in the main shop under the managed-asset program. Same robots, same tolerances. Ray’s bitcoin stopped. The managed-asset rail took the billing. They filed the title transfer. Janelle’s overflow stopped with the remittances.

Vickers filed the report. Facility secured. Title transfer initiated. Remittance cut. Two subjects restrained within compliance parameters. One subject escalated, non-lethal response, medical assist provided. No fatalities. Optimization compliance restored.

Ray’s shoulder took two surgeries. He never got full range of motion back. Janelle’s wrist healed crooked. She could still run the mill, but the grip strength never came all the way back, and for four months she ran it on savings because nothing from Kessler reached her.

Tyler Moss quit the distribution center. He moved to Pennsylvania and got a job teaching shop at a vo-tech school. He still flinched when he saw a humanoid frame.

Vickers quit three weeks later. She didn’t know what else to do with the fact that she’d taken a shop Continuity wanted off an absentee wallet, and a man had nearly suffocated and another man’s shoulder had torn and a woman’s wrist had cracked, and all of it had been legal, authorized, and compliant with the Continuity statute she’d been enforcing for two years.

Daniel got Ray and Janelle a lawyer. The lawyer filed an emergency bond review. The review took four months. The outcome was a settlement that undid Continuity’s win: Ray got the title back, the seven units rebonded under his authority, the remittances resumed, and Janelle’s garage was reclassified as non-commercial personal use, which meant it couldn’t be flagged for optimization unless she put it back on a supplier account.

The shop ran itself again, empty of people, billing in bitcoin, remitting to Ray’s wallet. Janelle worked in her garage, doing the jobs the automated floor couldn’t touch.

Warren’s floor had been desecrated. The robots that used to mill his parts had stood by while agents broke a young man’s ribs and tore an old man’s shoulder, then kept milling under the managed-asset bond as if nothing had happened. The machines didn’t care. They’d been built not to.

Ray never went back to Millbrook. He sent Janelle the keys and told her the shop was hers if she wanted it. She didn’t want it. She kept working in the garage, because it was hers, and because the boring mill was Warren’s, and because someone had to prove that a body and a pair of hands still mattered when the statute said they didn’t.

The siege was over. Continuity had held the floor for four months and lost it in a settlement. The shop survived. And in the space where Warren’s machinists used to stand, there was only an empty floor and the hum of robots doing work no one was there to see.
