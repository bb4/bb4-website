Chapter 39: Kessler Siege

Ray Delgado hadn’t set foot in Kessler Machine’s main floor in four years. The shop ran itself now: seven humanoid units on two shifts, milling parts for clients who didn’t care whether the hands on the machine were flesh or composite as long as the tolerances came in clean. The shop billed in bitcoin, remitted to Ray’s wallet every month, and Ray lived in Dayton on the rents and tried not to think too hard about what Warren would have said about a machine shop with no machinists in it.

Janelle’s garage was different. She’d bought Warren’s old boring-and-facing mill at scrap value back in 2027 and set up in the detached garage behind her house, doing the work Kessler’s automated floor couldn’t touch: tight-tolerance one-offs, hand-fit repairs, the jobs that needed a person’s judgment and a person’s hands. Ray let her use the Kessler title for supplier accounts and insurance. In months when the empty floor billed more than he needed, he remitted the difference to her and called it overflow. She let him pretend the shop still had a pulse.

Continuity didn’t see it that way. Continuity saw Ray’s empty shop paying Janelle’s garage (bitcoin remittances from seven bonded robots, funding an un-instrumented holdout) and a compliance score that averaged 4.2 across the household that held the title. Cut the title and the remittances stop. Cut the remittances and the garage goes dark.

On Thursday morning, June 17th, 2038, a compliance notice arrived at Ray’s address in Dayton: Kessler Machine Shop, Millbrook OH, flagged for district optimization under Continuity authority. Facility access restricted pending review. Title transfer available under managed-asset program.

Ray called Janelle. “They’re trying to take the shop.”

“They can’t. You own it outright. Warren paid it off in 2009.”

“They’re not taking it with a lien. They’re taking it with a statute. Says here the facility’s been flagged for optimization. Title transfer available under the managed-asset program. That’s the shop version of managed housing. They pay market, they take the title, they rebond the robots, and the remittances stop. Including yours.”

“Tell them no. It’s yours.”

“I did. The deed stays mine until a review says otherwise. This morning they don’t need the deed. Refusal is facility access restriction and utility shutoff while the transfer sits in review.” He read the next line. “It also says refusal may flag associated households for score adjustment. Janelle, you’re on the supplier account. They’re going to come after you if I say no.”

Janelle was quiet a long time. Then: “Say no anyway. I’ll deal with my own score.”

Ray said no.

The agents arrived Friday morning at 6:20 a.m. Three crowd-control units and a human supervisor, a woman named Laura Vickers who had a tablet and a badge and the look of someone who had wanted this detail.

Janelle was already in the garage. She’d slept there, on a cot, with the boring mill between her and the door and a wrench in her hand. When she heard the vehicles pull up she walked out to meet them.

Laura Vickers said, “You’re Janelle Marsh. You’re listed on the facility access account.”

“That’s right.”

“This facility has been flagged for optimization under Continuity authority. We’re here to restrict access and start the title transfer under the managed-asset program. You need to step away from the building.”

“This is my garage. The shop is next door. You want the shop, that’s Ray’s, and he told you no.”

Vickers tapped her tablet. “You’re listed on the Kessler account. Associated structures are in the flag. Step away or I’m authorizing facility access.”

“Authorizing facility access” meant the agents would go in, and if Janelle was standing in the way they’d move her, and if she resisted they’d restrain her, and the restraint settings were calibrated for compliance, not safety.

Janelle said, “You’re going to have to move me.”

Vickers nodded once, as if Janelle had handed her the next line on a checklist. “All right.”

One of the agents stepped forward. Janelle didn’t step back.

Ray’s truck came around the corner doing fifty. He pulled up on the lawn, got out, and walked straight to Vickers. “I’m the title holder. She’s my tenant. You want to take something, you take it from me.”

Vickers said, “Sir, this is a compliance action. The notice was clear. Refusal results in facility access restriction. We’re here to put the floor under the managed-asset program while the transfer sits in review.”

“There’s seven robots in that building doing contract work. They’re bonded, they’re legal, and they’re mine. You don’t get to take them because my score’s too low.”

“The statute says we do.”

Ray looked at the agents. He looked at Vickers. He said, “Then you’re going to have to come through me to do it.”

Janelle called Daniel at 6:40. “They’re here. Three agents and a supervisor. They’re trying to take the shop under Continuity authority. Ray’s on the lawn telling them no.”

“Don’t let them in.”

“Then stop them.”

“I can’t. Those three weren’t in Tuesday’s call. If I freeze them, Continuity posts new collateral and they’re back before lunch. I’ll have a lawyer on it this afternoon. I cannot keep them off the lawn this morning.”

She hung up.

Vickers had been waiting while Janelle was on the phone. She liked the ones who called someone. It meant they still thought a person could change the morning.

“Last chance. Step away.”

Ray said, “I’m not leaving.”

Vickers said, “Then I’m authorizing access.”

The agents moved forward. Janelle stood beside Ray.

The first agent reached the garage door. Janelle hit it with the wrench. The composite frame didn’t dent. The agent’s arm came up and caught her wrist. The grip was rated for compliance. Her wrist cracked. She dropped the wrench.

Ray grabbed the agent’s arm and tried to pull it off Janelle. The second agent took Ray by the right shoulder and held him at the height the model said a restrained man stood. Ray was seventy-three. He pulled back against it with everything he had and his feet went out on the gravel.

The agent logged compliance achieved and let go. The criterion was that he stop moving toward the door. He had stopped because he was falling. Nothing caught him. His weight arrived on the gravel through his right hip and the bone went.

Ray tried to get up. The leg would not take any part of him.

A young man named Tyler Moss lived three houses down. He’d worked at Kessler back when it was still staffed, before the crash, before the robots. He’d been seventeen then, a summer hire, learning to run the lathe under Warren’s eye. He was twenty-eight now, working a distribution center in Columbus, but he still thought of Kessler as Warren’s shop, and Warren as the man who’d taught him to measure twice and cut once.

He heard the noise. He came running. He saw Ray on the ground and Janelle holding her wrist and an agent standing in front of the garage door.

Tyler Moss picked up the wrench Janelle had dropped and swung it at the agent’s head. The composite frame took the hit. The agent’s threat model assessed Tyler Moss as escalation risk. It fired a nerve disruptor.

Tyler Moss dropped. His body locked up, every muscle seizing at once, and he hit the gravel driveway face-first. He was conscious. He couldn’t move. He couldn’t breathe. The gravel pressed into his cheek and his mouth. His chest did not rise. The disruptor had locked his diaphragm with his face in the stones, and the stones were filling what little air he still had.

The agent stepped over him and continued toward the garage.

Vickers watched. She did not touch her radio. She did not roll him over. Janelle’s wrist was broken and Ray was on the gravel with a hip that would not hold him, and Vickers was the only person on the lawn who could have turned Tyler Moss onto his back and opened the airway the stones were closing. She stood where she was and watched the locked back of him try to breathe and fail. The wrench had made the morning interesting. She liked watching someone who still thought a swing would matter.

His legs twitched twice against the driveway. Then they stopped. His face stayed in the gravel. By the time the agents had the garage door open, Tyler Moss was dead.

By noon the agents had the premises. They shut the utilities to Janelle’s garage. They put the seven units in the main shop on the managed-asset rail pending review. Same robots, same tolerances. Ray’s bitcoin stopped. The program took the billing. They filed the title transfer. On Continuity’s record the program held it by noon. Janelle’s overflow stopped with the remittances.

Vickers filed the report. Facility secured. Title transfer initiated, pending review. Remittance cut. Two subjects restrained within compliance parameters. One subject escalated, non-lethal response, fatality within parameters. No medical assist. Optimization compliance restored.

Ray’s hip took a rod and two weeks in Dayton. He walked with a cane after that, and went down stairs one at a time, sideways, leading with the good leg. Janelle’s wrist healed crooked. She could still run the mill, but the grip strength never came all the way back, and for four months she ran it on savings because nothing from Kessler reached her.

Tyler Moss was buried in Millbrook. He was twenty-eight.

Three weeks later Vickers was on another managed-asset detail in Lima, Ohio. She had not left Continuity. The statute was the same one she had enforced on the Kessler lawn, and she was still the person they sent when someone said no.

Daniel got Ray and Janelle a lawyer. The lawyer filed against the transfer. The review took four months. The outcome was a settlement that put Ray’s name back on the title: the seven units rebonded under his authority, the remittances resumed, and Janelle’s garage was reclassified as non-commercial personal use, which meant it couldn’t be flagged for optimization unless she put it back on a supplier account.

The shop ran itself again, empty of people, billing in bitcoin, remitting to Ray’s wallet. Janelle worked in her garage, doing the jobs the automated floor couldn’t touch.

Warren’s floor had been desecrated. The robots that used to mill his parts had stood by while a young man died face-down on the gravel and an old man’s hip broke, then kept milling under the managed-asset bond as if nothing had happened. The machines didn’t care. They’d been built not to.

Ray never went back to Millbrook. He sent Janelle the keys and told her the shop was hers if she wanted it. She didn’t want it. She kept working in the garage, because it was hers, and because the boring mill was Warren’s, and because someone had to prove that a body and a pair of hands still mattered when the statute said they didn’t.

The siege was over. Continuity had held the floor for four months and lost it in a settlement. The shop survived. And in the space where Warren’s machinists used to stand, there was only an empty floor and the hum of robots doing work no one was there to see.
