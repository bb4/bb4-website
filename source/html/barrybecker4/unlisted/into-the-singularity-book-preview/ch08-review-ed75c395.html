Chapter 8: Same Asset, Opposite Temperament

Halcyon’s trading floor emptied out by seven most nights. Mara usually stayed another two hours. The trades she made for Halcyon appeared in the firm’s records. The much larger bitcoin bet she was building for herself did not.

That afternoon she’d spent forty minutes talking a nervous client off a position he wanted to sell two weeks too early. She walked him through the same three charts she’d walked him through in March. It worked. It usually worked.

She’d hung up and felt the specific flatness of having spent real skill on a decision she found almost embarrassingly small. The actual decision she cared about sat completely outside the building.

Mara did her real trading at the kitchen counter of her apartment in Chicago at 9 p.m. Three monitors. A spreadsheet she’d built herself to decide how much to buy. A mug of tea going cold. That kitchen, at that hour, was the only room in her life nobody else got a vote in.

She’d believed the same thing for two years before her father died. Governments owed more than they could comfortably repay. As AI took over more work, wages would not remain as dependable as economists had assumed. In that world, bitcoin was not a side bet. It was the main bet. No central bank could create more of it.

She’d said some version of this at a dinner for the firm’s senior managers eighteen months ago. One of them had smiled at her as older men sometimes smiled at a younger woman who had said something correct before it became fashionable.

What she actually remembered about that dinner wasn’t the smile. It was the part before, when somebody’s wife asked where she’d grown up, and Mara heard her own voice give the answer in a way that made Millbrook sound like a place she’d passed through. Nobody at that table was going to find out she was the machine shop’s daughter from the town nobody moved to.

What she had now that she hadn’t had eighteen months ago was capital that was actually hers to deploy without asking anyone’s permission. And a father’s entire fourteen-year silent bet sitting in her wallet.

Devon picked up on the second ring.

They’d known each other since business school. Devon had left a bank to run a small, aggressive company that made loans against bitcoin. Mara needed one of those loans.

“Same terms as we talked about. I want a loan equal to forty percent of what my bitcoin is worth, and I want to use it to buy more bitcoin.”

“You’re aware that’s how people find out what a margin call feels like.”

She knew what he meant. A margin call was the lender demanding money back the moment the price dropped far enough that the bitcoin she’d put up no longer covered the loan.

“I’m aware that’s how people who don’t respect the price that forces a sale find out. I’ve run the number four different ways. The price can fall a long way before that happens.”

“Everybody has room right up until the week they don’t.” Devon didn’t sound worried, exactly. “You know I say this to every client who calls me after nine.”

“You don’t have a lot of clients who call you after nine, Devon.”

“I have exactly enough to notice a pattern. It’s usually grief, or a divorce, or both, dressed up as conviction. I’m not saying you’re wrong about the thesis. I’m saying I’d be a worse friend than banker if I didn’t ask whether the size is about the number or about the last eight weeks.”

“Both,” she said. “Run the paperwork. Aggressive schedule.”

For the next twenty minutes they talked in the quick shorthand of two people who trusted each other’s math. When it was done, the loan had let her increase her bitcoin holdings by forty percent. The lender would force a sale if bitcoin lost somewhere around sixty percent of its value.

She thought about their father while she watched the transaction settle.

Fourteen years, an envelope at a time, never explaining himself. She wondered whether a man who understood the math that early and still only ever bought what a machinist’s paycheck could absorb without flinching had been making a much more disciplined bet than either of his children understood how to make.

She remembered being sixteen, asking him at the kitchen table why he didn’t just put real money on whatever he was so quiet about. The money that scares you into a bad decision isn’t money, it’s a leash, he’d said.

She was, this exact night, holding a leash. She knew that. She had decided it counted as a different category of leash, one she’d chosen with her eyes open.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Janelle.

Hi, sorry to bother you. Ray wanted to know if the trust’s ok with pushing the Q3 equipment budget up a little. New coolant system, not huge. Also hope you’re doing alright.

She typed back Sounds fine, have Ray send the numbers to the estate email, thanks before she’d fully finished reading the second sentence.

She saw hope you’re doing alright sitting there unanswered. She did not answer it. The phone went back onto the counter face-down.

Halcyon’s own investment fund kept one percent of its money in a regulated fund that tracked bitcoin’s price.

Mara sat through Wednesday’s risk review nodding at slides about “measured digital asset exposure,” a tiny official bitcoin bet, with the practiced blankness of someone keeping a much larger one entirely off any slide anyone in that room would ever see.

Her manager, Farid, caught her after the meeting.

“You’ve seemed somewhere else lately.”

“Lot going on. Family stuff.”

“The room noticed you disagreeing with the crypto allocation slide without saying anything out loud. That’s usually when you say the most out loud.”

“The committee picked one percent because if it goes to zero, nobody has to explain much to the board. If it goes up, everybody gets to say they saw it coming. It’s not a serious bet. It’s protection against being wrong for not betting.”

“That’s an interesting theory for someone whose personal-holdings form I approved eight months ago. Haven’t seen it updated since.”

“I’ve been busy.”

“Update it by Friday. I’m not asking as your manager. I’m asking as someone who’d rather not be the person explaining to compliance why he didn’t.”

She said she would.

She almost told him the rest of it. My personal bitcoin is twelve times larger than the last number I put on that form. It came partly from my dead father and partly from a loan designed so nobody here would see it.

“I’ll be more disagreeable next time,” she said, and Farid laughed like she’d meant it as a joke, and she let him.

She checked the number one more time before bed.

The number was up since the settlement that afternoon. Not by a lot. Just the ordinary grinding appreciation of an asset a growing number of people were starting to take as seriously as she’d been taking it alone at managing directors’ dinners for two years.

She thought, turning off the monitor, that this was probably the best she’d felt in the two months since Warren was gone.

She decided she’d examine that question later, once more gains had made the loan safer. Later, like most things this family postponed and called handling.
