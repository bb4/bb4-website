Chapter 16: The Unwind

By the autumn of 2031 the forty-five bitcoin Mara had inherited were worth just under thirty-eight million dollars at eight hundred thirty-eight thousand each, and she had borrowed against them more than once, through a lending desk outside compliance. Argentina had voted bitcoin legal tender that same season, and Daniel had called to say Backstop Bond was live: underwrite, not review; bitcoin in escrow before an agent acted unsupervised. She had congratulated him and heard the opposite of her own structure in the same asset.

The first crewed lunar landing in sixty years happened on a Monday in the spring of 2032. For about thirty-six hours it was the only story anyone could hold in their head at once. Mara watched the feed standing at her kitchen counter, four people in white suits crossing grey dust no human being had touched since 1972, and left for the office twenty minutes late.

By Wednesday morning it was a sidebar. Congress had failed, overnight, to pass the borrowing authority that kept the federal government’s own payments moving. Treasury payments seized in stages. Rating agencies moved before the markets opened in New York.

Mara texted her desk before the open.

Watching?

Watching. Don’t do anything. Give the open an hour.

The open didn’t stop at an hour. Equities went first, then real estate names. Bitcoin moved with them, not first, not alone, just in the same violent, indiscriminate company as everything else people had borrowed against.

By the close it was down nineteen percent from Monday and two regional banks had halted withdrawals for “operational review.” The desk called at 4:07.

“Say the number out loud.”

“Six hundred seventy-nine thousand.”

“And the cushion.”

“Thirteen percent left before you call me about margin.”

Two years ago that answer would have been sixty. At one and a half times her own money, bitcoin had to fall sixty percent before anyone could touch her coins. She had gone to two times in the spring, and the desk had made her say the new number out loud before they would write the paper. She had said it, and signed.

“Ridgeline put out a statement twenty minutes ago. They’re ‘reviewing covenant compliance.’ That’s the sentence a treasury company writes when it’s about to sell the thing its entire balance sheet is made of. If they dump into this, your thirteen percent becomes a different conversation before the close tomorrow.”

“Ridgeline’s been reviewing covenant compliance since Christmas. It’s theater.”

“It was theater at eight hundred thirty-eight. At six seventy-nine it’s math.”

Thursday morning two European spreads blew out before London opened, and a mid-sized Asian sovereign that had been quietly rolling short-term paper for eighteen months missed a refinancing window. By nine Chicago time sovereign debt crisis was on every terminal and the banks were seeing withdrawal traffic their own models flagged as cascade risk.

Bitcoin was down twenty-four percent from Monday when Ridgeline’s second statement hit the tape at 10:41.

Not reviewing. Selling. A scheduled, disclosed, covenant-required liquidation over the next seventy-two hours.

Within seven minutes the price had dropped through six hundred thousand. Mara’s liquidation threshold sat at five hundred eighty-six thousand six hundred. She had done that arithmetic already. There was nothing to do with it but watch.

The desk called at 11:03.

“I’m sorry,” the voice said. “You’re done. Collateral and borrowed exposure both. The coins, the loan, the position you built on top of the position. All of it.”

She sat very still and watched the number keep falling without her. The terror arrived. Not the loss of the money, exactly. The older recognition underneath it. That she had not, after all, gotten out. The Kessler girl from the machine shop. Still. Again.

Farid found her twenty minutes later.

“My office. Now.”

She went. The Moon feed was still running, muted, on the television behind his desk.

“How much of it was yours?”

“All of the personal piece. None of it through the firm.”

“I told you last fall to keep the paperwork current.” He looked at her for a long time. “Go home, Mara. Don’t come in tomorrow. We’ll talk Monday about what still exists of your seat here.”

She stood. The rage arrived all at once, not at him, not even at the desk, but at herself for having signed. She picked up the ceramic mug from his desk and threw it at the wall. It shattered. Coffee ran down the paint.

Farid didn’t move.

“I’m done,” she said. “With this conversation. With pretending a crash this size makes anyone whole. With all of it.”

She left. He let her go.

Daniel heard about Ridgeline from Sanjay at Thursday noon. Backstop’s Round Rock office had the wrong quiet of a room full of people refreshing pages.

Full-reserve bitcoin, no leverage, every catastrophic claim Backstop had ever underwritten. At Thursday’s price the reserve ratio had compressed below the standard Daniel set when Bond went live. Still solvent. Still adequate by any honest measure. In a week when fractional and synthetic competitors were quietly collapsing, adequate was not what clients wanted to hear.

Sanjay stood in the doorway with Concord’s email open. Updated attestation or they pulled the renewal. Three other clients had forwarded the same template.

“We need to add reserves,” Daniel said. “Real coins. Prove we’re not Ridgeline.”

Ellis had already run it. Daniel turned the monitor.

Borrow 25% against the inherited 45.7 BTC. Buy ~11.4 BTC at crash prices. Restore the ratio. Loan stays safe even if the price falls another 75%.

He thought about selling a slice of the inherited coins. Then he thought about the 2018 entry in Warren’s journal, where his father had talked himself out of selling everything in a crash and written down why while it was still going the wrong way. He was not going to sell in this one.

“Dad’s coins,” Daniel said. “Twenty-five percent. Buy at the crash. Put them in the reserve.”

Sanjay read it twice. “That’s what she did.”

“No. She borrowed to amplify gains on the way up. I’m borrowing to backstop claims on the way down. Same asset, handled the other way.”

He wrote Elena before he called Mara. Thursday, not their standing Tuesday.

Borrowing against the coins. Not selling. Long story.

Tell me Tuesday. The whole thing. And call your sister first, not after.

He called Mara that night.

“I heard,” he said. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t. If you say I’m sorry in the voice people use when they mean I told you so and are too decent to say it, I’m going to hang up.”

“I wasn’t going to say I told you so.”

“I know. That’s worse, in a way.” She was quiet. “Farid sent me home. I broke his mug. Compliance is going to eat whatever the market didn’t. You were supposed to call me this quarter. About capital. I can’t be that call anymore.”

“I know. I’m calling to tell you what I’m doing instead.”

He told her.

“You’re giving yourself a seventy-five-percent drawdown cushion. I needed thirty percent and didn’t have it.”

“I know.”

“Do the loan. Fund the reserves. Don’t make this week about me being a cautionary tale in your board deck. Just do the version of it I didn’t.”

She hung up first. He signed the custody documents and moved the collateral on chain that night.

The text from Carol Weiss came in late Thursday night.

Ray Delgado filed to buy Kessler this afternoon. Option from the will, five years due. He closes next week. Shop’s already empty. Cheaper robots and the stimulus finished the floor after the crash. He bought the building and the billing. Not the people.

Daniel read it and did not forward it to Mara. Not the same week she lost everything.

Janelle was in the garage when Daniel called. Friday morning. Warren’s machine, the boring-and-facing one she had bought at scrap value, had a job on it that could wait.

“Carol Weiss texted last night,” he said. “Ray filed to buy Kessler. The option from the will. He closes next week. The shop is already empty.”

“I know. Marisol called.”

“There’s a file on Dad’s laptop we never got into. The first password was taped inside a logbook. This one isn’t written down anywhere in the house. I’ve had the machine three years. I think the file’s for you.”

She looked at the machine. She had not offered him a guess in 2029, in her kitchen, when he and Mara brought the laptop over after the house sale. She had not said the line since.

“Hold onto something you don’t need yet,” she said. “He told me that once, locking the office.”

Keys on his end. “No.”

“All lowercase, no spaces. That’s how he wrote the shop’s wifi on the wall for twenty years.”

More keys, and a longer wait.

“It’s open.” She heard him let go of a breath. “Thirty characters. Nobody was ever going to guess it. He picked a sentence one person alive could say back to him. I’m sending it. I’m not going to read it.”

The text came through on her pane. Warren’s voice, terse.

For J. Not the kids. Not Ray. Carol has the option written the way it has to be written. I couldn’t put you on the building. They would have fought it, and Ray would have heard it as an insult. The one coin is a down payment, not a thank-you. When the five years are up, if you want in, you have the money. You are under no obligation. That part I did put in the will. If you don’t, let Ray have it. He’ll buy it even empty. That’s not stupid.

She could spend the coin and own a building that billed without people. Or she could leave it where it was, in the coffee can, and let Ray’s name go on the empty shop.

“I’m not buying an empty building,” she said. “I work on parts. Tell Carol to let Ray close. Don’t tell Ray about this.”

“It’s yours. You can still…”

“I don’t want it, Daniel. I wanted the floor when there were people on it. That isn’t this.”

“All right.”

She ended the call, put the pane down next to the machine, and went back to the job.

By the Friday open the borrowed dollars were coins in Backstop’s reserve, bought into Ridgeline’s selling.

A regulatory briefing Sanjay forwarded the next week listed the institutions that had needed a rescue, and then a short column headed no facility drawn. Backstop Bond was in it: full-reserve bitcoin, auditable, zero public money. Victor’s Bureau would have filed the same line. Daniel did not call him. Recused still meant recused.

Within a month Backstop Bond was the only full-reserve bonding platform to come through without external rescue. Clients stopped pulling renewals. New ones signed up because Backstop had weathered it. The boring structure became the industry standard because the crash killed everything else.

He did not sell a single inherited coin. The loan was never in danger.
