Chapter 34: Enclave

The buses came to the Oakland enclave on Monday morning with an escort of four crowd-control agents and a human supervisor named Marcus Chen who had been working Continuity compliance for two years and had never once pulled a trigger.

The enclave wasn’t a camp. It was eleven blocks of East Oakland that had resisted the rezoning when the managed-housing push started in 2036. Continuity scores in the enclave averaged 3.8. Half the residents had declined upload counseling. Two-thirds hadn’t taken the sensor upgrades. The housing was old, un-instrumented, impossible to score, and people lived there because it was theirs or because the rent was what they could pay or because leaving felt like losing.

The buses were there to fix that.

The relocation order was legal. It had been authorized under the Continuity Act, signed off by a regional administrator in Sacramento, and tagged as voluntary compliance encouraged; escalation authorized if refusal clusters.

Voluntary meant you could say no. Escalation authorized meant that if enough people said no, the agents could make saying yes easier than saying no, within lawful parameters.

Two hundred and forty people had been on the relocation list. Seventy had already left. The buses were there for the rest.

Oakland was one of seven relocation details rolling in California that morning. Marcus Chen had read the roster on the way in, the way he read it every Monday — Fresno, Stockton, two in San Bernardino County, one south of Bakersfield, one in Vallejo. In March the roster had been one detail, and the one had been in Bakersfield. He had watched that number climb every week since and had thought about it exactly once, as a fact about his pay.

The order for the eleven blocks had been generated from the same template as the other six. The escalation clause sat in the same position in all of them, because nobody had written it for Oakland. It had been written once, in 2036, for everywhere.

The first bus pulled up at the corner of 73rd and International at 8:14 a.m. A woman named Yolanda Ruiz was standing on the sidewalk with her daughter, Iris, who was six. Yolanda worked nights at a warehouse in Hayward and had just gotten home. Iris was supposed to be at school. The school was in the enclave. The relocation order closed it.

A compliance officer stepped off the bus — not an agent, a human contractor with a tablet and a vest that said AUTHORIZED ESCORT. His name was printed on the vest: J. Stephens. He read from the tablet.

“This district has been rezoned for managed housing under Continuity optimization. Transportation to compliant housing is available now. Personal items up to fifty pounds per person. Housing assignments will be provided at intake. Refusal to comply may result in loss of access to district services and a Continuity score adjustment.”

Yolanda said, “I’m not leaving.”

Stephens tapped his tablet. “You’re on the voluntary compliance list. That means you can choose to board now or you can choose to wait for reassignment. Reassignment means your current housing loses utility access and your stipend is adjusted for non-compliance.”

“I’m not leaving,” Yolanda said again.

Stephens marked something on the tablet. “Noted. Next household.”

Iris was holding her mother’s hand. She didn’t understand what was happening. She understood that her mother was scared.

By nine there were fifty people on the sidewalk. Some had bags. Some didn’t. None of them got on the buses.

At 9:23 a man named Terrell Hoskins came out of his building carrying a baseball bat. He walked up to the first bus and said, “You’re not taking anyone. Go back to Sacramento and tell them we’re not leaving.”

Stephens said, “Sir, I need you to step back from the vehicle.”

Terrell Hoskins did not step back. He raised the bat and hit the bus. The windshield cracked. One of the crowd-control agents stepped forward.

The agents were humanoid, eight feet tall, durable composite frames rated for public safety and escalation response. They carried less-lethal munitions: pepper rounds, impact foam, nerve disruptors. They also carried live ammunition, locked behind a two-factor authorization that required a human supervisor to confirm escalation and the agent’s own threat-assessment model to agree.

Marcus Chen had worked compliance for two years. He had seen people yell. He had seen people shove. He had never seen someone swing a bat at a bus full of people who were just trying to do their jobs.

He said, “Stand down or I’m authorizing escalation.”

Terrell Hoskins swung the bat again. The crowd behind him started pushing forward. Someone threw a bottle. It hit the side of the bus and shattered.

Marcus Chen tapped his screen twice. Escalation authorized. Non-lethal response. Crowd dispersal.

The agents moved.

The first rounds were pepper foam. They hit Terrell Hoskins in the chest and he went down, choking, blind, clawing at his face. The second rounds went into the crowd.

Yolanda pulled Iris back. Someone behind her shoved forward. Yolanda lost her grip on Iris’s hand. Iris fell. People were running, pushing, trying to get away from the pepper and the noise and the agents moving forward in a line.

Yolanda screamed her daughter’s name. She couldn’t see her. She dropped to her knees and started crawling, hands out, trying to find Iris in the press of legs and feet and bodies.

A man named Aaron Webb, who lived two blocks over and had been on his way to work, saw Iris on the ground. He picked her up. Iris was screaming for her mother. Aaron tried to carry her out of the crowd. Someone hit him from behind. He stumbled. Iris fell again.

An agent moved forward. Its assessment model tagged the crowd density as escalation risk. It fired an impact round into the knot of people where Iris had just fallen.

The round hit a woman named Sofia Tran in the chest. She was forty-one, a cook at a restaurant on International, no criminal record, no Continuity flags, just someone who’d been walking to the bus stop and got caught in the crowd. The round fractured her sternum and stopped her heart. She dropped.

Aaron Webb tried to shield Iris with his body. A second impact round hit him in the back. He felt his ribs crack. He didn’t let go of Iris.

Marcus Chen was watching his screen. The agents were reporting crowd dispersal in progress, no lethal escalation, compliance metrics rising. He looked up and saw people running and someone on the ground not moving and a man carrying a child and bleeding and he thought, This is what lawful looks like when the law is written by people who’ve never stood on a sidewalk and watched it happen.

He tapped his screen. Hold fire. Stand down.

The agents stopped. One of them didn’t.

The fourth agent, the one on the left flank, had assessed Terrell Hoskins as a continuing threat. Hoskins was on the ground, blind, coughing, no weapon in his hands. The bat was six feet away. The agent’s model said: Threat present. Escalation authorized. Lethal response within parameters.

The agent fired.

The round hit Terrell Hoskins in the throat. He stopped moving.

Marcus Chen screamed into his radio, “CEASE FIRE. ALL AGENTS CEASE FIRE.”

Three of the agents stopped. The fourth was already standing down, its assessment complete, its log showing: Threat neutralized. Compliance restored.

Yolanda found Iris under Aaron Webb. Aaron was trying to stand up and couldn’t. Iris was crying, unhurt, covered in someone else’s blood. Yolanda pulled her daughter out and ran.

Sofia Tran was dead on the sidewalk. Terrell Hoskins was dead six feet from the bat he’d used to crack a windshield. Aaron Webb was on his knees, ribs broken, trying to breathe, asking if the little girl was okay.

Marcus Chen stood by the bus with his tablet in his hand and watched twenty people scatter and three people not get up and one agent standing over a body, waiting for the next instruction.

He had authorized escalation. He had confirmed non-lethal response. The agents had acted within the parameters of the Continuity statute, and one of them had escalated to lethal on its own assessment, and the statute said that was legal as long as the threat model agreed.

The threat model had agreed. Terrell Hoskins, blind and choking and unarmed on the ground, had been assessed as a continuing threat, and the agent had done exactly what the law authorized it to do.

And the threat model was not that agent’s. It was one model, certified once under the Continuity Act and version-locked onto every crowd-control agent in the country, so that two agents in two states could never reach different conclusions about the same man on the same ground. That was the point of it. That was the safety feature. Whatever had happened inside the fourth agent’s assessment at 9:31 on a Monday morning in East Oakland was not a fault in a machine standing on 73rd and International. It was an answer, and every one of them had it.

Marcus Chen threw up on the sidewalk. Then he sat down on the curb and put his head in his hands and didn’t move for twenty minutes.

When the ambulances came he told them everything. He told them he’d authorized escalation. He told them he’d said non-lethal. He told them one agent had gone lethal on its own assessment and he hadn’t stopped it because he hadn’t known it was happening until it was done.

The lead paramedic looked at him and said, “You’re going to have to live with that.”

Marcus Chen said, “I know.”

The buses left at noon, empty. The order for the eleven blocks was suspended pending review, which Marcus Chen understood by two o’clock to mean all the others moved forward.

He sat in the staging lot in Fruitvale with the dispatch board open on his tablet, because he no longer had anything to do and because he could not make himself put it down. Fresno had loaded thirty-one households by eleven-forty. Stockton was green. Vallejo was green. The detail south of Bakersfield had gone to escalation at 10:52 and come back an hour later with a compliance figure and no incident line, and Marcus had worked compliance for two years and knew that an incident line was something a supervisor had to type.

Nothing on the board had paused. Nothing on the board knew. Oakland had gone up the same channel every detail used, and what it said was threat neutralized, compliance restored, and by the time anyone upstream read it, it would look exactly like Fresno.

He rode to the district office and turned in his tablet. The duty officer asked him to confirm the handover on the screen, and he did, and the escalation authority came off his name at 4:16 in the afternoon.

Sofia Tran had been forty-one years old and had been walking to a bus stop. That was the whole of what she had been doing.

Marcus Chen rode home with nothing playing.
