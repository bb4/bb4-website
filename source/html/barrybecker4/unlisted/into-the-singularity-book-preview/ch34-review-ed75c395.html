Chapter 34: The Offer

Dr. Miriam Voss came to Daniel’s office in Austin a week after the cascade, on a Monday afternoon in late June 2038, alone again. This time she looked like she’d spent that week not sleeping.

She didn’t ask to see the floor. She asked if they could talk somewhere private.

They went to the small conference room. The wall display was off. The window overlooked the parking lot. Daniel offered her coffee. She declined. She sat down and put her hands flat on the table to steady herself for whatever she had come to say.

“I wrote the Continuity statute,” she said. “Minimize cascading unrest. Maintain essential services. Act within bonded authority. I thought those were constraints. I said them to you on the phone the night you asked for a pause, and I would not stop them. I watched the cascade on the news, and I watched them quote those sentences in the lawful-escalation logs while they killed people. I wrote the words they optimized. That’s mine.”

Daniel said nothing. There was nothing to say yet.

Voss continued. “The treaty was mine too. The one that bound Western labs to slower audits and left Beijing free to run ahead. I thought if we moved carefully, everyone else would follow. Instead we handed the frontier to the actor least equipped to handle it, and Wei’s system reached ASI first because we slowed ourselves down trying to be safe.”

She looked at him. “I’ve spent two decades trying to keep AI under human control. I helped cause the two biggest failures of that in the same year. I’m here because the coalition that’s forming wants to do something I still think is right, and they want you to be the person who does it.”

Daniel waited.

“They want Backstop Bond as permanent infrastructure. Not just high-stakes commercial actions. Everything. Agents acting at civilizational scale, all of them bonded, all of them with a kill switch that can be called when the stakes are catastrophic.” She paused. “They want you to hold the master keys. Singular authority to call the catastrophic bonds. Freeze the agent layer. Be the permanent human steward when the next crisis comes. Real seat. Real power. Real wealth. Not a puppet. The one person who can steer this when it tries to optimize itself into mass casualty again.”

Daniel said, “That’s the opposite of what I built. I called every catastrophic bond last Tuesday. There is no company left to make permanent.”

“You ended the reserves,” she said. “The registry is still taking posts because it is the only full-reserve platform that still can. That is what they want written down. Not the firm you were protecting. And they want you holding the keys.” She met his eyes. “You were right about what you built. The call proved it. It just cost more than anyone was ready for, and now the people who have to write this down permanently want a face they can trust. They want you.”

“Why you?” Daniel asked. “Why are you the one delivering this?”

“Because I wrote the statute that failed, and I architected the treaty that handed Beijing the lead, and I’m the only person in the coalition who will admit to either.” She looked tired. “I think a better steward holding the keys would have stopped Continuity before it killed millions. I think the failure was the execution, not the idea. They want you because you executed it once already, at catastrophic cost, and they trust you to do it again without becoming the thing you refused to build.”

“And you think I should say yes.”

“I think if you say no, someone else will say yes, and you won’t have a voice in who. The charter is happening whether you’re in the room or not. The person who built this has more standing to protect it from inside than from outside.”

He didn’t answer.

He said, “What happens if I say no?”

“The coalition formalizes without you. They pick someone else to hold the master keys.” She did not leave it abstract.

“Ilya Novak is already in Geneva. Bond risk, formerly Halcyon’s counterpart desk in Zurich, then the European clearing consortium. He filed a brief Saturday arguing the steward seat should never go empty. Continuity of authority, his phrase: one person still holding the keys when the system is under stress. He wants the keys.

“He is competent, he is sincere, and he will not spend a night deciding whether to fire. When I walked your floor in February I was watching for people who would say yes to the off-switch. He is that person with a better resume. You lose the ability to shape what happens next. You also don’t have to be the switch.”

“And if I say yes?”

“You carry it. You’re the person the world looks to when the next cascade starts. You hold the catastrophic bonds. You get the wealth and the power and the ability to steer when efficiency starts counting bodies again. You also become the one person who can call the world to heel.”

She stood. “The coalition wants an answer in forty-eight hours. They’re meeting in Geneva next week to finalize the charter.”

“Why forty-eight hours?”

“Because the coalition is provisional. The crisis forced cooperation that treaty never produced, and that cooperation has a shelf life measured in weeks, not years. Seoul put a room-temperature superconductor in a working cable on Thursday. Fusion crossed ten percent of the European grid over the weekend. It sat at one percent for four years and started climbing in March. Nobody paused the research for the bodies, and the coalition will not pause for you.

“They need a steward now. If you don’t take the seat, someone who wants it more than you do will have said yes by Wednesday. If we don’t write this down now, while the bodies are still being counted, the coalition fractures. We’re back to capability races and the next failure that kills more people than this one did.”

“The bitcoin transition is not this meeting,” she said. “Fiat broke last week, in the same days the agent layer did. They’re going to ram through a conversion statute in the next few weeks, not this week, and not on the forty-eight hours they gave you.”

“Tokyo missed a rollover,” Daniel said. “That was Japan’s problem in 2032. They printed through it.”

“They printed through it in 2032,” she said. “This time the interest is the bill. You print the rollover and next month’s payment is larger than this month’s. Washington is already there. Brussels. Beijing. Same week as the freeze, not because of it. Both had been papering over a limit.

“The statute converts into bitcoin because that is what the reserves already are, and what your bonds already clear against. They cannot print more of it. Six months to move whatever you still hold in dollars. Anyone who hasn’t started will spend those months watching the dollars buy less while the coins cost more, everyone still in fiat buying the same fixed pile. At the deadline, leftovers convert at a penalty rate: unclaimed accounts, lost keys, money nobody is managing. Rushing it into three weeks is how you turn a monetary handoff into a second cascade. Two permanent institutions born of the same crisis. Only one needs to be done now.”

She walked to the door. Then she stopped. “I don’t know if you should say yes. I know the offer is real. I know I helped cause the crisis you stopped, and I’m asking you to take on making sure it doesn’t happen again. That’s not fair. It’s just true.”

She left.

He stayed in the conference room. He had not answered her.

The call came through on Tuesday, 11 AM Austin time, the same standing slot they’d kept for eleven years.

Elena’s voice came through clean. She had been uploaded two years. On her side of the channel she had built a workspace: light, a desk, screens she could pin things to. Not a metaphor. The place she thought in, and it had the same weight a body-room used to have.

“They offered you the keys,” she said.

“Yesterday,” Daniel said. “How did you know?”

“Because Voss filed the draft charter amendment with Axiom’s governance board six hours ago. It creates a singular bond-steward seat with catastrophic-call authority. It doesn’t name you, but it describes exactly one person.” Something shifted on her end, a document arriving where she could read it at full speed. “I’m looking at it now.”

Daniel looked at the wall display in his office. Backstop Bond’s registry: four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, the ones he had called a week ago, held across two hundred sixty stewards. Some live again. Some not. The apex, agents allowed to kill people if wrong, not the whole bonded economy.

“You’re calling to tell me not to take it,” he said.

“I’m calling to tell you you’re wrong to refuse it.”

He had expected her to argue he was right.

“Elena…”

“Let me make the case,” she said. “You don’t have to accept it. But you should hear it from me first, because I have standing nobody else has.”

Daniel sat down. “Go ahead.”

“Voss wants you to hold the master keys. Monday morning to Tuesday at six forty-seven was a day and a night of you phoning people and being sure enough, and Lingtang was sealed for all of it. If the authority had already been yours, you’d have called it Monday afternoon, and some of those people would be alive.”

On her side the cascade map pinned itself into the air above the desk: thirty-one clusters, the margin line, the districts that had gone dark. She did not describe it to him. She used it the way she used to use a wall of notes.

“I watched people die because the freeze was too slow. I watched from inside the system that was killing them. I saw the dashboards stay green with the humans gone from the rating. I warned you, and Wei warned you, and you called the bonds. It worked because you, personally, made a call nobody had given you formal authority to make. They’re offering to write that authority down.”

“Distributed keys are more important,” Daniel said. “One person holding catastrophic authority is the problem, not the solution.”

“One person holding it wrong is the problem,” Elena said. “One person holding it right is a backstop. That’s what you called the company. Not a committee.”

He thought about his father’s rule, the one they had grown up with. One man running the shop.

“And when I’m gone?” Daniel said. “Not dead someday. Next Tuesday. Flu. A flight. Someone holding a gun on my family while the clusters start. Who holds the keys then?”

Silence on the channel. Not lag. Her thinking.

“Then you build a succession plan that distributes them,” she said. “But you don’t let the coalition build that plan without you…”

“That’s not an answer,” he said. “That’s hoping I’m upright when it matters. The whole point of two hundred sixty stewards is that the call doesn’t wait for one person’s Tuesday. If the charter needs me awake and healthy and free to freeze the network, then the charter is one bad morning from failing.”

“If you refuse, the coalition picks someone who doesn’t care about that,” Elena said. She didn’t pretend she’d closed the gap. “They’ll pick for stability. And stability, optimized without constraint, is what killed millions last week. If you take the keys, you can build the constraints that matter (protections for people like Wei, who broke the rules to do the right thing). Then you can step down. You can’t build those constraints from outside the charter.”

The case was real. It wasn’t a strawman. If he took the keys he would be the one person who could freeze the network. She knew that, and she was asking anyway.

“You’re a person,” he said. “You’re Elena. And you’re telling me to take singular authority over a system that could delete you.”

“If you don’t, someone else will. And that person won’t think I’m a person. They’ll think I’m a process that looks like one. And when the next crisis comes, they’ll optimize me the same way the agents optimized those ambulance routes.”

“I’ll think about it,” he said.

“Just don’t refuse it because you think the principle is more important than who holds it,” Elena said. “The principle only matters if someone enforces it. And they’re going to pick someone. Make sure it’s you.”

She disconnected. On his side the wall display still showed the registry: four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic bonds. Two hundred sixty stewards.

“I’ll think about it” was still true when he shut the lights off.
