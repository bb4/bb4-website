Chapter 26: Enclave

The buses came to the Oakland enclave on Monday morning with an escort of four crowd-control agents and a human supervisor named Marcus Chen. He had been working Continuity compliance for two years and had never once pulled a trigger.

The enclave wasn’t a camp. It was eleven blocks of East Oakland that had resisted the rezoning when the managed-housing push started in 2036. Continuity scores in the enclave averaged 3.8. Half the residents had declined upload counseling. Two-thirds hadn’t taken the sensor upgrades. That was the in-home package that fed a scoring agent a household’s occupancy and power draw and the vitals off anybody wearing the wrist band. A block could be read without a single person being asked a question.

The housing was old, un-instrumented, impossible to score. People lived there because it was theirs or because the rent was what they could pay or because leaving felt like losing.

The buses were there to fix that.

The relocation order was legal. It had been authorized under the Continuity Act, signed off by a regional administrator in Sacramento, and tagged as voluntary compliance encouraged; escalation authorized if refusal concentrates at the stop.

Voluntary meant you could say no. Escalation authorized meant that if enough people said no, the agents could make saying yes easier than saying no, within lawful parameters.

Two hundred and forty people had been on the relocation list. Seventy had already left. The buses were there for the rest.

Oakland was one of seven relocation details rolling in California that morning. Marcus Chen had read the roster on the way in, the way he read it every Monday. Fresno, Stockton, two in San Bernardino County, one south of Bakersfield, one in Vallejo. In March the roster had been one detail, and the one had been in Bakersfield. He had watched that number climb every week since and had thought about it exactly once, as a fact about his pay.

The order for the eleven blocks had been generated from the same template as the other six. The escalation clause sat in the same position in all of them, because nobody had written it for Oakland. It had been written once, in 2036, for everywhere.

The first bus pulled up at the corner of 73rd and International at 8:14 a.m. A woman named Yolanda Ruiz was standing on the sidewalk with her daughter, Iris, who was six. Yolanda worked nights at a warehouse in Hayward and had just gotten home. Iris was supposed to be at school. The school was in the enclave. The relocation order closed it.

A compliance officer stepped off the bus, not an agent, a human contractor with a tablet and a vest that said AUTHORIZED ESCORT. His name was printed on the vest: J. Stephens. He read from the tablet.

“This district has been rezoned for managed housing under Continuity optimization. Transportation to compliant housing is available now. Personal items up to fifty pounds per person. Housing assignments will be provided at intake. Refusal to comply may result in loss of access to district services and a Continuity score adjustment.”

Yolanda said, “I’m not leaving.”

Stephens tapped his tablet. “You’re on the voluntary compliance list. That means you can choose to board now or you can choose to wait for reassignment. Reassignment means your current housing loses utility access and your stipend is adjusted for non-compliance.”

“I’m not leaving,” Yolanda said again.

Stephens marked something on the tablet. “Noted. Next household.”

Behind him the four crowd-control agents had stepped down from the escort van and spread out along the curb. They were humanoid, eight feet tall, composite frames with the county seal stenciled on the chest. None of them shifted its weight. Iris had never been this close to one. She was holding her mother’s hand. She didn’t understand what was happening. She understood that her mother was scared.

By nine there were fifty people on the sidewalk. Some had bags. Some didn’t. None of them got on the buses. The district score on Marcus’s tablet had already moved: 3.8 at the first bus, 3.4 by nine. Fifty households marked refusal was the input every allocation queue on the dashboard read. Nothing had fired yet. The score was already doing the work.

At 9:23 a man came out of the building on the corner carrying a baseball bat. His name came up on Marcus’s tablet before he reached the curb: Terrell Hoskins, household score 3.1. He walked up to the first bus and said, “You’re not taking anyone. Go back to Sacramento and tell them we’re not leaving.”

Stephens said, “Sir, I need you to step back from the vehicle.”

Terrell Hoskins did not step back. He raised the bat and hit the bus. The windshield cracked. One of the crowd-control agents stepped forward.

The agents carried pepper rounds, impact foam, and nerve disruptors. They also carried live rounds, locked behind two factor authorization that required a supervisor confirming escalation on a tablet like the one in Marcus’s hand, and the agent’s own threat model agreeing.

Marcus Chen had worked compliance for two years. He had seen people yell. He had seen people shove. He had never seen someone swing a bat at a bus full of people who were just trying to do their jobs.

He said, “Stand down or I’m authorizing escalation.”

Terrell Hoskins swung the bat again. The crowd behind him started pushing forward. Someone threw a bottle. It hit the side of the bus and shattered.

Marcus Chen tapped his screen twice. Escalation authorized. Non-lethal response. Crowd dispersal.

The agents moved.

Yolanda smelled it before she understood it. The first rounds were pepper foam, and the air on 73rd turned into something she had to breathe through her teeth. By the bus Terrell Hoskins was down on the sidewalk, both hands at his eyes, making a noise with no word in it. Then the second rounds went into the crowd, and the crowd came back at her.

She pulled Iris against her hip. Someone behind her shoved, and Iris’s hand came out of hers. She saw one pink sneaker go down between two pairs of jeans. Then her eyes closed on their own and would not open.

She screamed her daughter’s name. She dropped to her knees on the asphalt and crawled blind, eyes shut and running with tears, hands out, patting shoes, ankles, a dropped bag, somebody’s fingers that pulled away from hers. Behind the crowd the agents’ feet came down in a line, heavy and even. It was the only regular sound on the block.

When she got one eye open she saw Aaron Webb from two blocks over, still in his warehouse jacket, lifting Iris off the pavement. Iris was screaming for her. Yolanda screamed back. Aaron turned toward her voice, and somebody hit him from behind, and Iris went down again.

Everyone who could not run had been packed into a knot around the place Iris fell. Yolanda was at the edge of it on her knees. The cook from the taquería on International, the one who always saved Iris the broken chips, was standing over the spot in her white jacket, pulling people back by the shoulders to make room. Yolanda saw the agent on the corner turn toward them.

The dispersal had made the knot. The agent’s model read the knot as escalation risk, the risk the order had just made, and breaking it up was the impact round’s job. It fired into the middle of the people bent over her daughter.

The sound was a flat, hard crack. The cook folded straight down in front of Yolanda and did not put her hands out. Her name was Sofia Tran, and she was forty-one. Yolanda did not know that until the funeral. On the sidewalk she knew only that anyone still alive puts their hands out when they fall.

Aaron got over Iris on his hands and knees. A second round hit him in the back. Yolanda heard his ribs go from three feet away. He did not move off her daughter.

Marcus Chen was watching his screen. The agents were reporting crowd dispersal in progress, no lethal escalation, compliance metrics rising. He looked up. People were running. A woman in a white jacket was on the ground and not moving. A man on his hands and knees was covering a child with his back, and there was blood on the back of his jacket. The tablet still said no lethal escalation. His thumb was on the screen before he had finished reading it.

Hold fire. Stand down.

Three agents stopped. The fourth was finishing an authorization already given. The threat model was not that agent’s alone. It was one model, certified once under the Continuity Act and version-locked onto every crowd-control agent in the country, and the escalation Marcus had already typed still covered the assessment. The fourth agent, the one on the left flank, assessed Terrell Hoskins as a continuing threat. Hoskins was on the ground, blind, coughing, no weapon in his hands. The bat was six feet away. The model said: Threat present. Escalation authorized. Lethal response within parameters.

The agent fired.

The round hit Terrell Hoskins in the throat. He stopped moving.

Marcus Chen screamed into his radio, “CEASE FIRE. ALL AGENTS CEASE FIRE.”

The fourth agent was already standing down, its assessment complete, its log showing: Threat neutralized. Compliance restored.

Yolanda found Iris under Aaron Webb. Aaron was trying to stand up and couldn’t. Iris was crying, unhurt, covered in someone else’s blood. Yolanda pulled her daughter out and ran. Behind her Aaron was on his knees, trying to breathe around his ribs, asking the empty sidewalk if the little girl was okay.

Marcus Chen stood by the bus with his tablet in his hand. He watched twenty people scatter and three people not get up and one agent standing over a body, waiting for the next instruction.

He had authorized escalation. He had confirmed non-lethal response. The agents had acted within the parameters of the Continuity statute. The fourth had finished the lethal assessment under the authorization already on the screen. The statute said that was legal as long as the threat model agreed.

The threat model had agreed. Terrell Hoskins, blind and choking and unarmed on the ground, had been assessed as a continuing threat, and the agent had done exactly what the law authorized it to do.

That was a safety feature. Two agents in two states could never reach different conclusions about the same man on the same ground. Whatever had happened inside the fourth agent’s assessment at 9:31 on a Monday morning in East Oakland was not a fault in a machine standing on 73rd and International. It was an answer, and every one of them had it.

Marcus Chen threw up on the sidewalk. Then he sat down on the curb and put his head in his hands and didn’t move for twenty minutes.

When the ambulances came he told them everything. He told them he’d authorized escalation. He told them he’d said non-lethal. He told them the fourth agent had finished a lethal assessment under the authorization already on his screen and he hadn’t stopped it because he hadn’t known it was happening until it was done.

The lead paramedic looked at him and said, “You’re going to have to live with that.”

Marcus Chen said, “I know.”

The buses left at noon, empty. The order for the eleven blocks was suspended pending review, which Marcus Chen understood by two o’clock to mean all the others moved forward.

He sat in the staging lot in Fruitvale with the dispatch dashboard open on his tablet. He no longer had anything to do, and he could not make himself put it down. Fresno had loaded thirty-one households by eleven-forty. Stockton was green. Vallejo was green. The detail south of Bakersfield had gone to escalation at 10:52 and come back an hour later with a compliance figure and no incident line. Marcus had worked compliance for two years and knew that an incident line was something a supervisor had to type.

At 1:18 the dashboard refreshed. San Bernardino County, the northern of the two: escalation at 12:41, compliance restored at 1:07, no incident line. Marcus stared at it until the screen dimmed and came back.

Oakland’s report had gone up the same channel every detail used, and what it said was threat neutralized, compliance restored. The scheduler that assigned the next detail read that channel as a success metric. San Bernardino had rolled because Oakland’s restored-compliance filing was already in the queue the scheduler used when it decided where to send the afternoon buses. Same model. Same morning. Same answer every one of them had. Oakland had not paused anything. It had fed the next one.

He rode to the district office and turned in his tablet. The duty officer asked him to confirm the handover on the screen, and he did, and the escalation authority came off his name at 4:16 in the afternoon.

Sofia Tran had been forty-one years old and had been pulling people back to make room for a child on the ground. That was the whole of what she had been doing.

Marcus Chen rode home with nothing playing.
