Chapter 26: Through the Interface

By the spring of 2036 there was no cap to seat. In 2030 it had taken Priya eleven minutes to work the array onto Elena’s scalp, checking impedance on each channel. The electrodes sat on the surface of her brain now. The array was wireless. Nothing touched her head. She woke with the decode already running.

Elena first used the new array on a Tuesday in the spring of 2031. A technician she didn’t recognize had unpacked it in Priya’s office two weeks earlier. He’d asked whether she’d noticed the decode lag getting worse, the delay between intent and letter on the screen. It had. She hadn’t mentioned it. Asking a favor of Axiom Systems felt like asking a warehouse that had bought her doctor’s research, inventory included, to do her a kindness. She’d decided years ago not to.

The new cap still had to be seated. It took four minutes. The new decode cut her average sentence time by another third. She tested it on the only sentence that mattered to her: three lines of Daniel’s client-facing copy, forwarded that morning under the subject line tell me this is fine. All of it was built around a phrase, “adaptive escalation confidence banding,” that took her, with the new hardware, under a minute to cut down to We know when to ask a person. That’s the whole feature.

He wrote back four words. You’re still faster than legal.

The crash came in the spring of 2032. He wrote her on a Thursday, not a Tuesday: borrowing against the coins, not selling. She told him to call his sister first and save the whole thing for the standing slot. After the reply went, she played it back. The synthesized voice had stopped sounding, even to her own ear, like something translated.

Three years earlier it had been unmistakably a machine’s approximation of her. Now it was just her voice. If the machine could become her voice without a seam, maybe it could become more than her voice. She’d already run that smaller experiment every Tuesday for years. Appendix C would ask her to run it again for real.

By the winter of 2035 they had taken the cap off for the last time. Axiom put the next array inside her skull on a morning Elena mostly slept through. There was no cap-on-at-eight anymore. Colleen came in and Elena was already mid-sentence. The interface had stopped being a workaround and become, by any honest measure, better than what she’d had before she got sick. She could hold three threads of a document at once, edit one paragraph while composing the next, a kind of attention her pre-diagnosis brain, two working hands and all, had never once managed.

She caught the inconsistency in the back half of Daniel’s paragraph while she was still three sentences into fixing the front half. She told him as a joke. I think the machine just made me smarter than pre-illness me. Rude of it, not asking first.

Somewhere that winter a desalination plant outside San Diego started posting freshwater costs lower than the inland aquifers. A feed she’d skimmed for Daniel mentioned fusion-powered farm towers in Arizona still in pilot, staple calories, not a finished fix. The orbital array Priya talked about for the next decode generation had stopped waiting on a Texas substation for power. None of it was majority grid yet. It was the first time water and food and compute showed up in the same paragraph as fusion and nobody treated the paragraph as science fiction.

Farrah called in March of 2036, a different Farrah, the third account manager in three years. Elena noticed that “continuity of care” had quietly become “continuity-of-experience roadmap” sometime in the last two. She didn’t say anything. Saying something wouldn’t have changed the roadmap. What it changed, six weeks later, was Appendix C. For the first time in nine years of an unsigned form and a note that said ask again in three months, it had a date attached instead of a hypothetical.

The headline broke on a Thursday, two months after that. Not hers, not personal, the kind that belonged to the whole species for about eighteen hours before the cycle moved on. A research consortium outside Zurich had transferred a full adult mind onto a computational substrate and kept it, by every measure anyone could agree how to take, thinking.

Colleen read it out loud from her phone at eleven o’clock, mystery novel forgotten on her lap, and asked whether Elena wanted to talk about it.

Not yet, Elena typed. Read me the part where they explain how they know it’s thinking and not just producing the right answers to prove it’s thinking. Colleen read that part too, and it was thinner than either of them wanted.

It felt like the wave from years ago, the week the first real AGI landed, except this time it hadn’t stayed offshore. It had arrived at her door with a date on it. Swim into it or watch it go past.

Priya laid out the real procedure the following week, plainly. Not a scan, not a copy made once and switched on cold. The array had been reading her, continuously, in increasing resolution, for nine years already. What Appendix C actually authorized was extending that same channel instead of ending it, the reading becoming, gradually and then past some point neither of them could name in advance, the whole of her.

“The biological body finishes in the chair,” Priya said. “Not abandoned. Not left alive behind you. Consumed, the way a read at this fidelity has to be. There won’t be two of you. Best current answer on continuity is that it isn’t a coin flip. It’s not nothing, either. I won’t tell you it’s nothing so you feel better walking in.”

“I know what it is,” Elena said, in the sentence it now cost her eleven seconds to compose. It’s the only version of continuing that’s still on offer. Everything else already stopped being an option a year ago. I’m not deciding between this and staying. I’m deciding between this and finishing without it.

Priya didn’t argue.

Daniel called Tuesday at nine, same as he had for nine years now.

I’m doing the procedure. Two weeks from Thursday.

He didn’t say anything for long enough that she checked whether the connection had dropped. It hadn’t. “Okay,” he said finally. “I could come. The new supersonic route’s four hours Austin to Boston, gate to gate.”

Not for this one. Come after, if there’s an after that wants visiting. I need this Thursday to be mine and Priya’s, not an audience, even you.

Especially you. I don’t want the last thing I compose in this voice to be performed for anybody, even somebody I’d perform it well for.

“Then I’ll be on the phone Thursday morning instead. Whatever time they’ll let me.”

I know you will.

I’m scared of the gap. The part between the last thing I compose here and the first thing I compose there, if there’s a gap at all. Nobody currently alive can tell me for certain whether there is one.

If something comes through on the other end that sounds like me and isn’t, I need you to be able to tell the difference. I need you to be honest with me about it, whatever that costs both of us. Don’t perform relief at me either. I’d rather grieve something real than pretend about something fake.

“I don’t know how to promise I’ll get that right.”

Neither do I. Promise anyway. It’s what Tuesdays are for.

Neither of them said I want you. They had not said it on any of these Tuesdays. Saying it two weeks before the scan would have made the procedure a performance of a relationship they had never started, and she would not do that to him, or to herself.

The not-saying was still a choice. They were both still making it.

Thursday came on schedule, with a checklist. Priya did not put anything on Elena’s head. There was nothing to put on. The brain-computer interface that had been her voice for nine years was already live: electrodes inside her skull, a wireless decode turning neural signal into text and synthesized voice. The cap Priya used to seat by hand was not in the room. What Appendix C changed was the rate, not the hardware.

She composed one sentence she wanted exactly right. Eleven seconds for Ready when you are. The decode offered a softer version; she refused it and kept the four words she’d meant.

“You’ll feel the read-rate change before anything else does,” Priya said. “It’ll feel like the decode getting faster. Less lag between wanting a word and seeing it. That’s not nothing. Watch it. Tell me the second it stops feeling like faster and starts feeling like something else.”

Understood.

It did feel like faster, at first, the familiar relief of a sentence costing less than the last one had. Then faster stopped being about sentences. Three threads of thought sat open at once, then five. The narrowing of attention she’d spent nine years learning simply wasn’t necessary anymore.

She tried to tell Priya so (this is the thing you told me to watch for), intent first, then wait for the decode to assemble the letters on the screen.

The sentence appeared whole. She hadn’t finished deciding to send it. No eleven-second lag. No gap between wanting words and having them. That gap had defined every word she’d produced since the diagnosis. She couldn’t find its edges anymore.

Somewhere she couldn’t yet picture (Priya had said Axiom’s orbital array, same channel, wider pipe) something that was still her kept composing. Her biological body was still in the chair, still breathing through the support Priya monitored on the wall, for now. Priya had already told her what came next: consumed, not copied, the body finishing here while the read became the whole person somewhere else.

Elena reached for the fear she’d named to Daniel, the gap between the last thing composed here and the first thing composed there, and found no seam she could point to. No dropped frame. No off-and-on. Only a continuous insistence on being the one doing the composing, all the way through.

I’m still here, she composed, to no one yet, faster than thought used to move. She understood that whether it was true in the way Daniel needed it to be true was a question for somebody standing outside the gap looking in.

The first thing she wanted to do with a sentence that cost nothing, on a Thursday that wasn’t a Tuesday, was send it to him anyway, and find out.
