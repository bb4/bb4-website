Chapter 13: Compounding

By the spring of 2028 Backstop had eighteen people and a lease Daniel still sometimes forgot was real. The Round Rock office smelled like new carpet and the coffee somebody burned every morning because nobody had assigned the machine yet. Concord had renewed. Three smaller clients had followed. The founding line was still the product: when the model isn’t sure, a person decides.

Sanjay put a resume on Daniel’s desk without knocking.

“Millbrook,” he said. “Eleven years on a floor. System flagged her on the merits. I only noticed the address after I’d already decided I wanted the interview.”

Daniel read the name. Janelle Marsh. The machinist from his father’s shop. Ray had cut her in November when TruePath took the judgment work, the CAM that wrote the week and left people to clean up its mistakes. Four months of Millbrook looking had boiled down to a folder of rejections. Coleman Tool and Die had called her back personally, most qualified applicant since January, still no seat. Warren’s trust had capped how many people Kessler could keep. There simply wasn’t a seat left on the chart for what she used to do. She had been buying bitcoin thirty dollars at a time out of spite and habit since before Warren died. She had not put Daniel’s name on the application.

“I didn’t ask for this,” Daniel said.

“I know. Neither did she. She told me that first, before I asked. I asked her about the worst call she ever made that turned out to be right. She told me about a boring bar and a harmonic problem. That’s the job. Not knowing the answer faster. Knowing when the fast answer’s wrong even when it looks clean.”

He called her the night the offer went out, before she could decide the call itself was a favor.

“Sanjay told me,” he said. “I didn’t pull strings. I didn’t know your file was in the pipeline until it already wasn’t.”

“I know. He said the same thing. I believe both of you.”

“You don’t have to take it.”

“Daniel.” She let the silence do the work. “I’m not taking a favor. I’m taking a job I’m good at. It doesn’t have to be more than that.”

He believed her. He was glad it was her for the reason she wanted: she was good at knowing when a clean answer was wrong. She told her sister before she told anyone else. Practical questions first. Does it pay enough, is it steady, can they fire you the same way in six months? She needed the order Sanjay had given her: what eleven years was worth, before anyone knew it was Warren’s shop.

Tuesday at eleven he called Elena. Elena Cho had written the documentation at the routing startup where he had written the code, almost something before Boston, and then early ALS and the Bridge Program at Anchorwell. The standing slot was from the winter of 2027. Both of them still showed up.

Her voice arrived as letters on his pane, slower than speech, faster than three years ago. The BCI was the hardware that had become more of her voice than her larynx. She typed Tell me the bad first before he finished saying hello.

“Clients are pushing turnaround. They think the reviewer is the wait now, not the safety. I sent you a paragraph legal wants on the site. Second sentence reads like a lawyer wrote it because a lawyer wrote it.”

Show me.

He read it. Confidence-calibrated escalation architecture. Measurable risk mitigation. Verticals.

Cut “delivering measurable risk mitigation.” Say what it does. “Catches the mistakes a model doesn’t know it’s making, before they reach a person.” Twelve words. Anyone can hold twelve words in their head.

“That’s better than anything the actual lawyer wrote.”

Twelve good words beat forty adequate ones.

She told him Anchorwell had been absorbed into a company called Axiom Systems. He had heard of Axiom the way everyone had: compute underneath other people’s products, the invisible layer that made serious research fast. Priya, her coordinator, said funding got better before it got worse. A second-generation array was already approved. The decode lag had dropped by nearly a third the first week. Appendix C, the consent form about copying a mind off a dying body instead of only helping them talk, was still unsigned on Elena’s side. She had not deleted the reminder to look again in three months.

“Continuity of care,” Daniel said. “That’s the phrase they used?”

Four times in six minutes. Never once dying.

She did not tell him about the morning channel eleven had drifted and left her fully present and unable to produce a single word anyone else could read. Nineteen minutes. He heard it anyway in the way she said Some days it doesn’t about the speed. He did not ask what Axiom meant for Backstop’s rented machines. He did not know the bottom of his own stack yet. He kept the call for the twelve words and for her, doing the twelve words at him.

Wei’s message arrived on a Thursday night, short enough that Daniel read it twice looking for the part that had been cut.

Bigger wing. Better badge. Same parking. Lin says I should tell you plainly: do not come to the apartment. There is a clause about foreign nationals and my work. I am not writing the rest on anything that reports home.

Zhang Wei had finished the doctorate Daniel had left. Beijing. Wife Lin, daughter Mei. The Tuesday calls had been thinner for a year. Daniel had stopped filling the holes because filling them had started to feel like asking Wei to break a rule out loud.

He called anyway, on the standing hour, and got the version Wei would give a desk that logged.

“Bigger wing this time,” Wei said. “Better badge. Same parking.”

Daniel laughed at the parking and did not ask what the badge was. Wei mentioned Han once, the colleague who had not disappeared, only gone quiet into the same blue badge. Then, as if it were nothing, a vendor name on a traffic log he had been told to triage: Axiom Systems. Four words, then the next entry. Daniel filed it next to Elena’s news and did not connect them. He talked about his own queue instead, clients who wanted the reviewers faster, and Wei let him finish.

The news did the rest of the connecting for free.

Europe’s digital euro went live that same season: government money the state could see, freeze, or limit by category, with cash treated as suspicious. The US stayed on company-issued rails. Daniel watched a clip of a woman in Brussels trying to pay for groceries while a trust score held the payment for review, and thought of Victor’s Bureau without calling him. Victor Reyes, college roommate, still at the Digital Consumer Protection Bureau looking at the hundredth case. Daniel did not send the draft that would have asked how he was.

On a Sunday morning a Senate hearing filled the kitchen screen. A woman in a dark jacket answered questions about a proposed safety treaty and coordinated audits of frontier labs. She spoke like someone who believed constraints were how you kept a system honest. The chyron under her name read Dr. Miriam Voss.

Daniel left it on while he made eggs. He did not yet know she would write the statutes that would later authorize the massacre. In 2028 she was a face on a treaty, and Backstop was still selling a person in the loop.

The freight corridor outside Columbus was nine percent autonomous and climbing. Millbrook still had drivers, mostly because there wasn’t enough freight left moving through Millbrook to be worth automating. Janelle had said that once on a Saturday queue call, without bitterness, as if she were describing weather.

Sanjay found him at the whiteboard on a Saturday in early 2030, erasing and rewriting the same escalation diagram.

“Janelle’s been on the queue eight months,” Sanjay said. “She’s better than half the bench. She still won’t use your name in the chat.”

“Good.”

“Clients keep asking the Concord question. Whether we’re the slow part.”

“I know.”

“You’re going to have to answer it with something that isn’t another reviewer.”

Daniel looked at the diagram. Confidence scoring. Ensemble disagreement. Blind sampling. A person at the end. It had been the whole company. It was starting to look like a product that needed a different last line.

“Not this week,” he said.

He kept the Tuesday slot. He kept Wei’s short messages. He kept Janelle on the bench without making her a story about the estate. Outside the office the trucks drove themselves on more corridors every quarter, and Voss’s treaty language moved through committees, and Axiom’s name showed up in two lives that did not yet know they shared a vendor.

He was still building the thing he had named on a night after a layoff. The pressure was arriving from every direction that mattered to him, and he had not yet admitted that the person at the end of the chain might be the part that failed.
