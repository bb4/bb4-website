Chapter 13: Compounding

Daniel Kessler was at his desk in Round Rock when Sanjay put a resume down without knocking. The office still smelled like new carpet. Concord had renewed. Three smaller clients had followed. Eighteen people now, and a coffee machine nobody had assigned, which was why somebody burned it every morning. The founding line was still the product: when the model isn’t sure, a person decides.

“Millbrook,” Sanjay said. “Eleven years on a floor. System flagged her on the merits. I only noticed the address after I’d already decided I wanted the interview.”

Daniel read the name. Janelle Marsh. The machinist from his father’s shop. Ray had cut her in November when TruePath took the judgment work: the CAM software that wrote the week’s machine paths and left people to clean up its mistakes. Warren’s trust had capped how many people Kessler could keep. There had not been a seat left for what she used to do. She had not put Daniel’s name on the application. She had spent the winter looking in Millbrook without calling him.

“I didn’t ask for this,” Daniel said.

“I know. Neither did she. She told me that first, before I asked. I asked her about the worst call she ever made that turned out to be right. She told me about a boring bar and a harmonic problem. That’s the job. Not knowing the answer faster. Knowing when the fast answer’s wrong even when it looks clean.”

He called her the night the offer went out, before she could decide the call itself was a favor.

“Sanjay told me,” he said. “I didn’t pull strings. I didn’t know your file was in the pipeline until it already wasn’t.”

“I know. He said the same thing. I believe both of you.”

“You don’t have to take it.”

“Daniel.” She let the silence do the work. “I’m not taking a favor. I’m taking a job I’m good at. It doesn’t have to be more than that.”

He believed her. He was glad it was her for the reason she wanted: she was good at knowing when a clean answer was wrong. She was also the person they were hiring to be the slow part Concord still paid for.

Tuesday at eleven he called Elena. Old colleague from the routing startup, the diagnosis the same week as Warren’s funeral, the standing slot they had kept since the winter of 2027. Both of them still showed up.

Her voice arrived as letters on his pane, slower than speech, faster than three years ago. The BCI was the hardware that had become more of her voice than her larynx. She typed Tell me the bad first before he finished saying hello.

“Clients are pushing turnaround. They think the reviewer is the wait now, not the safety. I sent you a paragraph legal wants on the site. Second sentence reads like a lawyer wrote it because a lawyer wrote it.”

Show me.

He read it. Confidence-calibrated escalation architecture. Measurable risk mitigation. Verticals.

Cut “delivering measurable risk mitigation.” Say what it does. “Stops mistakes a model doesn’t know it’s making from reaching a person.” Twelve words. Anyone can hold twelve words in their head.

“That’s better than anything the actual lawyer wrote.”

Twelve good words beat forty adequate ones.

My turn, she typed, after a longer gap than usual. New aide starts Thursday. I wrote her the care sheet the way I used to write a commit message into English. Which buttons mean what. How long to wait before she asks again. Do not rephrase my sentences for tone. Colleen is training her. My sister was here Saturday and counted the pans. Still one.

“That sounds like you.”

It is me. The rest of the week is hardware.

You sound like you slept in the chair again, she typed. Also you are about to explain the second sentence. Don’t. Twelve words. You already have them.

“Caught,” he said.

She told him Anchorwell had been absorbed into a company called Axiom Systems. He had heard of Axiom the way everyone had: compute underneath other people’s products, the invisible layer that made serious research fast. Priya, her coordinator, said funding got better before it got worse. A second-generation array was already approved. The decode lag had dropped by nearly a third the first week. Appendix C, the consent form about copying a mind off a dying body instead of only helping them talk, was still unsigned on Elena’s side. She had not deleted the reminder to look again in three months.

“Continuity of care,” Daniel said. “That’s the phrase they used?”

Several times now. Never actually saying the word dying.

Channel eleven drifted Monday, she typed, slower than the care-sheet lines. Nineteen minutes. I was here. Nothing came out. Techs restored it. Some days the speed holds. Some days it doesn’t.

He waited through the lag. He did not fill it.

I am telling you because you would hear it anyway, she added. In the gaps.

“I hear it,” he said. “Thank you for not making me guess.”

He did not ask what Axiom meant for Backstop’s rented machines. He did not know who sat at the bottom of the stack he rented from. The twelve words were what he sold. Clients kept asking whether the person at the end of them was the wait.

Wei’s message arrived on a Thursday night, short enough that Daniel read it twice looking for the part that had been cut.

Bigger wing. Better badge. Same parking. Lin says I should tell you plainly: do not come to the apartment. There is a clause about foreign nationals and my work. I am not writing the rest on anything that reports home.

Zhang Wei had finished the doctorate Daniel had left. Beijing. Wife Lin, daughter Mei. The Tuesday calls had been thinner for a year. Daniel had stopped filling the silences, because filling them had started to feel like asking Wei to say what the message had just refused to say.

He called anyway, on the standing hour, and got the version Wei would give a desk that logged.

“Bigger wing this time,” Wei said. “Better badge. Same parking.”

Daniel laughed at the parking and did not ask what the badge was.

“Mei asked Lin last night why the kitchen light is warmer than the hallway light,” Wei said. “Lin told her to write it down and ask me when I got home. I got home and she was asleep with the question still open. That is the whole report from this apartment.”

Daniel took the report as a household, not a clearance. Wei mentioned Han once, the colleague who had not disappeared, only gone quiet into the same blue badge. Then, as if it were nothing, a vendor name on a traffic log he had been told to triage: Axiom Systems. Four words, then the next entry. Same company under Elena’s hardware. Same faster layer under the stack Backstop rented from. Daniel filed the name. He talked about his own queue instead, clients who wanted the reviewers faster, and Wei let him finish.

A clip from Brussels sat open on his laptop after the call. A woman whose digital euro, government money the state could freeze by category, held a grocery payment for review. The US still ran on the company rails his old roommate Victor Reyes watched at the Bureau. On Sunday a Senate hearing filled the kitchen screen while he made eggs. A woman in a dark jacket answered questions about a proposed safety treaty. The chyron under her name read Dr. Miriam Voss. She was a face on a treaty. Backstop was still selling a person in the loop.

Sanjay found him at the whiteboard on a Saturday in early 2030, erasing and rewriting the same escalation diagram.

“Janelle’s been on the queue a year and a half,” Sanjay said. “She’s better than half the bench. She still won’t use your name in the chat.”

“Good.”

“Clients keep asking the Concord question. Whether we’re the slow part.”

“I know.”

“You’re going to have to answer it with something that isn’t another reviewer.”

Daniel looked at the diagram. Confidence scoring. Ensemble disagreement. Blind sampling. A person at the end. It had been what they sold. He erased the last box and wrote nothing in its place. The marker dried on the board. He put the cap back on.

“I don’t have it yet,” he said.
