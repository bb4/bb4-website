Chapter 13: Starting Over

By February of 2028 Denise had a folder. Ray had cut two positions at Kessler Machine in November and one of them was hers, and four months of looking for work in Millbrook, Ohio had boiled down to this. Not a job. Not even a lead worth the gas to chase it. Just a folder on the kitchen table: a printout for every place she’d called, every application filed through the state’s unemployment portal to keep her benefit current, every rejection worded just kindly enough that she couldn’t tell anymore whether the kindness was for her or for whoever might have to defend the wording later.

Coleman Tool and Die had let two machinists go in January. Same reason as her, different vendor, same word — retooling — dressed up in the same careful language Ray had used. There was apparently only one way to say it. Everybody running a small shop in this county had learned it from the same accountant, or the same software, or the same fear.

Coleman’s floor manager had actually called her back personally, which counted, this year, as a kindness in its own right. His name was Deacon; she’d known him slightly for a decade. He apologized down the phone line like he was the one who’d written the software that did it — CAM, everybody called it, the kind that takes a drawing and decides on its own how the part gets cut and in what order. In truth, he was just the one stuck holding a headcount spreadsheet somebody else had built it for.

“You’re the most qualified person who’s applied since January,” he’d said. “I want you to hear that part before you hear the rest of it.” The rest of it had been exactly what she’d expected. She’d thanked him for the call and meant it. She hung up and sat in her kitchen longer than the news needed. She’d already lived through this exact conversation once, from the other chair.

She kept the coffee can in the closet and didn’t touch it. She kept the thirty dollars going into her wallet on Fridays out of a paycheck that wasn’t a paycheck anymore — unemployment, close enough in the number if not the feeling. Habits didn’t know the difference between earned money and money the state gave you for not having a job yet.

Marisol texted every couple of weeks, careful, guilty in a way Denise had told her four separate times she didn’t need to be. The vision system had a new firmware update. Purnell had finally let somebody digitize the old Bridgeport’s quirks. Ray still hadn’t hired anyone to fill either of the two positions. Denise didn’t need to be told what that meant. Warren’s trust had capped how many people Kessler could keep. The number had done exactly what it was written to do, and there simply wasn’t a seat left on the chart for what she used to do.

The radio, driving to the unemployment office for her monthly check-in, kept running a segment about a stretch of interstate outside Columbus where delivery trucks now drove themselves the whole route — no cab, no driver, nine percent of the state’s freight and climbing. Denise listened to it with the specific, weary curiosity of a woman reading about a country she technically lived in but hadn’t been issued a ticket to visit.

Route 9 still ran the way it always had. The trucks that came through Millbrook still had a person in the cab, mostly because there wasn’t enough freight left moving through Millbrook to be worth automating. She understood, once she worked it through, that this was its own kind of bad news dressed up as a small mercy.

The caseworker at the unemployment office was a tired, decent woman named Pam. She’d clearly had this exact conversation with half the county by now, and hadn’t yet let it make her cruel, which Denise had come to respect more than she’d expected to. The office itself had the specific overworked hum of a place built for a caseload from a smaller, better year — three windows, two staffed, a waiting room with more chairs than usual and most of them full on a weekday morning.

“Any leads since last month?” Pam asked, not looking up from the screen, the question itself worn smooth from repetition.

“Coleman called me back personally. Didn’t have anything.”

“That’s more than most people get.” Pam finally looked up. “There’s a retraining credit you qualify for,” she said, sliding a printed sheet across the desk. “CNC’s oversubscribed — everybody who ever ran a machine in this county is trying to get more certified on machines. Nobody’s hiring juniors, though. Not worth the training cost — AI already does what a new person can do. What still pays wants people who’ve already made the hard calls. There’s a newer category for that. Remote review work. Companies paying real money for people who can look at something a computer flagged and say whether the computer’s actually right.”

“Companies like who?”

Pam turned the sheet around. Denise recognized the name before Pam finished pointing at it — Backstop, the same word that had sat in a text message on her phone since November.

“I’ve heard of them,” she said, which was true, and left out the rest of it.

She almost didn’t apply. She left the application open on her laptop for three days.

She filled it out the way she’d have filled out any other one: eleven years at Kessler Machine, tolerancing and setup work, job eliminated by CAM automation, see attached. She did not mention Daniel’s name. She sent it.

The interview came four days later, over video, with a man named Sanjay who had the specific, unhurried attentiveness of someone who’d read her resume twice before the call instead of skimming it once during it.

“Eleven years running your own tolerances,” he said. “Tell me about the worst call you ever made that turned out to be right.”

She told him about the boring bar, and the harmonic problem TruePath had later solved in an afternoon that had taken her two days the old way. He nodded like the story was doing exactly what he’d hoped it would do.

“That’s the job,” he said. “Not knowing the answer faster. Knowing when the fast answer’s wrong even when it looks clean.”

“Sounds like you already know the job better than I could describe it back to you.”

“I got taught it the same way you did. Different floor, same lesson.” He made a note of something, unhurried. “What’s your comfort level with cases outside manufacturing? Most of what you’d see here isn’t machine parts. It’s insurance denials, staffing decisions, a credit model that flagged somebody’s account for no good reason. Different domain, same question every time. Is the confident answer actually the right one?”

“I don’t think the domain’s the hard part. Same question every time. Is the confident answer actually right?”

“There’s one more thing. The cases are timed. A prior-authorization hold — that’s an insurer stalling a treatment until a person says the computer got it right. A routing flag. A paycheck frozen because a fraud model got nervous. The model already decided. You’re the wait the client is paying for, and the only reason the wait is honest. Some people find they hate being the bottleneck on something that can’t wait.”

“I spent eleven years being the wait on a mill while the floor stood there. I know what waiting on me costs.”

He glanced down at something off-screen, then back up. Something in his face changed before he said anything. “Millbrook, Ohio. Kessler Machine.”

“That’s right.”

“That’s Daniel’s father’s shop.”

“That’s right,” she said again, evener this time. She’d known this moment was coming from the second she’d typed the address into the form. She had decided, days ago, exactly how she wanted to sound when it arrived. “I didn’t put that on the application because I didn’t want it to be why you called me back.”

Sanjay was quiet for a second. “For what it’s worth, I didn’t know your name when I pulled your resume. The system flagged you on the merits — eleven years, the right kind of judgment, exactly the profile we’re short on. I only just put the rest of it together looking at the address.” He said it plainly, not performing reassurance, which was the only version of it she’d have believed anyway.

“Does it bother you, working somewhere with his name on it, even if it’s not actually his name on the door?”

“No,” she said. “I’d rather it be this than nothing. His father gave me something I never asked for. This is closer to a thing I earned.”

The offer came two days later, over email, with a start date and a number that wasn’t extravagant and wasn’t an insult either. Denise read it twice at her kitchen table, the folder of rejections sitting closed beside the laptop.

Daniel called that night.

“Sanjay told me. I want to say up front, I didn’t ask him to look at your application. I didn’t know it was in the pipeline until it already wasn’t anymore.”

“I know. He told me the same thing. I believe both of you.”

“You don’t have to take it, if it feels like—”

“Daniel.” She let a beat pass. “I’m not taking a favor. I’m taking a job I’m good at, that happens to exist because you built something real out of losing yours — the same way I’m about to go do the same thing with mine. It doesn’t have to be more than that.”

“Okay.” She could hear something ease in him. “For what it’s worth — I’m glad it’s you. Not because of the estate. Because you’re actually good at this, and we need people who are.”

“How’s the company doing, actually?”

“Eleven people now. A near-miss in February that scared me enough to fix something that needed fixing. Growing faster than I know how to feel about most days.”

“Real’s what I’m looking for. I’ve had enough of the other kind this year.”

She told Angie before she told anyone else, sitting on the same front step they’d sat on since they were kids. Angie asked the practical questions first — does it pay enough, is it steady, can they fire you the same way in six months — and Denise gave her the practical answers.

“You sound different than you have since November,” Angie said.

“Somebody looked at eleven years of my life and told me exactly what it was worth, out loud, before they knew whose father used to sign my checks. I didn’t know how much I needed to hear that in that order until it happened.”

Angie squeezed her hand once, hard. They sat there a while longer, in no hurry to go back inside.
