Chapter 10: Public

Seven months in, Backstop had four employees, one lawyer paid for four hours a month, and enough cash to keep operating for a limited number of months. Daniel tracked that deadline in a spreadsheet he was superstitious about opening on weekends. The review queue had processed just over ten thousand cases without making the news for the reason he most feared: a wrong call that hurt a real person.

Sanjay called at 11:40 that Wednesday night. “I need a second set of eyes now. Case 47-Bravo. Solstice Health. I’m ninety percent sure the model’s about to deny a prior authorization it shouldn’t, and I want somebody else looking at this before I overrule medical software at midnight on my own signature.”

Daniel had the case open before Sanjay finished. A prior authorization was the insurer’s required yes before a treatment could start. Solstice was one of Backstop’s first real clients. The model in question decided, several thousand times a day, whether a requested treatment matched the diagnosis code closely enough to approve without a human ever seeing the file.

This one had flagged a resubmission from a fifty-six-year-old woman. Her second round of a chemotherapy drug had been coded slightly differently than her first, a billing quirk, not a medical one. The model’s confidence score on “diagnosis mismatch, likely fraud pattern” had come in just over the line that used to mean automatic denial before Backstop’s contract put a person in the loop.

“Walk me through why you think it’s wrong.”

“Because I’ve seen four of these exact patterns in six weeks. New billing software, same drug, same doctor, coded different enough to spook a model that’s never been told billing systems change vendors.”

Daniel looked at it for four minutes, found the same pattern, and typed his name into the second-reviewer field. The denial never went out. A woman in a state neither of them would ever visit stayed on schedule for a treatment neither of them would hear about again.

“That’s four this month with the same mistake,” Sanjay said. “We should tell them the model needs to learn what happens when a billing company changes its software.”

“Write it up. I’ll send it tomorrow.”

He’d hired Sanjay in the third month, after four unanswered emails and one that finally got a reply. Daniel had written I know what happened to you in the spring, and I think what you’re good at is exactly what I need directly in the subject line. Sanjay had written back nine minutes later: I’ve had three job interviews since April where nobody once asked what I actually know how to do. Tell me more.

The other two were part-time. Beth was the Meridian contractor who had found out she was fired from an email, in the same spring round that took Sanjay off an adjacent team. Odalys was a former claims auditor Sanjay had found through a forum for displaced expertise. Four people, three time zones, a review queue that never fully slept.

The news about an AI teaching itself to do research had done something to Backstop’s inbox that no amount of sales email had managed: it made “a human is checking this” sound like something clients would pay extra for, not an expense to cut.

Signups tripled in a week. A regional credit union. A staffing platform. A hospital system twice the size of Solstice.

He posted the Solstice case himself, under his own name: We caught this because a person looked at it. The model was 91% confident it was fraud. It was a billing software migration. Somebody’s chemotherapy almost didn’t get approved over a formatting difference. When the model isn’t sure, a person has to decide. That’s the whole company.

By Thursday morning the post had spread beyond Daniel’s control. A reporter from a trade publication asked for twenty minutes. Daniel gave her twenty-five.

“Isn’t this just nostalgia with a business model?” she asked. “Humans checking the computer’s work. Does the human review actually make the system safer?”

“Ask Solstice’s patient. She doesn’t care whether it’s comforting. She cares whether her chemo got approved.”

“But isn’t this basically what a federal fraud desk already does? Somebody flags a weird transaction, a human looks at it. That’s decades old.”

“It is. And it only exists because Congress specifically told banks and benefit programs they had to build it. Nobody told Solstice’s authorization model it needed one. Nobody told the hiring platform signing up this morning. There’s no mandate for any of that. So it just doesn’t happen until either a regulator gets around to it in twenty years or somebody sells it as a service today.”

“Here’s my actual question. The model’s the one deciding what counts as uncertain. What stops it from just not flagging something?”

“Nothing, if that’s the only layer. Which is why it isn’t. We run a second model against the same decision that wasn’t trained the same way, and disagreement gets a look. We pull a fixed percentage at random, no flag required. And a third model spot-checks what our human reviewers decided.”

“That sounds like a lot of machines checking machines before a person ever gets involved.”

“It is. I’m not going to tell you it’s airtight. What I can tell you is why it’s still a person at the end and not a fifth model. A smarter model reviewing a smart model doesn’t solve anything. It gives the same problem to another machine. A person signs their name to the final decision.”

Victor saw Daniel’s name in his feed Friday morning. Daniel Kessler, his old college roommate. The quote about chemotherapy sitting above his drafts folder like an accusation. He’d started sorry about your dad the week of the funeral and deleted it. Now he typed glad you’re building something. should’ve reached out sooner, read it back, and deleted it again. He closed drafts with nothing in it, same as always.

The name turned up a second time that week, and not in his feed. The Bureau’s monthly summary of payment-rail activity carried a footnote listing companies that were now moving enough money to show up in a report. Backstop was three lines from the bottom. No action indicated at this volume. Victor read his friend’s company name in a Bureau document, agreed that no action was indicated, and went on to the next section.

By Friday, a company called Clearline put out its own post announcing a “confidence-calibrated escalation layer.” They had four times Backstop’s headcount. The language was close enough to Daniel’s own that three different people sent it to him.

What was different, as far as he could tell, was that Clearline’s escalation layer routed to a more expensive model when confidence dropped. A smarter machine standing behind the first one. It was not the same thing Backstop did. Daniel wrote nothing about them. He wrote instead a second post about the vendor-migration blind spot, a problem other companies could go check for themselves this week. A genuinely useful thing beat a clever rebuttal.

The letter from the payments processor arrived Friday afternoon. Backstop was now moving enough money that it had to file extra reports under rules Daniel didn’t recognize. The deadline was six weeks away. He forwarded the letter to the accountant with probably nothing, flag if it’s not in the subject line. He did not think about it again before the weekend.

Janelle texted that afternoon. saw your thing online, Ray showed me, didn’t understand half of it but the chemo part got me. He wrote back thank you, that means more than the reporter’s questions did.

Mara called Saturday morning.

“You led with the model. You should have led with the woman.”

“I don’t have the woman. I have a patient ID and a diagnosis code.”

“Then you needed a better anonymized version of her.” A pause. “It’s good, Daniel. I read it twice before I found anything to criticize, which from me is basically a standing ovation.”

“Take the Clearline thing seriously. Money like that doesn’t chase an idea unless the idea’s already proven it’s worth chasing. You’re going to need real capital in the next year. When you get there, call me first.”

“I don’t want to make this weird between us.”

“It’s already weird. We’re both spending Dad’s money on the same bet from two different directions and pretending the connection doesn’t matter.” She hung up.

He sat on his balcony that evening watching the traffic on 35. Seven months ago this had been a sentence at the top of a blank document. Tonight it was four people, a real client’s real patient, a competitor with real money, and a sister who’d just told him she was paying attention.

He thought about the house in Millbrook, still technically for sale, still holding a laptop in a locked drawer nobody had gone back for. He’d traded one postponement for another. The laptop for the company. And he was, for tonight at least, at peace with which one he’d chosen to finish first.
