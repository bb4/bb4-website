Chapter 14: The House

Brazil voted overnight, in 2029, to add bitcoin to its sovereign reserves outright. Not a seizure pile left in a drawer. A G20 economy buying the asset on purpose, with a line item a finance minister would have to defend. The price stepped fourteen percent before Daniel was out of bed.

His sister Mara called from Chicago before nine. Halcyon Capital, the inheritance she had levered while he held his. Two years into reading the Fort Knox revaluation bills nobody else thought would ever get a vote.

“Washington’s still sitting on the gold-revalue version,” she said. “Revalue Fort Knox, buy bitcoin with the difference, balance-sheet neutral. When that moves, that’s the actual headline. Brazil is the warm-up.”

“You sound like you already did something about it.”

“I did it before the open.” She moved on before he could ask what. “The house sold. Closing in three weeks. Somebody has to clear Dad’s office. Saturday. I can fly. You’re closer.”

“I’ll be there.”

“While I have you: runway?”

“Twenty people. The problem isn’t hiring. Clients want money sitting behind a bad call. A guarantee. Not another reviewer. That’s a different kind of capital than a round.”

She heard insurance. He could tell from the pause. She took it as him talking around an equity ask he wasn’t ready to make.

“You’ll get a fair term sheet like everybody else,” she said. “Not a favor.”

“I’m not asking for a favor. I’m asking you to pick up the phone when I call. Saturday. Bring something you don’t mind getting dusty.”

Her car drove itself from the airport. Daniel met her at the house. The FOR SALE sign was already down, replaced by SOLD in red faded to pink. She had the car take the long way past Kessler first. He did not ask why. Four fewer cars in the lot than at the funeral. Ray’s truck in the same spot.

The office hadn’t been touched since the will reading. The desk. The drawer. The laptop, exactly where Carol had said it would be.

Above the desk, pinned where it had been pinned since before either of them could read, a strip of paper gone the color of weak tea. To err is human, but to really foul things up you need a computer. Warren had brought it home off the shop office wall in some year nobody remembered. Daniel took it down without being asked and put it in his shirt pocket.

“We could still call someone.”

“We’re already here.” He sat in their father’s chair and pulled the laptop toward him. “Password’s the actual problem.”

Mara found it going through the shelf: a spiral-bound tolerance logbook, decades old. Taped inside the front cover, in pencil, a string of characters that meant nothing on its own.

“Of course it’s in a logbook.”

It worked on the first try.

There wasn’t much on the drive. Tax documents, scanned invoices. Then, filed under a name that gave away nothing, a text document clearly added to for years: dated entries, terse.

Some of it was the crypto correspondence they had half expected. A friend named Russ, arguing about wallets and forks across a decade.

Then Daniel went quiet over an entry about Ray. The day only one man in the shop can run it is the day the shop is one bad Tuesday from closing. Doesn’t matter how good the one man is.

Mara was three entries further down, at one with Janelle’s name in it.

March. Had a letter of intent on the desk. Buyer out of Columbus, cash, enough to walk away clean while the divorce was still eating everything. Thought the shop was already gone. Same feeling as 2008, only this time I was the one selling it. D. found the real numbers. What we still cleared. What five more years looked like if I didn’t panic. Put them in front of me and never once said don’t sell. Never told Ray. Never told the kids. Tore the letter up that night. Put a note in the trust file. Whatever else happens to this place, she gets taken care of.

Daniel read it slowly. “She stopped him selling the shop.”

“And he never told her he knew.”

Near the bottom, an entry from 2018, the year bitcoin had lost most of its value.

Talked to Russ tonight about pulling all of it out. Told him I would. Didn’t. Don’t know if that’s discipline or just not having anywhere else I’d rather put it. Going to bed.

Then, shorter: Still here. Glad, mostly.

“He almost sold the coins too.”

“Everybody almost sold that year.”

“He wrote down why he didn’t,” Daniel said. “While it was still going the wrong way. Everybody else only knows why afterward.”

There was one more file. Locked. No name that meant anything. It asked for a second password.

They tried the string from the logbook. It failed. Daniel tried their mother’s name. Mara tried the shop’s street. Nothing.

“Dad’s letter said we’d figure it out,” Daniel said. “The line Carol read. This is probably what he meant.”

“Then we didn’t.”

“I’m taking this.” He put the laptop in his bag.

She closed the lid on the empty desk. They had read as much of a dead man as either of them could stand, and they still had a lock left.

“We should tell her,” Daniel said, in the car. “Before you fly. She’s in Millbrook.”

Mara had not wanted another stop. She wanted the airport and a number. She said, “If we fly out without telling her, we’re doing what he did.”

Janelle’s kitchen was small, clean, a headset on the table from the Saturday queue. She was on Backstop’s remote bench now, the Millbrook hire who had refused to use Daniel’s name, and she looked at them both and then at the laptop under Daniel’s arm and understood the visit before he had said why they were there.

“We opened Dad’s laptop,” Daniel said. “There’s an entry about you. From the divorce. You found the real numbers on the shop, put them in front of him, and never said don’t sell. He tore up a letter of intent from a buyer in Columbus. He wrote that he knew. He never told you he knew.”

Janelle stood with her hand on the chair back.

“I knew which favor he meant,” she said. “I didn’t know he wrote it down.”

“He put a note in the trust file the same night. That’s why you were in the will.”

“I didn’t ask him to do that.”

“We know,” Mara said. It came out shorter than she meant. “We’re not here to make you explain it.”

Janelle nodded once. She did not sit. “All right. Thank you for telling me.”

Daniel shifted the laptop. “There’s another file we couldn’t open. Second password. If he ever gave you something that would…”

“If he locked it, it wasn’t for today,” Janelle said. She looked at the machine and did not reach for it. “I’m not guessing at his locks.”

They didn’t stay long after that. On the step Mara looked at the street the way a person looks at a life she is trying not to fall back into.

They didn’t talk much on the ride to the airport. The car’s dashboard surfaced a segment about a commercial reactor outside Oak Ridge that had crossed net energy gain, the same containment approach a research system had sketched out two years ago in nine unsupervised days.

“You’re doing the math thing,” Mara said. “The one where you go quiet.”

“I’m thinking about how many good headlines we’re actually going to get out of this, against how many of the other kind.”

“That’s called being an adult, Daniel.”

At her gate she called her desk. “I want to move another twenty percent into the position before Monday.”

A pause long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped. Daniel heard enough of the other side to know the person on the line was telling her this morning had been the market handing her a reason, and this sounded like her handing herself one.

“Does it matter which, if the number’s the same either way?” she said.

She sat for a while in a plastic airport chair after she hung up. If the bet went the other way, she ended up back in Millbrook. Not visiting. Living there.

She flew home. Daniel drove back to Austin with the laptop in the passenger seat and his father’s scrap in his pocket, and the locked file still locked.
