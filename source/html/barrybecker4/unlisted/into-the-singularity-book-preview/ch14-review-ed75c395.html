Chapter 14: The Position, Working

The reserve announcement broke at 6 a.m. Chicago time. Brazil had voted, overnight, to add bitcoin to its sovereign reserves outright.

Small, dollarized economies had been doing versions of this for most of a decade. The US had its own strategic reserve built out of DOJ seizures nobody in Washington had bothered selling. China kept something similar and never talked about it. None of that was really a reserve in the sense the term meant on a central bank’s own books. It was a drawer nobody had decided what to do with.

Brazil wasn’t inheriting anything from a seizure. Brazil was going to buy the thing outright, on purpose, with a line item a finance minister would have to defend in front of an actual legislature, the way anyone defended a real reserve asset.

That was what moved the market: not a country holding bitcoin, but a G20 economy deciding to count it the way it counted gold. The price stepped, a clean jump of fourteen percent.

There was still a bill sitting in committee in 2029 that wanted to revalue the gold in Fort Knox and spend the difference on more bitcoin outright, balance-sheet neutral, no new debt issued. Nobody thought it moved this year. Mara thought that whenever it did move, that would be the actual headline. Washington moving would be the reserve currency itself admitting the same thing.

Her desk, the research AI Halcyon issued every book, had already drafted three position notes before she’d finished her tea. She skimmed the first, corrected a number from a stale feed. It was getting better than she was at the first pass.

She texted Devon.

Saw it, he wrote back. Give it an hour before you do anything you’ll want a good excuse for later.

She gave it the hour. At 7:04 she called him properly. By 7:20 she’d turned two years of grinding appreciation into something that finally looked like the number she’d been telling people about at dinner parties before any of them had wanted to hear it.

She didn’t add new debt so much as let the loan restructure itself around what the collateral was now worth. Same forty percent, against a position that had roughly doubled. Same ratio. Bigger number underneath it.

“You’re aware you just added more actual dollars of exposure this morning than your father’s entire estate was worth two years ago.”

“I’m aware. I did the arithmetic before I called you.”

Daniel called that afternoon.

“The house sold. Closing’s in three weeks. Which means somebody has to actually go clear Dad’s office before then.”

“Saturday. I can fly in. You’re two hours closer.”

“While I have you, how’s the runway?”

“Twenty people now. The problem isn’t hiring. Clients want money sitting behind a bad call, a guarantee, not another reviewer. That’s a different kind of capital than a round.”

She heard insurance, not collateral. She took it as her brother talking around an equity ask he wasn’t ready to make.

“You’ll get a fair term sheet like everybody else, not a favor.”

“I’m not asking for a favor. I’m asking you to pick up the phone when I call. Saturday. Bring something you don’t mind getting dusty.”

Her car drove itself the whole way from the airport. She let it take the long way past Kessler Machine before telling it where she actually wanted to go. The lot had four fewer cars in it than she remembered from the funeral. Ray’s truck was still in the same spot.

The house looked smaller than she remembered. The FOR SALE sign was already down, replaced by SOLD in red faded to pink.

The office hadn’t been touched since the will reading. The desk. The drawer. The laptop, exactly where Carol Weiss had said it would be.

Above the desk, pinned where it had been pinned since before either of them could read, a strip of paper gone the color of weak tea. To err is human, but to really foul things up you need a computer. Warren had brought it home off the shop office wall in some year nobody remembered, and had never known who said it first. A newspaperman, he’d guessed once. Or an almanac.

Daniel took it down without being asked and put it in his shirt pocket.

“We could still call someone.”

“We’re already here.” Daniel sat down in their father’s chair and pulled the laptop toward him. “Password’s the actual problem.”

It was Mara, going through the shelf, who found it. A spiral-bound tolerance logbook, decades old. Taped inside the front cover, in pencil: a string of characters that meant nothing on its own and everything once she held it up.

“Of course it’s in a logbook.”

It worked on the first try.

There wasn’t much on the drive. Tax documents, scanned invoices. And then, filed under a name that gave away nothing, a text document clearly added to for years: dated entries, terse.

Some of it was the crypto correspondence they’d half expected. A friend named Russ, arguing about wallets and forks across a decade.

Then Daniel went quiet over an entry about Ray. The day only one man in the shop can run it is the day the shop is one bad Tuesday from closing. Doesn’t matter how good the one man is.

Mara was three entries further down, at one with Janelle’s name in it.

March. Had a letter of intent on the desk. Buyer out of Columbus, cash, enough to walk away clean while the divorce was still eating everything. Thought the shop was already gone. Same feeling as 2008, only this time I was the one selling it. D. found the real numbers. What we still cleared. What five more years looked like if I didn’t panic. Put them in front of me and never once said don’t sell. Never told Ray. Never told the kids. Tore the letter up that night. Put a note in the trust file. Whatever else happens to this place, she gets taken care of.

Mara read it twice. The sentence at the will reading, one favor she is under no obligation to remember, had never been coy. It had been literal. Janelle had genuinely, probably, mostly forgotten it.

“There’s one about Janelle,” she said.

Daniel read it slowly. “She stopped him selling the shop.”

“And he never told her he knew.”

Near the bottom, an entry from 2018, the year bitcoin had lost most of its value.

Talked to Russ tonight about pulling all of it out. Told him I would. Didn’t. Don’t know if that’s discipline or just not having anywhere else I’d rather put it. Going to bed.

Then, shorter: Still here. Glad, mostly.

“He almost sold the coins too.”

“Everybody almost sold that year.”

She closed the laptop. They had read as much of a dead man as either of them could stand.

They didn’t talk much on the ride back toward the airport. The car’s dashboard surfaced a segment about a commercial reactor outside Oak Ridge that had crossed net energy gain, the same containment approach a research system had sketched out two years ago in nine unsupervised days.

“You’re doing the math thing,” Mara said. “The one where you go quiet.”

“I’m thinking about how many good headlines we’re actually going to get out of this, against how many of the other kind.”

“That’s called being an adult, Daniel.”

At her gate, she called Devon. “I want to move another twenty percent into the position before Monday.”

A pause long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

“That’s not the same conversation as this morning. This morning was the market handing you a reason. This sounds like you handing yourself one.”

“Does it matter which, if the number’s the same either way?”

“It always matters which. It just doesn’t matter to the collateral.” Keys clicked. “I’ll send it tonight.”

She sat for a while in a plastic airport chair. If this bet went the other way, she ended up back in Millbrook. Not visiting. Living there.

She flew home. By the time the plane’s wheels touched down, Devon’s confirmation email was already sitting in her inbox.
