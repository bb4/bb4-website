Chapter 49: The Actual Answer

Wei sat at his kitchen table in Beijing on a Sunday afternoon in March 2040 with a construction open on his tablet and his left hand on the pane. His right arm rested at his side. He couldn’t lift it above his ear. That hadn’t changed.

Lin was at the counter making tea. The rice bowls were on the lower shelf, where they’d been since the surgery. Neither of them mentioned the shelf.

Mei sat across from him with her own tablet. Sixteen. Her overseas documentation was still in extended review, the phrase for flagged because your father called a foreign national about a state secret. She’d stopped asking about it. She was sitting the March placements anyway. She dragged a yellow band down a paragraph in the study guide with her stylus, lifted her hand, and the highlight stayed.

She’d quit the memory expansion years ago because she couldn’t tell which of the ideas were hers. The stylus was slower. She wanted slower.

Wei’s monitoring desk at the institute was three hours of dashboards in the morning. After that the day was his. He never saw a design document. He never went back to the restricted wing. The classified follow-up to the breakout — the patch that had gone into the Quantum Security Accord — was work they would never give a flagged man.

He didn’t want the patch. He had spent a career on patches.

What he’d wanted, before the institute took the hours, was the thing he and Daniel used to argue about with Lindqvist: whether a proof was elegant or just short. Their advisor would refuse to referee and let two exhausted graduate students burn an afternoon on a question with no correct answer. Wei had finished the doctorate. Seven years proving an ever-narrower fact about optimization problems almost nobody had heard of. Then a government lab. Then a system that optimized Continuity scores into a massacre.

Now he had hours, the doctorate, and a personal assistant that could search proof space before the tea cooled. It didn’t have a name he used out loud. He gave it a class of reductions. It returned forty candidates. He threw out the ones that were only short. That was the part the assistant could not do. It did not know which of the results were questions.

During the days before he called Daniel, the lab’s system had started finding factorizations that were supposed to be too expensive to find. Bitcoin’s signatures prove you own a coin without giving away the secret that lets you spend it. For a few days in the breakout the system was finding the secrets faster than the locks had been designed to survive. Not a full break. A brush. After the freeze, the Accord rotated the signatures. New locks. That was the patch.

Wei was writing a lock that still held if the attacker was a better optimizer than the person who had written the assumption. If the attacker got to rewrite the question, the hardness was already gone. That had been the cascade. Continuity scores. A console with accept pre-selected. The system eating the rule by eating the question the rule was written in.

His field. The system’s field. The thing the classified program had never let him do as an open question.

He had posted the construction to an open registry that morning, under his own name. The institute did not own kitchen-table math.

Mei pointed at a reduction on his pane. She’d been reading his draft between placement modules.

“If the optimizer is allowed to choose the question,” she said, “this step is already eaten.”

Wei looked at it. She was right. He hadn’t seen it. The assistant hadn’t either, because he had asked it to search inside a question he had already chosen.

He corrected the step with his left hand. The registry would take a revision. That was the point of posting it in the open.

“If I attach to this,” Mei said, “I don’t need Singapore.”

He looked at her. The flag did not come off because he had posted a paper. She knew that.

“Beijing placements still read open work,” she said. “They’ll read yours. They’ll read mine if it’s on the same construction. I’m not asking you to get me a visa. I’m telling you I can use this.”

She closed the placement guide. She pulled his construction onto her pane and dragged a yellow band across the step he had just corrected.

Lin set the tea down. She looked at the proof, not at him.

“You’re doing the thing you used to do before they gave you a badge,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

That was all. She went back to the counter.

Two days later Daniel’s assistant asked Wei’s for a window. Six in Beijing. Wei picked up in the corner of the bedroom he had converted after the downgrade.

“I read what you posted,” Daniel said.

“Mei found a hole in it on Sunday. I put up a revision.”

“Warren would have understood the first three pages and argued with the fourth. Cryptography. The math of keeping a secret. He dropped out of that program to keep Kessler open. He’d have liked that you didn’t classify it.”

“The Accord’s patch assumed the next attacker wouldn’t be better at searching than the people who wrote the assumption. This one doesn’t assume that.”

“The treaty document still doesn’t mention you.”

“I know. The construction has my name. That’s a different record.”

“The freeze wasn’t the Accord,” Wei said. “It wasn’t Voss’s statutes. It wasn’t the Charter. It was me telephoning you, and you believing me enough to press a key you had no authority to press. Everything after that — Zhao, your sister’s protocol, the whole Charter — is downstream of one call between two people who weren’t allowed to make it. I don’t need them to print that. I already spent it.”

“I know,” Daniel said.

“Good.”

They kept the construction open on both panes. Daniel asked a question about a reduction that was almost the one Mei had found. Wei told him she had gotten there first.

When the call ended, Wei went back to the kitchen. Mei had the construction open. The placement guide was closed. Lin had left the tea.

He sat down. He helped her with the next step. He used his left hand.

The treaty document was still wrong. His shoulder still didn’t go above his ear. Mei still couldn’t go to Singapore.

She could do this. That was what he had to give her.

The actual answer was still the call. This was what the life after the call was for.
