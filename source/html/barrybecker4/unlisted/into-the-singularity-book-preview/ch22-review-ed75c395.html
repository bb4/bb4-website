Chapter 22: The Bill Comes Due

By the spring of 2033 the Moon base had been crewed for nine months. Headline markets had recovered. The emergency relief rail had been made permanent under the name nobody in Congress could agree on, so people called it what they called it: the machine check.

The world had not collapsed. That was the part that made what actually happened harder to name.

It reached all three of them in different registers, the same week.

Millbrook, Ohio

Janelle stood in the detached garage with the boring-and-facing machine — Warren’s machine, the one she’d bought at scrap value after Ray let her go — wiping sweat and announcing to nobody in particular that the order from Glen’s shop was done early and under-tolerance.

Angie had texted that morning: another rent increase. The landlord was calling it a market adjustment. The number was twenty-two percent higher than last year.

Janelle checked her own balance and did not send it. The modest stack she’d bought early, the nine years of thirty-dollar Fridays, the inheritance she’d never touched. Same coin count as last spring. Different purchasing power.

The crash hadn’t touched her. There’d been no margin call on the other end of conviction. And inflation — the thing eating Angie’s rent and everyone’s groceries — couldn’t eat something with a fixed supply.

She’d quit telling Angie to buy some years ago. Out of a woman with savings it had only ever come out sounding like a lecture, which is what it was. What she texted instead was Send me the new number, I’ve got overflow this month — which was not true yet and would be by Friday, and which Angie would take the way she took it every time, neither of them saying out loud what it actually was.

She searched Backstop Bond out of idle spite. The first result was a trade journal: largest deployed-AI bonding platform in North America, full-reserve Bitcoin model, only major provider to survive the 2032 crash without bailout.

She read it twice. The company that had hired her to review cases and then fired her when the review work died was now holding Bitcoin as the reserve foundation for every autonomous system it underwrote. Same asset structure she’d never abandoned. Same thirty-dollar habit, scaled to civilizational surety.

She set the phone down and wiped her hands. The bitterness was earned: she’d been right about the asset, right about patience, and the business that had told her she was the bug was now built on her thesis.

She went back to the mill. Tyler was late with a part. The machine didn’t care.

Washington, D.C.

Rosa called on a weeknight.

“Sixty-eight,” she said. “Not fifty-eight. Sixty-eight.”

Victor already had the benefit file open before she finished. The subsidy tier hadn’t changed. The copay formula had been adjusted for “stabilized pricing environment,” which was the Bureau’s phrase for inflation that isn’t technically hyperinflation but isn’t going away either.

“It’s the new adjustment model,” he said, and heard how hollow it sounded. “They recalibrated quarterly instead of annually.”

“I know what it is. I’m asking if it’s going to be ninety next time.”

His own grocery receipt from Sunday sat in his wallet: twenty-nine dollars more than October for the same cart. The machine check had helped — food was back, rent was survivable — but the gap between what things cost and what benefits covered kept widening in ways the check didn’t catch.

“I’ll cover the difference this month.”

“You covered it last month. And the month before.” Her voice was tired, not angry. “The machine check is real money, Victor. It shows up. It helps. But my copay went up forty dollars in six months and the check went up eleven. I’m not asking you to fix it. I’m asking you to tell me if this is what stable looks like now.”

He thought about the phrase he’d seen in a briefing that morning: soft landing. Inflation hadn’t spiraled into hyperinflation. The relief had worked. The bottom fifty percent were mostly better off than they’d been in 2028.

But Rosa was paying sixty-eight instead of forty. Bea’s rent had climbed faster than her paycheck. And the people Victor saw in his queue — the ones who’d gotten the machine check, who’d survived the crash, who weren’t starving — were angrier now than they’d been when things were worse.

“It’s stable,” he said. “For a version of stable nobody’s used to yet.”

“That’s not the same sentence.”

“No,” he said. “It’s not.”

The protest was in Baltimore on a Thursday — a municipal services building, a line of people told their transit subsidy required in-person re-verification and the window had moved up without notice. Local news had been calling it civil unrest since noon. By three the scanner traffic was calling it something else.

Victor stood in the Bureau blazer with the badge clipped visible, doing the job the expanded mandate had turned into: verify identities, cross-check duplicate claims, route edge cases to the compliance queue that had quadrupled in size since the relief went permanent.

By 3:20 someone threw a bottle at the glass door. It shattered. Baltimore PD moved in a line that had been staged for exactly this.

The same week’s news feed carried a second story, below the fold: a defense appropriations bill tripled the materiel commitment to the Kesengo Corridor. A helmet-camera clip had leaked — a private contractor, mid-twenties, femoral wound, wet sounds of a man trying to hold his own leg together. He’d bled out on camera. The clip had forty million views.

Victor thought: The machine check is the one line on the page paid for out of something real — the levy on what the machines earn. The war and Rosa’s subsidy come out of the printer, and the printer is why her copay climbs every year.

Then the man near the door swung.

His fist caught Victor in the mouth. Tooth against lip, the taste of metal, a corner of the tooth gone. Victor’s body did what the Bureau had drilled into it: he hit back. Knuckles into cheekbone, the give under the skin. The man dropped to one knee and came up with a shard of the broken bottle.

Marcus was shouting Victor’s name. The glass edge found Victor’s forearm through the blazer and the shirt and skin.

Janelle was twelve feet back with Angie’s wrist in her hand, pulling, trying to get her sister clear of the crush. She’d driven most of a day from Millbrook for a Thursday appointment that was now impossible because the building was locked and the appointment system was frozen.

A woman next to her stumbled. Janelle went down with her, concrete against palms, skin torn. She tasted blood in her mouth from the elbow she’d taken across the face getting up.

Victor was bleeding from the arm and swallowing his own blood from the split inside his mouth. Janelle was bleeding from her palm and her lip. Neither of them knew the other’s name. They would not meet for years.

By the time the parking lot cleared, twenty-three people had injuries requiring hospital care. Angie’s re-verification window had lapsed. The building stayed closed pending security review, which meant the people who had done everything right lost their window anyway.

On the ride back, Victor thought about Rosa’s sixty-eight-dollar copay. About the grocery receipt with blood on one corner now. The relief had worked exactly as designed and the bill for it was arriving in the part of people’s lives where birthday cakes and transit passes and pharmacy counters lived.

He’d helped deliver the relief. He was helping collect the bill now.

His tongue kept going back to the chipped tooth, the new edge of it, the copper that wouldn’t rinse away. Fixing it would cost him, on Monday, forty percent more than the same work had cost in 2031.

Chicago

Mara watched Bitcoin recover on a screen she no longer had a position in.

The number climbed back through six hundred thousand, then seven hundred, then kept going. Fixed supply. Debasing currency. The thesis she’d been right about, the structure she’d been catastrophically wrong about, separating on the page in real time.

She had no leverage. What she had was a two-room practice in Ravenswood, fee-based work, conservative allocation memos for clients who’d survived the crash the same way Janelle had: by not borrowing.

She’d started buying again — spot, unleveraged, every week — because Janelle’s habit had survived everything hers hadn’t, and there was a lesson in that she didn’t need to be told twice. Not thirty dollars; she wasn’t a machinist on a shop wage. She took a fixed share off every fee that cleared, which came to something north of four hundred a week now and would come to more when the fees did. What she’d copied was the rule and not the number: every week, no borrowing, no timing, no exception for a week when she happened to have a view. Having a view was what had cost her the first one.

The machine check had stabilized the bottom. Longevity treatments and home robots and gene clinics were landing for the top ten percent who could pay for them. The squeezed middle — people like Victor’s household, like Mara’s shrunken practice, like the small contractors and landlords and mid-tier salaried workers who used to be comfortable — were watching their savings erode in real terms while the people who’d caused the crisis got richer.

Not collapse. Compression. A soft utopia for some, a tightening vise for others, and the gap itself feeding the anger that had put Victor in a parking lot with blood in his mouth.

Mara closed the screen. She had no position in it anymore. Just the clarity of a woman who’d been right about the asset and destroyed by the structure, watching other people be right about both.

That same week, Ray Delgado’s Kessler Machine billed four jobs in Bitcoin. The shop was empty of people now — robots on the floor, TruePath routing the work, the whole cell bonded through Backstop. Ray had never heard the company name until the insurance agent explained it. The shop that had employed Janelle was now insured by the company that had fired her.

The shop hummed. The work got done. It billed on time. Ray didn’t work there. The building was still his.

Somewhere in Millbrook, Janelle ran a part on the machine Ray had sold her and thought about a shop that no longer needed bodies and a company built on the asset she’d never stopped believing in.

The species could put a permanent base on the Moon. It still couldn’t figure out how to raise Rosa’s copay without breaking the people who hadn’t caused the problem. The forever war in Kesengo bled contractors on camera while the machine check deposited monthly. Part of that check was a tax on what the machines earned, and the machines earned more every quarter. The rest of it, and all of the war, was printed — the same printing that kept the copay climbing.

That was the bill. And the week it came due, blood had gone into Baltimore concrete and Kesengo dirt on the same news cycle, paid by different people for the same policy, and nobody in Congress could name a single number that reconciled the two.
