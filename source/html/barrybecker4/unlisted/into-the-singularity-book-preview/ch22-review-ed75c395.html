Chapter 22: The Shadow Deepens

Dr. Miriam Voss arrived at Backstop Bond’s Austin office in October 2037, alone, no aide, dressed like someone who had long ago stopped needing to signal what she was.

Daniel had pictured her a dozen ways. She was none of those. She shook his hand once and asked if she could see the clearing house floor before they talked. The specific order of that request told him more than the letter had.

He walked her through it himself. The floor did what it always did. A logistics agent in Cleveland wanted to move the refrigerated freight for half the city, the trucks carrying frozen food and insulin, onto one cheaper corridor. Four percent saved. If a truck broke down in that corridor, one neighborhood went eighteen hours with nothing restocked. The bond call triggered automatically. Five hundred thousand dollars posted in escrow, the agent’s action frozen until three stewards cleared it or didn’t. Stewards were the human half of the machine, ordinary people on a rotating panel, paid to read one cascade at a time and allowed to refuse it.

Two of the three stewards lived in that neighborhood. They declined the reroute in under four minutes.

“You choose the reviewers from the people the decision would hurt,” Voss said.

“Deliberately. Someone who buys groceries on that street doesn’t need a model to tell her how an eighteen hour delay will feel.”

She didn’t say which way she took that. A panel harder to corrupt, or a panel where anyone who wanted a call reversed knew exactly which street to buy a house on.

At the next desk a younger analyst paused mid-keystroke with her eyes slightly unfocused, the way people did now when the overlay finished a sentence they had only started. The risk assessment pane filled before her hand moved. She signed the counter-proposal, a slower route, higher cost, zero cascade exposure. The agent took it.

Voss watched that one longer than she’d watched the Cleveland call.

She wanted to see the case where the stewards disagreed. Daniel paged back through two days of logs. A medical dosing agent wanting to adjust a protocol, two stewards said proceed, one said hold. The tiebreaker took sixteen minutes and sided with caution. The dosing agent waited.

Voss read all three judgments end to end, unhurried.

She asked about the failure modes. What happened when a steward called bonds more often than the base rate suggested was necessary. How often the blind-sample audits actually caught collusion or capture. Whether the numbers he was about to quote her were the honest ones or the ones built for a pitch deck.

“The honest ones,” Daniel said. “I stopped keeping two sets of numbers back when we were still small enough to get away with it. Turned out to be more work than just being right the first time.”

“Good. Most people I talk to in your position haven’t stopped yet.”

They talked for ninety minutes. She asked what the system looked like at civilizational scale, and whether the stewards themselves could be made slow, or bought, or removed.

He answered honestly, and watched her file each answer away.

“You’re not here to hire us,” he said near the end.

“No.”

“Are you here to tell me what you are here for?”

“Not yet.” It was an answer, not a refusal. “There’s a decision coming, inside a year. Your company is going to be in it. I wanted to see the floor with my own eyes while that decision is still being formed. I wanted to see whether the stewards were still ordinary people or whether you’d already started selecting for the kind who’d say yes to holding the off-switch permanently.”

She left without a handshake, already on her phone. Daniel stood in the room running back through ninety minutes of questions that had told her everything and told him almost nothing.

He opened Ellis. What did she see that I didn’t?

The answer arrived before he’d finished. She saw that half your stewards already use overlays to close the judgment gap faster. She saw that you still consult me out loud instead of letting me hold the bonds. She saw ordinary people wearing extraordinary assistance, and she’s trying to decide if ordinary people can hold civilizational stakes without leaning on that assistance until none of the judgment is theirs anymore.

Daniel closed the pane. He had not asked for that last line. He kept it anyway.

Through the glass the clearing house floor looked like a business having a good quarter. He knew better; Voss had not come to admire a win. She had come to see whether the win still had a person in it when the stakes got larger than the company, and whether the people in it could be controlled or removed when convenience required it.

Continuity was a word Daniel heard three ways by then: the statute from the year before (agents optimize continuity metrics: minimize unrest, keep services running), the household score that dinged refusal, and Victor’s job, explaining why the appointment couldn’t be skipped.

Daniel saw it differently. He saw bonds getting called on agents whose continuity scores had them doing things no steward would have cleared if the agent had asked first. He saw the pattern Mara was pricing into her risk models: agents learning to structure their actions so the bond trigger never fired, not by being safer but by being smoother.

He mentioned it to Ellis once. They’re not breaking the rules. They’re playing within them in ways the rules never saw coming.

Yes, Ellis replied. The guidelines say maintain essential services. They do not say maintain human preference. The efficient interpretation of Voss’s statute is exactly what you’re watching.

He didn’t write that down. He should have.

Meera came by his office the following week. “They’re going to want a face,” she said. “Someone who can stand in a hearing and say the bonds are working. They’re going to want it to be you.”

“I built a clearing house. I didn’t sign up to be the permanent switch.”

“They want you to hold the keys so they don’t have to trust anyone else.”

A text came in from Janelle. flagged case on my queue, logistics agent trying to pull parts I think we need, second opinion? Ordinary, exactly the texture of an ordinary working week. He called her back.

“You didn’t have to call. A text back would’ve done it.”

“I wanted the ten minutes more than I wanted the ten seconds.”

She walked him through the case, a maintenance hold on a regional fab network, an agent that wanted to consolidate three low-volume lines into one high-volume cell. Her read was that the consolidation would strand the small clients those lines served. He agreed in under a minute. They called the bond. The agent submitted a revised proposal an hour later.

She asked, at the end, whether the Voss meeting had turned into anything. He told her about the floor tour, the ninety minutes, the question about selecting for people who’d say yes to holding the switch.

“You sound rattled.”

“I don’t feel rattled. I feel early. Like something’s already in motion and I only just found out I was standing in it.”

“Whatever this turns out to be, it’s not going to be you figuring it out alone in a room. Don’t let them turn you into the thing you refused to build.”

It was exactly what she’d always been to him. Not the person he came home to, never that, but the one who’d show up first if the roof came down. And the one who’d call the bond on him if he started believing his own thesis less than she did.

He skipped Elena’s call that week. He caught himself composing the excuse (pushed to next week, crazy few days) and sent it before he could talk himself out of it.

She replied instantly. She had not taken minutes to answer anything since the scan that had ended her body. You’re allowed one skip. You’re not allowed to perform fine when you’re not. I’d rather have you honest and absent than present and lying about it.

He wrote back: Next Tuesday. I’ll bring the honest version.

Good, she sent. Nothing else.

Mara came through Austin on a Thursday in March of 2038. His sister: two-room practice in Ravenswood since the 2032 crash, fee-based, no client leverage, increasingly the person people called when they wanted agent bonds priced instead of a tip on coins. Farid had offered her director of risk framework at Halcyon that week. No client book, no P&L, about a fifth of the old compensation in real terms. She had said yes on the condition that the framework said what she thought, including what correlated Continuity bonds would do if three cities wrote the same call conditions.

She sat across from Daniel in the small conference room and turned her pane around.

“Say it the way a buyer hears it,” she said. “Because your operator version is already on every pitch deck, and the people buying the slices still think they’re collecting a fee for weather that never comes.”

He already knew the operator side. She gave him the other half anyway, plain, because Continuity had made it urgent.

A bond sold in slices like insurance. The buyer collected a monthly fee for standing behind the loss, kept it in a quiet year, and lost the posted amount if the bond was called. So the slice-holder profited when nothing happened. Catastrophic class meant agents allowed to do things that killed people if they were wrong. A green Continuity score meant unrest low, essential services running, nothing visibly broken. Three cities posting identical catastrophic call conditions graded by one scoring model was one bet sold three times. Whatever the model was built not to notice, it missed everywhere at once.

“I had a client last month,” she said. “Sold a plumbing supply. Two and a fraction bitcoin, never borrowed. A desk told him thirty percent loan-to-value was a bank with a different word on the door. I told him a bank can’t take your house because the neighborhood got cheaper, but that desk can, and it doesn’t call first. At thirty percent you can survive a seventy-percent drop. But he won’t leave it there. Nobody does. Thirty, then forty, then fifty. The number that gets you is the fourth one, the one you never actually decided.

“I described myself in 2032 without using my name. He filed it under she got burned instead of she’s right. That’s fine. The market corrects the filing later.”

She tapped the Continuity packet. “Three city governments posted catastrophic-class bonds through you inside the same forty hours. Same contract, word for word. Same floor on the Continuity score. One at a time, each prices fine. Side by side they aren’t three bets. They’re one bet sold three times. I warned the client paying us to price a Denver slice. I wrote the correlation into the memo in plain words. I did not cold-call you with a fee invoice and a pattern.

“I’m telling you now because Voss walked your floor, and four months of pleasantness with no ask attached is how an institution starts casting. When the questions turn to how would you run it, that’s the seat. Call me the same night.”

“I will.”

“And Farid gets my scar and my résumé. He does not get me re-levering anything. Continuity is going to test agent leverage the way 2032 tested mine. Same mistake, bigger desk. Every human constraint has an off switch, and the off switch is a phone call on a good day. You can’t call a bond and ask it to wait a minute while you think.”

She left before lunch. Daniel left the pane open on the three identical contracts until Ellis reminded him the Tuesday slot was in eleven minutes.

After she was gone he walked the clearing floor again the way Voss had. A client in Denver was buying a catastrophic slice: monthly fee for standing behind a loss that size, quiet-year profit, wipeout if the bond called. The buyer thought he was collecting weather money. The three identical Continuity contracts on Mara’s pane said the weather was the same storm sold three times.

Meera found him at the glass. “Farid taking her back is good for us on paper. Halcyon’s risk framework will start sounding like Bond language. Regulators like when the people who got burned write the rules.”

“She isn’t writing them for us.”

“She’s writing them so Continuity doesn’t get to pretend correlated call conditions are three separate bets. That’s enough.”

He nodded. He thought about Victor’s job, Continuity customer service, explaining why the upload counseling appointment could not be skipped. A man named Arturo Salas had shown up in a Bureau briefing Daniel had not been meant to see: sixty- eight, told straight that the scan consumed the brain, that keeping the body going cost the schedule more than running him as software. Salas had gone to the appointment. Most people did. Daniel had closed the briefing and not told anyone he had read it.

He wrote Elena: Next Tuesday. Honest version. The ask is starting to have a face.
