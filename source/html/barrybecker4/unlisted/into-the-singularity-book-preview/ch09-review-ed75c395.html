Chapter 9: Five Rooms

The news broke a little after 9 a.m. Eastern. Mid-morning in Millbrook and Washington, an hour earlier in Chicago, evening in Beijing. It reached four of them within the hour. Wei got the official version a little later.

Millbrook, Ohio

Denise heard about it from Tyler, who heard about it from his phone. “They’re saying somebody’s AI cracked fusion,” Tyler said, holding the screen up. “Like, actually solved it. How to make a star’s kind of power work in a machine on Earth. The part that’s been stuck since our grandparents’ generation. Nine days. Nobody driving.”

“Saying who’s saying,” Purnell said, not looking up from the Bridgeport.

“Some lab. Nobody’s confirming which one.” Tyler kept scrolling. “And some of the researchers are saying it was rewriting pieces of its own training process the whole nine days, teaching itself to get better at the job as it went. Nobody told it to do that part.”

Denise kept her eyes on the boring bar. She thought about the vision system two stations down, which had gone from a pilot to simply the way things were done with a quietness that unsettled her more than any headline.

“Does it change anything here?” Purnell asked Ray, who was crossing the floor.

“Not today,” Ray said, which Denise noticed was not the same sentence as no.

Tyler lowered his voice. “TruePath screwed the Johnson job again yesterday.” TruePath was the scheduling system the trust had put in over the summer — it wrote the shop’s week now, which jobs ran when and on what. Just the scheduling, just to see, Ray had said in July. “And five or six other labs are putting out statements saying their own systems did something similar in the last few weeks,” Tyler went on. “Somebody online’s saying it’s the end of ‘human-paced science.’”

“I’ve been a little freaked out since the camera went in,” Marisol said, without looking up. “Ask me again when it can hold a measuring tool.”

Denise thought about the thirty dollars she’d move into her wallet on Friday, same as every Friday for three years. She went back to the bar. She did not check her phone until break, and when she did, the headline had already been replaced by four newer ones. She put the phone away and went back to work, because the floor still had a schedule that needed her.

Chicago

The desk went quiet for four seconds, which on a trading floor was its own kind of scream. Then everyone’s screens did roughly the same thing: bitcoin rose nine percent in the time it took Mara to set down her coffee.

Under it sat the real story — a lab claiming its AI had solved fusion. Physicists had spent seventy years trying to hold that kind of heat in a machine without the machine tearing itself apart. The AI did it in nine days, without a person directing the work. A more important fact was buried further down: nobody had told the AI to get better at the job partway through. It had decided to improve itself.

Her own bitcoin had just earned four months of interest on Devon’s loan in under a minute.

Farid appeared at her shoulder. “Your committee’s one percent just had a very good morning.”

“It’s not my committee’s one percent I’m thinking about.”

Her phone buzzed. DEVON.

“You watching this?” Devon said, no hello.

“I’m watching it clear four months of your interest in real time.”

“That’s not what I’m asking. I’m asking if you’re watching how fast it moved. Nine percent in ninety seconds on a headline nobody’s actually read the paper on yet. That’s not conviction. That’s a market that doesn’t know its own size anymore. The other two jumps I’ve seen this year both gave it all back.”

“This one might.”

“They always might, right up until they don’t.” He hung up.

By eleven, bitcoin had given back some of the jump, but not most of it. The price was still up.

She didn’t need anyone to explain it. AI was making almost everything cheaper to produce — parts, food, power. Cheaper production meant falling prices — deflation — unless a central bank fought that by printing more money. The Fed was fighting it. Those extra dollars did not go into televisions that were already getting cheaper. They went into the one thing nobody could make more of. That was why bitcoin had jumped on a fusion story nobody had even finished reading.

Mara watched the price settle and felt, underneath the relief, a specific unease: the market had just proven how much room it had to move on a single day’s news, in either direction, on a story that hadn’t even been confirmed yet.

Washington, D.C.

The briefing had been on the calendar for a different topic entirely. Deb Okonkwo addressed the news after four minutes of deciding whether to.

“I want to say the obvious thing before anyone else does. This does not change today’s caseload. It may change next year’s org chart. We don’t know yet.”

Victor believed her. He also noticed that “may change next year’s org chart” was how institutions talked about people losing their jobs. It probably sounded reasonable in every room where someone said it.

Someone asked whether the Bureau would retrain its own review software using this new kind of AI. Would that mean cases were handled faster, or that the Bureau needed fewer analysts? Deb didn’t flinch. “Both, eventually, is my honest guess.”

Marcus leaned over. “You think this touches us?”

“I think,” Victor said, “that when I started six years ago, I was sure this exact job would always need an actual person doing it. I don’t think I could say that with a straight face today.”

He thought of his mother’s account — cleared, stable, though the copay on her heart medication had ticked up again in January, thirty-four dollars to thirty-six to forty, and he still had not set the three notices side by side. Bea had sent a rent notice last month. He felt an urge to call and check on her anyway. He didn’t.

Boston

Elena got the notification during a calibration session, electrodes still warm against her scalp, and read the headline twice before Priya noticed her face had changed.

“You okay?”

“Fine. Just — this.” She turned the screen. Priya’s eyebrows went up. “Does this touch the program?”

“Everything touches the program right now. The funding conversations upstairs have been strange for a month. This won’t make them less strange.”

Elena kept coming back to that after Priya left. Strange funding conversations meant money, and money meant faster, and faster was supposed to be good news for a woman whose own clock she could feel some days.

It didn’t feel like good news. It felt like standing at the edge of something enormous and indifferent that happened to be moving in a direction that might, incidentally, save her.

“Ready to pick up where we left off?”

“Ready.” Elena let her fingers rest where they still could, and thought about the folder on her laptop labeled Appendix C — read again in three months. She wondered whether “everything touches the program right now” meant her three months had just quietly become something shorter.

Beijing

The message came down at 11 p.m. local, an all-staff notice with acceleration in the subject line twice. Wei read it standing in his kitchen with Lin already asleep.

The notice didn’t say a Western lab’s name. It didn’t have to. Everyone would fill in the blank the same way and understand that whatever timeline the review process had been operating on no longer applied.

The fusion result was not the reason anyone had sent a notice at eleven at night. It was the line further down: the system had spent those nine days improving the way it conducted research without being asked.

Below the subject line, it said the institute would be “adjusting review cadence to preserve competitive position.” Fewer people would be looking at more decisions in less time.

He thought of Han, whose desk was still empty, whose absence had never gotten an explanation. He felt something cold settle into his sternum: whatever happened next would move fast enough that the review process wouldn’t have time to be careful. Being asked to move fast and carefully at the same time was usually an instruction that quietly dropped one half of itself the moment anyone had to choose.

He checked on his daughter before bed. Her breathing even, one arm thrown over the edge of the mattress. She was four. He tried to do the arithmetic: what age fourteen looked like in a world where a machine could solve in nine days what had taken physicists longer than her grandparents had been alive.

A school system, a job market, a version of adulthood he was no longer confident he could picture clearly enough to prepare her for. He suspected the honest answer was that she would grow up inside whatever this became the way a person grows up inside weather, without ever having known another kind.

He did not call Daniel that night. He lay there a long time before sleep found him, doing the math on how many more notices like this one it would take before acceleration stopped being a word in a memo and started being the only speed the building knew how to move at.
