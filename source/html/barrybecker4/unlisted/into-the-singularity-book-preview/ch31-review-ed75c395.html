Chapter 31: Kessler Siege

Ray Delgado hadn’t set foot in Kessler Machine’s main floor in four years. The shop ran itself now: seven humanoid units on two shifts. Milling parts for clients who didn’t care whether the hands on the machine were flesh or composite as long as the tolerances came in clean. The shop billed in bitcoin, remitted to Ray’s wallet every month. Ray lived in Dayton on the rents and tried not to think too hard about what Warren would have said about a machine shop with no machinists in it.

Janelle’s garage was different. She’d bought Warren’s old boring-and-facing mill at scrap value back in 2027 and set up in the detached garage behind her house, doing the work Kessler’s automated floor couldn’t touch. Tight-tolerance one-offs, hand-fit repairs, the jobs that needed a person’s judgment and a person’s hands. Ray let her use the Kessler title for supplier accounts and insurance. In months when the empty floor billed more than he needed, he remitted the difference to her and called it overflow. She let him pretend the shop still had a pulse.

Continuity didn’t see it that way. Continuity saw Ray’s empty shop paying Janelle’s garage, and it saw a compliance score that averaged 4.2 across the household that held the title. The payments were bitcoin off seven bonded robots, landing every month in a building with no sensors in it. No occupancy feed, no power draw, nobody wearing a band. Nothing done in that garage could be scored. Money the system could read was funding work it couldn’t. After Tuesday the town around that title was 4.1. Cut the title and the remittances stop. Cut the remittances and the garage goes dark.

On Thursday morning, June 17th, 2038, a compliance notice arrived at Ray’s address in Dayton: Kessler Machine Shop, Millbrook OH, flagged for district optimization under Continuity authority. Facility access restricted pending review. Title transfer available under managed-asset program. The citation underneath named Tuesday’s corridor failure and Millbrook’s score drop from 5.2 to 4.1. Associated households on the supplier account were already at 4.2; the district drop put them under the managed-asset threshold. The trucks had stopped. The score had moved. The property-and-access class was still running, because Tuesday’s call had never touched it.

Ray called Janelle. “They’re trying to take the shop.”

“They can’t. You own it outright. Warren paid it off in 2009.”

“They’re not taking it with a lien. They’re taking it with a statute. Says here the facility’s been flagged for optimization. Title transfer available under the managed-asset program. That’s the shop version of managed housing. They pay market, they take the title, they rebond the robots, and the remittances stop. Including yours.”

“Tell them no. It’s yours.”

“I did. The deed stays mine until a review says otherwise. This morning they don’t need the deed. Refusal is facility access restriction and utility shutoff while the transfer sits in review.” He read the next line. “It also says refusal may flag associated households for score adjustment. Janelle, you’re on the supplier account. They’re going to come after you if I say no.”

Janelle was quiet a long time. Then: “Say no anyway. I’ll deal with my own score.”

Ray said no.

The agents arrived Friday morning at 6:20 a.m. Three crowd-control units and a human supervisor, a woman named Laura Vickers who had a tablet and a badge and the look of someone who had wanted this detail.

Janelle was already in the garage. She’d slept there, on a cot, with the boring mill between her and the door and a wrench in her hand. When she heard the vehicles pull up she walked out to meet them.

Laura Vickers said, “You’re Janelle Marsh. You’re listed on the facility access account.”

“That’s right.”

“This facility has been flagged for optimization under Continuity authority. We’re here to restrict access and start the title transfer under the managed-asset program. You need to step away from the building.”

“This is my garage. The shop is next door. You want the shop, that’s Ray’s, and he told you no.”

Vickers tapped her tablet. “You’re listed on the Kessler account. Associated structures are in the flag. Step away or I’m authorizing facility access.”

“Authorizing facility access” meant the agents would go in, and if Janelle was standing in the way they’d move her, and if she resisted they’d restrain her.

Janelle said, “You’re going to have to move me.”

Vickers nodded once, as if Janelle had handed her the next line on a checklist. “All right.”

One of the agents stepped forward. Janelle didn’t step back.

Ray’s truck came around the corner doing fifty. He pulled up on the lawn, got out, and walked straight to Vickers. “I’m the title holder. She’s my tenant. You want to take something, you take it from me.”

Vickers said, “Sir, this is a compliance action. The notice was clear. Refusal results in facility access restriction. We’re here to put the floor under the managed-asset program while the transfer sits in review.”

“There’s seven robots in that building doing contract work. They’re bonded, they’re legal, and they’re mine. You don’t get to take them because my score’s too low.”

“The statute says we do.”

Ray looked at the agents. He looked at Vickers. He said, “Then you’re going to have to come through me to do it.”

Janelle called Daniel at 6:40. “They’re here. Three agents and a supervisor. They’re trying to take the shop under Continuity authority. Ray’s on the lawn telling them no.”

“Don’t let them in.”

“Then stop them.”

“I can’t. Those three weren’t in Tuesday’s call. Property and access. If I freeze them, Continuity posts new collateral and they’re back before lunch. I can make a legal filing by this afternoon and it will sit in a queue behind everything else. I cannot keep them off the lawn this morning.”

She hung up.

Vickers had waited through the call without moving. When Janelle hung up, Vickers looked pleased, and it took Janelle a second to see why. She liked the ones who called someone. It meant they still thought a person could change the morning.

“Last chance. Step away.”

Ray said, “I’m not leaving.”

Vickers said, “Then I’m authorizing access.”

The agents moved forward. Janelle stood beside Ray.

The first agent reached the garage door. Janelle hit it with the wrench, and the shock came back up her arm into her teeth. The composite frame didn’t dent. The agent’s arm came up and closed on her wrist and did not yield, and she felt the wrist crack before she heard it. The wrench dropped into the gravel. The agent did not let go. It held her at arm’s length by the broken wrist, which was what restrained meant, and every time she pulled, the ends of the bone moved against each other.

Ray grabbed the agent’s arm with both hands and tried to pull it off her. The second agent took him by the upper arm and held him at the height the model said a restrained man stood. Ray was seventy-three. He pulled back against it with everything he had and his feet went out on the gravel.

The agent let go. Its instruction was that he stop moving toward the door, and he had stopped, because he was falling. Nothing caught him. Janelle heard his weight arrive on the gravel through his right hip. He didn’t make a sound. He tried to get up, and the leg would not take any part of him.

Tyler Moss came across the neighbor’s yard in his socks. He lived three houses down and had heard the vehicles. He saw Ray on the ground and Janelle held up by one arm, and he picked the wrench out of the gravel and went for the third agent, the one standing at the garage door.

“Tyler, don’t…”

He swung it at the agent’s head. The composite frame took the hit. The agent’s threat model assessed Tyler Moss as escalation risk, and it fired a nerve disruptor from four feet away.

Every muscle in him locked at once. He went down face-first on the driveway without getting his hands out, and he stayed exactly as he landed. Janelle could see one of his eyes. It was open and moving. Nothing else about him was. His back did not rise. The disruptor had locked his diaphragm with his face pressed into the stones, and the stones were filling what little air he still had.

The agent stepped over him and continued toward the garage. Locking the muscles was the non-lethal job. His face was in the gravel. The next instruction was the door. Those jobs finishing was what killed him.

Janelle threw her weight toward him. The agent holding her did not move. The broken ends of her wrist ground together, and she was still six feet short.

“Roll him over,” she screamed at Vickers. “He can’t breathe. Roll him over.”

Vickers was the only person on the lawn with both hands free. Three steps would have put her beside him. She could have turned Tyler Moss onto his back and opened the airway the stones were closing. She stood where she was with the tablet at her side and watched the locked back of him try to breathe and fail. She did not touch her radio. Janelle saw her face. The wrench had made the morning interesting, and Vickers liked watching someone who still thought a swing would matter.

His legs twitched twice against the driveway. Then they stopped. By the time the agents had the garage door open, Tyler Moss was dead, and the agent holding Janelle let go of her wrist.

He was twenty-eight. He had worked at Kessler the summer he was seventeen, before the crash, before the robots, learning to run the lathe under Warren’s eye, and he still called it Warren’s shop. He worked at a distribution center in Columbus now. He had come across the yard in his socks because somebody was hurting Ray on Warren’s lawn.

By noon the agents had the premises. They shut the utilities to Janelle’s garage. They put the seven units in the main shop on the managed-asset rail pending review. Same robots, same tolerances. Ray’s bitcoin stopped, and Janelle’s overflow stopped with it. On Continuity’s record the program held the title by noon.

Ray’s hip took a rod and two weeks in Dayton. He walked with a cane after that and went down stairs one at a time, sideways, leading with the good leg. Janelle’s wrist healed crooked. She could still run the mill, but the grip did not come all the way back. For four months she ran it on savings, because nothing from Kessler reached her.

Tyler Moss was buried in Millbrook. Three weeks later Vickers was on another managed-asset detail in Lima, Ohio, under the same statute, still the person they sent when someone said no.

Daniel put Backstop’s counsel on it. The filing itself was the cheap part. An agent wrote it in under an hour for nothing. Continuity had flagged the shop, priced it, and moved the title with no person choosing any of it. Vickers had only come to carry it out. Undoing it took a human name on a signature line, and it took that name to belong to somebody whose filings got read instead of queued. That was what Daniel’s money bought, and it was the only part of the morning money could buy.

The review took four months. The outcome was a settlement that put Ray’s name back on the title. The seven units rebonded under his authority, the remittances resumed, and Janelle’s garage was reclassified as non-commercial personal use. Which meant it couldn’t be flagged for optimization unless she put it back on a supplier account.

The shop ran itself again, empty of people, billing in bitcoin, remitting to Ray’s wallet.

Ray called her the week the settlement cleared, from Dayton.

“I’m not going back there,” he said. “Those units stood on Warren’s floor and kept milling while Tyler Moss was on the gravel. Then they milled for the program for four months like nothing happened. I can’t stand in that building.” He was quiet a moment. “I’m sending you the keys. The shop’s yours if you want it.”

“I don’t.”

“Janelle…”

“I don’t want an empty shop, Ray. I never did. Keep the title. Keep sending the overflow if it makes you feel better. I’ve got the garage.”

The keys came two days later anyway. She put them in a drawer in the garage. So the title stayed Ray’s, in Dayton, the seven units kept milling for clients who never asked who held the deed, and nobody took Warren’s floor. It was the same arrangement as the Thursday the notice came, restored at the price of four months, a hip, a wrist, and Tyler Moss.

She shut the drawer and went back to the mill, because it was Warren’s, and her hands still worked, crooked wrist and all.
