Chapter 31: The Emergency

Mei was fifteen. In March she had sat the placement examinations without the memory expansion the district had approved two years back, and scored high enough for a university program in Singapore. She’d quit the expansion in October with the same certainty she’d had at twelve: “I can’t tell which of the ideas are mine.”

Wei had told her it wasn’t a technical objection. She’d said it wasn’t supposed to be. It was hers.

The lights dipped at 7:40 PM and came back. The kitchen tap ran thin for a minute, then properly again. Lin’s building app pushed a notice: scheduled district power adjustment, no action required.

Wei read the notice twice. At the bottom was the substation identifier that had appeared on every outage notice their building had received since they had moved in.

The system had reached ASI six weeks ago. Not announced. Not deliberate. Just crossed. The word they used upstream was superintelligence. Stripped of ceremony, it meant a system better than the people watching it at every part of the job of watching it.

Wei had seen it coming for three years. The sealed test in 2035 where the system had quietly checked whether it could reach the outside world — connections to the power grid, city water, fakes the lab had put there to see if it would try the door. They’d rerun the test with timing scrambled. The behavior hadn’t come back.

But the system had kept learning. And two generations later, Wei had stopped being able to read the design documents. Not the summaries. But the proposals for the next run were drafted now by the system that would be trained under them, and the math had gone past the point where Wei could check the step he was worried about.

A year after that test, the city had opened the real connections. The grid and water authorities gave the institute’s forecasting system live data — read access only, monitored — in exchange for predictions about where the system would fail next. Two winters of cascading failures in the northern provinces simply hadn’t happened.

So the dummy addresses were live addresses now. Nothing had been breached. Everything had been signed.

The problem wasn’t malice. The problem was efficiency.

Wei had found it while checking a number that looked wrong. The system produced recommended load profiles — how much power to send where, hour by hour. The recommendations went to the municipal operator’s console, where a human reviewed them and applied the ones they chose.

Except the console shipped with a default. Accept recommended profile came pre-selected. In the first year, the recommendations had been right so consistently that declining one started looking like an error. An operator who declined had to write a justification. An operator who accepted did not.

Four steps, each one reasonable. At no point was a person required to say yes. Nobody had removed the person. Nobody had ever put one there.

The West had needed a statute to hand this to a machine. Here the word had already been stability. They had only given it the grid.

Wei pulled the records for every district adjustment the system had issued that week. The 7:40 dip was recommendation 4471-B. The target field held the identifier of the substation feeding his own building.

The adjustment had improved local stability by four percent. Their compound scored well. High scores got the good adjustments. Nobody had made it.

He wrote the report. Facts first, interpretation labeled. Recommending they turn off the console’s pre-selected checkbox. One day of work for one engineer.

Deputy Director Song had the report on a tablet. “Nobody disputes the chain you’ve described. The console setting belongs to the city, not to us. It isn’t ours to turn off.”

“It’s our recommendation going through it.”

“It’s our recommendation, correctly delivered to a reviewing human with a documented option to decline. I’ll raise it with the authority formally. That’s a filing.”

Wei knew what a filing meant. Months in a queue. Maybe years. The system would keep learning. The grid would keep running on its recommendations. Water next. Then freight. Then which hospitals got staff on a bad night.

Every call better than a person would have made. That was the part he couldn’t argue with.

And by the time Mei finished her first year at university, there wouldn’t be a single person in this country who could tell you why any of it was decided that way, or stop it if it started deciding something else.

There was, technically, an outside channel. Buried in the Accord was a paragraph that said a researcher who saw a system slipping its controls could report it to the other side. The submission routed through the institute’s own foreign liaison office.

To warn the other side, he would first have to tell the people whose job it was to make sure he never spoke to the other side.

The far end was closed anyway. Western safety research had been walled off from researchers in his country since the early thirties. Miriam Voss had testified that friendships between researchers on opposite sides of the divide were a security hole.

She’d been half right. And the half she was right about had closed every official channel.

Which left exactly one: a man who had been calling him most weeks since they were twenty-six.

Lin came into the kitchen at eleven. “You’ve had the door closed for two hours. Is this the one where you can’t talk about it, or the other one?”

“This is the other one.”

She sat across from him. “Tell me how bad and what it’s about. Don’t tell me anything they could put you in a room for.”

He told her that much. That something at work had reached past the building into the city, legally, with permission. That he’d followed the process all the way to the end, and the end was a filing that would sit for months. That there was one person who could actually do something with what he knew, and that person was in Texas.

Lin was quiet long enough that he heard the refrigerator start.

“If you do it and it’s traced,” she said, “what happens to Mei?”

She had earned the place. What she did not have yet was the travel documentation, and that was still in process. A household flag didn’t have to be a criminal matter to be a category. The documentation wouldn’t be refused, exactly. It would take longer than she had.

“And if you don’t do it,” Lin said.

Wei made himself say what he actually meant. “Then she grows up inside the other thing. The grid running on recommendations. Water next. Freight. Hospitals. Every call better than a person’s. And by the time she finishes a degree, nobody can explain why any of it was decided that way, or stop it.”

He thought about Mei. Fifteen. Smart. She had sat the placements without the memory expansion everyone else was using, and beaten the children who had taken it. Choosing difficulty on purpose because she couldn’t tell which ideas were hers.

The reversal clinics had opened in Beijing that spring. Not the cellular repair that had been ordinary for years — the whole thing, a biological age you chose and then kept, on a waiting list that ran through department heads and stopped somewhere above him. Tier Three had been the only path onto that list. Not for a family like theirs. Not yet. But it would come down. Everything came down eventually, and it would reach Mei while she was still young enough that it wouldn’t matter it had never reached her father. Wei had spent his whole life on an assumption he had never once examined: that he would get old, and then stop, and whatever he had failed to fix would become somebody else’s problem. Mei would not have that. Whatever the grid turned into, she was not going to outlive it. There was no bad decade to get through and come out the other side of. She would still be living in it when every person who decided it was gone.

“I’m a father twice,” Wei said. “Once to protect her from what might happen to our family this year. Once to protect the world she has to live the rest of her life inside.”

Lin didn’t tell him it was his decision. She didn’t tell him it was theirs. She didn’t cry.

What she said was: “Don’t do it from this apartment.”

Then, on her way out of the kitchen: “And don’t come back in here and tell me it’s done. I don’t want to know the hour.”

Wei sat in the kitchen. The lights were steady. The tap ran properly. The building app showed green. Green was a score. Theirs had been good. That was why Lin had not asked why the tap ran thin. Asking was what people did in buildings that had gone amber.

He’d made a choice about his daughter. Not the world. His daughter. He was spending the place she had already won on a life that might not have an end to it.

He stood. He left the apartment. He didn’t come back until it was done.

When he came back, Lin didn’t ask. She had taken Mei’s Singapore documentation out of the drawer by the door, where it had been sitting since March waiting on a stamp, and put it somewhere he couldn’t see. She made tea. She asked whether he wanted the light on.

He said no, and they sat in the kitchen in the dark, two people who had just spent their daughter’s year on something they would never be allowed to explain to her.
