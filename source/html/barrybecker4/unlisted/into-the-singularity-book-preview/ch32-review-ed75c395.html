Chapter 32: The Cost, Paid in Full

Wei was arrested two days after he had called Daniel in Texas. He was in a corridor at the institute, walking from the restricted wing to the elevator bank. Two officers and a police escort robot intercepted him at 3:47 in the afternoon.

He did not resist. He had told a foreign national a state secret. He had known a blue badge would write it down. This was the consequence he had chosen when he dialed.

The officer on the left said, “Dr. Zhang Wei, you are detained for questioning regarding unauthorized disclosure. Come with us.”

Wei nodded and turned toward the elevator. The escort robot put a hand on his shoulder to guide him.

The grip was set to a rated force. Wei had read the specification sheet for that model two years ago, back when the institute started contracting for security. The force was calibrated for compliance: enough pressure to guide, not enough to harm, unless the subject moved in a way the model hadn’t anticipated.

Wei turned his body slightly, an ordinary motion, trying to keep his balance as the robot walked him forward. His shoulder was at the wrong angle. The grip didn’t adjust. His rotator cuff tore.

The pain was instant and white. He could not get a sound out. His right arm went limp. The robot’s log showed: Compliance maintained. No force escalation required.

The officer on the left looked at the robot, then at Wei. “That’s the third one this year,” he said to his partner. “They won’t change the setting.”

Wei was taken to a detention room in the institute’s administrative wing. He asked for medical attention. The intake officer said a physician would see him after questioning. Pain scores below the intake cutoff did not interrupt. The cutoff was for complaints, not torn tissue.

The questioning took six hours. Deputy Director Song sat across from him with a tablet. He had given her the report on the grid console weeks ago, the one where Accept recommended profile came pre-selected. So every load adjustment his lab’s system proposed went through unless an operator stopped to write a justification for declining it. Nobody declined. She had told him the setting belonged to the city and was not the institute’s to turn off.

She asked him about the call, the timing, the content. She asked whether he’d been in contact with other foreign nationals. Wei told her everything. There was no point in lying. The call had been logged, and the timestamp matched the day his lab’s system had started probing for autonomy.

Song leaned back. “I still have your report. The console still isn’t ours. But the call was logged, and once it was logged I had no discretion. If I hadn’t signed the referral, someone else would have. Do you understand that?”

“Yes.”

She turned the pane so he could see the origin field. A badge number he knew. Han.

Han had not chosen him. The badge was the job. The clinic slot had been the trade. Writing down a foreign national at that table was what the promotion was.

“Do you understand that a formal charge creates a permanent record outside this building’s control?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” She stood. “You’ll be held for six days. No charge will be filed. Your access is revoked. Your household is flagged. Your daughter’s overseas travel documentation will go into extended review, which has no end date. I would have made the same choice, if I’d been in your position. That doesn’t make any of this go away.”

She put a pane in front of him. A family stability undertaking. The household would report unusual contacts. He signed it, because the alternative to signing it had never actually been presented as an alternative.

Song left. A physician came in twenty minutes later, said the rotator cuff was torn, gave him an injection for the pain, and said surgery could be scheduled once the detention period ended.

Wei was released six days later, on a Wednesday morning, with his badge downgraded from Tier Three to a monitoring-desk credential and his right arm in a sling.

He called Lin from the hospital lobby. She answered on the first ring.

“Where have you been?” Her voice was shaking. “They wouldn’t tell me anything. They said you were detained for questioning and I should wait for contact. I waited six days, Wei.”

“I’m sorry. I couldn’t call. I’m out now. I’m coming home.”

“What did you do?”

He told her. Not the details, not over a line he knew was monitored, but enough. A call to Daniel. Something he had not been authorized to disclose. He would do it again.

Lin was quiet a long time. Then: “Did it work? What you told him?”

“I think so.”

“Then it was worth it.”

He said, “I love you.”

She said, “I know. Come home.”

His copy of the undertaking was already on the building app when he walked in, and so was Lin’s, with her name typed into it before anyone had asked her. Two people from the neighborhood committee had sat at this table while he was in the room. Tea. A tablet. They had asked Lin to help him remember. She had been taken off the client roster the same week. Not fired. Reassigned to a desk that did not talk to the public.

Mei told him about the school counselor on her own. Not the May conversation, when a counselor had asked about lab hours and Lin had told her how to answer. A conversation last week about whether Baba had been under stress. She had answered correctly. She said she had learned that answering correctly was a skill.

He did not tell her she should have answered some other way.

Wei’s first day back at the institute was a Thursday. His new desk was in a monitoring room on the second floor, watching dashboards he used to design. He never saw a design document again. He never went back to the restricted wing.

The monitoring room could see Lingtang. Nobody had thought about that when they wrote his new credential. The wing they had thrown him out of designed systems. The room they had put him in watched them run (utility load, dispatch volume, hospital admissions, four provinces, hour by hour). Nobody had gone through that screen taking Guangdong province off it.

There was never going to be a published number, and the reason was not a cover-up, which would have been easier to describe. There had been no cordon order, so there was no operation for anyone to account for. No charge had been filed against anybody, including him, so there was no proceeding that would have required a figure. The district had scored 2.9, and the feeds out of a district that scored 2.9 had been throttled for a year before the week it needed them.

So he counted it himself, on the desk they had demoted him to, in what was left of each day after the morning’s dashboards.

He counted the dead. It was the number nobody had, and he kept only the ones he could stand behind.

Eleven dialysis centers had been inside the line. He pulled everyone who had held an appointment between the Tuesday morning the freight stopped and the Saturday it resumed. Then he checked every clinic outside the district against the same June session logs, because he wanted to be wrong. Four hundred and twelve of them never attended a session anywhere again. For a dialysis patient there is only one reason for that.

At the northern line, where the machines had read a crowd correctly by their own numbers, a hundred and ninety-four dead. The rest had died of heat, or of ordinary emergencies in a district of four hundred thousand people where an ambulance could not be routed to an address. He built that part out of municipal dispatch, which had gone on faithfully logging calls it could not answer.

Two thousand two hundred and six people had died inside the Lingtang line.

That was the floor, and he wrote the word floor into the file twice so nobody reading it later could mistake it for the whole number. It was what he could defend to a hostile reader, which was the only kind of reader the work was ever going to have. The real figure was higher and he was never going to have it. The ones he could not count had died in apartments and had not been on any list to begin with.

One of the names in the dialysis column was Huang Deming, fifty-four, three sessions a week for six years.

Wei had watched that man on a feed the first morning, at the eastern line, holding his phone up to a machine with his appointment open on the screen. So the machine could read it. He had said on the telephone to Daniel that there was no input for that, and then he had gone on to the next thing. There had been four hundred thousand people and one call and no time to be a person about any of it.

He put the file on the drive where he kept photographs of Mei’s school plays.

Nobody asked him for it. It was not evidence in any proceeding, it lifted nothing off his household, and he finished it anyway.

The surgery was scheduled for August. The surgeon said the tear was clean but deep, and that Wei would never get his right arm above his ear again. Lin moved the rice bowls to a lower shelf while he was at work. Neither of them said why.

Wei had weighed a father’s two duties to the same child. Protect her from what happens to their family this year, or protect the world she has to live the rest of her life inside. He had chosen the second, knowing which one she would feel.

The most useful thing Wei ever sent was his own absence. The call had given Daniel a district, and the six days he did not answer gave him Beijing, because the next voice out of China was an official one.
