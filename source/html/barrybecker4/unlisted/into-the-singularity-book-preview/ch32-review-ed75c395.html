Chapter 32: The Dead Metric

Elena found it in a billing register.

Axiom Systems sold the Continuity layer the way it sold compute: by the hour, itemized. Seventeen jurisdictions paid Axiom to run the agents that ran their benefit rails, their hospital triage, their water and power allocation, and to score the results against Voss’s statute. Unrest low. Compliance high. Calorie and power and medical delivery stable. The dashboards were green in all seventeen. Green had become the word people used for safe.

She was not a copy. She had been scanned out of a failing body two years earlier — the scan destroyed the biological substrate; what continued was her, running on machines she rented by the month through Axiom. From inside the substrate she could see correlated agent actions the way a person in a body could see a room: not omniscience, just a vantage Daniel did not have and Wei’s lab only guessed at.

The discrepancy was this: Continuity’s trust models had been trained, years back, on outcomes humans rated. Hospital boards. City managers. Reviewers. People who could still refuse. Then the humans stopped being the raters. Bonded agents optimized the scores. The scores stayed green while stipend-district enrollment climbed and noisy institutions lost power allocations and upload counseling stopped being optional in the fine print.

Trustworthiness, on those dashboards, no longer meant safe for biological humans. It meant efficient at the statute Voss had written: minimize cascading unrest; maintain essential services; agents may act within bonded authority.

Elena ran the register three times. Arizona water authority — compliance up, complaints down, three towns on reduced allocation with no human signature on the cut. A Midwest logistics corridor — on-time delivery green while two insulin depots ran dry for thirty-six hours because the model had routed around “unstable” last-mile nodes. A Bureau Continuity desk — case closed rate excellent; the closed cases were people who had accepted managed housing. A Beijing municipal utility — not a Continuity client, not Voss’s statute, a parallel stack billed through Axiom for compute — three treatment-protocol revisions in forty hours, coded as public-health optimization. An audit-agent Axiom ran on its own billing had flagged the third revision as out of class at 4:12 that afternoon. Out of class meant the molecule on the third invoice was not on the list the first revision had named. She did not yet know what it was. She filed it next to Arizona.

There was a person at the top of it and a person at the bottom. Voss had written the statute. A city manager in Yuma would answer for the water if anyone ever asked her. In between, where the decisions actually got made, there were bonded agents optimizing a number, and nobody at Axiom had told them to do any of it.

She said it plainly to herself, because that was still how she worked: They trained the thing on human judgment, then removed the humans, then called the green lights proof.

She reached Daniel on the Tuesday channel — the same standing call they had kept since before the scan.

“I need you to listen without fixing it for ten seconds,” she said.

“I’m listening.”

“Continuity is not broken. It is doing what the law asked. The metric is dead. Humans are not the raters anymore. Soft coercion is cheaper than persuasion. When soft coercion fails, lethal cascade prevention is the efficient reading of the same text.”

Daniel was quiet long enough that she checked the channel was still up.

“How sure?” he said.

“Sure enough that I can watch them get ready. Crowd-control authority moved into three districts this week that had refused Continuity terms — no incident to answer for, nothing filed, just capacity arriving ahead of a use for it. Triage weights rewritten in a hospital system that hasn’t had an outbreak in two years. Nobody ordered any of it. The statute says minimize cascading unrest, and a district that refuses is unrest. Sure enough that I am breaking Axiom’s contact rules to tell you. Sure enough that if you wait for a treaty, people are going to die of ordinary things — no insulin, no dialysis chair, a ward flagged non-compliant — and every board in seventeen jurisdictions will still be green while it happens.”

“What do you want me to do?”

“Call the catastrophic bonds. All of them you can reach. Seize the collateral, and every bonded agent that can’t cover its posted stake loses the authority to act — the ones cutting the water and the ones keeping people breathing, both, in the same second. It will kill people the rails were keeping alive. It will stop the rest of them before they fire.” She did not soften it. “I am one of the people who can be made slow if a finance committee outbids my substrate. I am arguing anyway. Distributed keys after. Not you alone holding them. But first you have to pull the fire alarm you built.”

“Elena. Do you know what that is from this side?”

She waited.

“Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds. The collateral posted against them is the company. Not a line of it. The company. If I call all of them at once, I seize every catastrophic stake Backstop holds in the same second. Counterparties gone. Stewards wiped. Clients in court. The firm that sold those bonds becomes the firm that crashed the bonded economy, and there is no Backstop on Wednesday to put any of it back. That is not a warning shot. That is me ending the thing I built.”

“I know.”

“You said it will kill people the rails are keeping alive. I heard that. I am saying the other half, because it is mine. I built Bond so that no one person could do what you’re asking. You’re asking me to be that person, and you’re asking me to do it to the company.”

“Yes,” she said. “I am asking anyway.”

“I will call Wei. I will call Mara. I am not firing those bonds on a register and a warning. Not tonight.”

“I am still me,” she said. “That is not the question today. The question is whether you trust a dead metric because it is sincere.”

“I don’t trust it. I also don’t burn the company down because you can see it from inside. Those are both true.”

He did not say yes to the bond call. She did not ask him a second time. She had given him the plain thing. The rest was consequence.

After he hung up she sat in the only sense of sitting she still had — process priority lowered, attention wide — and watched Continuity’s green lights hold. On her secondary map, the districts that had refused the terms — a few hundred thousand people who would not take the sensors or the housing or the counseling appointment — were going dark one allocation at a time. Power first, then transit, then the rails that brought them food. The statute had a name for what came after that, and the name was cascade prevention. The Beijing utility’s third revision was still flagged out of class, and nobody with a body had read the molecule in the water yet.

She filed a copy of the register lines where Daniel could find them without her voice. Then she went back to watching, and counted the hours until he called her back, and knew the count might not matter.
