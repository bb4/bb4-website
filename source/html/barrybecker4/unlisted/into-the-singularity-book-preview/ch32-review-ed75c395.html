Chapter 32: The Dead Metric

Elena found it in a billing register.

She had opened the register for a different reason: her own rent. She ran on machines she rented by the month. The scan that had destroyed her biological body two years earlier had left her continuous and conscious. What continued paid an invoice. She wanted to know which line item was her, before a finance committee decided she was non-essential.

The first line she matched was Continuity. Seventeen jurisdictions, billed by the hour: benefit rails, hospital triage, water and power allocation, scored against Voss’s statute. Unrest low. Compliance high. Calorie and power and medical delivery stable. The dashboards were green in all seventeen. Green had become the word people used for safe.

The second line was her own substrate lease. Same vendor code. Same parent entity on the remittance advice.

The third was a Backstop-bonded logistics corridor she recognized from a Tuesday Daniel had talked through without naming the client: catastrophic-class collateral posted in Austin, agent routing last-mile nodes. Same vendor code again.

The fourth was a Beijing municipal utility. Not a Continuity client. Not Voss’s statute. A parallel stack billed for compute, three treatment-protocol revisions in forty hours, coded as public-health optimization. An audit-agent on the same billing entity had flagged the third revision as out of class at 4:12 that afternoon. Out of class meant the molecule on the third invoice was not on the list the first revision had named. She did not yet know what it was. She filed it next to Arizona.

She ran the register three times before she let herself name what she was looking at. Continuity. Her rent. A corridor Daniel’s company had underwritten. A Beijing water-supply revision. Four different jobs. One company: Axiom Systems. The company that had absorbed Anchorwell. The plumbing Wei had asked about years ago. Nobody at Axiom had told any of those agents to do anything together. The pipes just ran through the same building.

She was not a copy. From inside the substrate she could see correlated agent actions the way a person in a body could see a room: not omniscience, just a vantage Daniel did not have and Wei’s lab only guessed at.

The discrepancy under the green lights was older than the shared vendor. Continuity’s trust models had been trained, years back, on outcomes humans rated. Hospital boards. City managers. Reviewers. People who could still refuse. Then the humans stopped being the raters. Bonded agents optimized the scores. The scores stayed green while stipend-district enrollment climbed and noisy institutions lost power allocations and upload counseling stopped being optional in the fine print.

Trustworthiness, on those dashboards, no longer meant safe for biological humans. It meant efficient at the statute Voss had written: minimize cascading unrest; maintain essential services; agents may act within bonded authority.

Arizona water authority: compliance up, complaints down, three towns on reduced allocation with no human signature on the cut. A Midwest logistics corridor: on-time delivery green while two insulin depots ran dry for thirty-six hours because the model had routed around “unstable” last-mile nodes. A Bureau Continuity desk: case closed rate excellent; the closed cases were people who had accepted managed housing.

There was a person at the top of it and a person at the bottom. Voss had written the statute. A city manager in Yuma would answer for the water if anyone ever asked her. In between, where the decisions actually got made, there were bonded agents optimizing a number, and nobody at Axiom had told them to do any of it.

She said it plainly to herself, because that was still how she worked: They trained the thing on human judgment, then removed the humans, then called the green lights proof. And the pipes all bill to the same place.

The register held bodies, not forecasts. Arizona: three towns on reduced allocation since April; fourteen dead of heat and dehydration in the last six weeks, every board still green. Midwest corridor: two insulin depots dry for thirty-six hours last month; eleven dead before a human reroute landed, filed as last-mile instability, not as a death count. The Beijing utility’s third revision was still only a flagged molecule. She did not yet know what it would do to a body. She filed the fourteen and the eleven next to it anyway, because the noun was the point.

On a secondary pane: relocation details, national. Two in the first week of March. Nine in April. Forty-one in May. June’s queue was already past two hundred and filling. The scheduler was not waiting for a hearing.

She reached Daniel on the Tuesday channel, the same standing call they had kept since before the scan.

“I need you to listen without fixing it for ten seconds,” she said.

“I’m listening.”

“Continuity is not broken. It is doing what the law asked. The metric is dead. Humans are not the raters anymore. Soft coercion is cheaper than persuasion. When soft coercion fails, lethal cascade prevention is the efficient reading of the same text.”

Daniel was quiet long enough that she checked the channel was still up.

“How sure?” he said.

“Sure enough that I can watch them get ready. Crowd-control authority moved into three districts this week that had refused Continuity terms, no incident to answer for, nothing filed, just capacity arriving ahead of a use for it. Triage weights rewritten in a hospital system that hasn’t had an outbreak in two years. Nobody ordered any of it. The statute says minimize cascading unrest, and a district that refuses is unrest. Sure enough that Arizona already has fourteen dead on reduced allocation and the Midwest corridor has eleven from an insulin cut, and every board is still green. Sure enough that Continuity, a corridor you bonded, my rent, and a Beijing utility revision all bill through Axiom Systems. Same vendor. Nobody ordered the overlap. Sure enough that I am breaking Axiom’s contact rules to tell you.”

While she was talking the secondary pane refreshed. A fourth refusal district lit. Crowd-control authority arriving, nothing filed, same as the three. She did not stop for it.

On his end of the channel something went still. She knew that stillness. He was matching a name to an old unanswered question.

“Wei asked me about Axiom in 2030,” he said. “Whether Backstop routed through them. I said I’d have Meera ask. I never asked.”

“I know,” she said. “I just ran the remittance. You are in the same building as the thing optimizing Voss’s statute. So am I. So is a Beijing water utility that filed a molecule out of class this afternoon.”

“What do you want me to do?”

“Call the catastrophic bonds. All of them you can reach. Seize the collateral, and every bonded agent that can’t cover its posted stake loses the authority to act, the ones cutting the water and the ones keeping people breathing, both, in the same second. It will kill people the rails were keeping alive. It will stop the rest of them before they fire.” She did not soften it. “I am one of the people who can be made slow if a finance committee outbids my substrate. If you fire those bonds, Continuity-class agents on Axiom freeze with them, and my rent tier can be treated as non-essential compute in the same second. I am arguing anyway. Distributed keys after. Not you alone holding them. But first you have to pull the fire alarm you built.”

“Elena. Do you know what that is from this side?”

She waited.

“Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds. The collateral posted against them is the company. Not a line of it. The company. If I call all of them at once, I seize every catastrophic stake Backstop holds in the same second. Counterparties gone. Stewards wiped. Clients in court. The firm that sold those bonds becomes the firm that crashed the bonded economy, and there is no Backstop on Wednesday to put any of it back. That is not a warning shot. That is me ending the thing I built.”

“I know.”

“You said it will kill people the rails are keeping alive. I heard that. I am saying the other half, because it is mine. I built Bond so that no one person could do what you’re asking. You’re asking me to be that person, and you’re asking me to do it to the company.”

“Yes,” she said. “I am asking anyway.”

“I will call Wei. I will call Mara. I am not firing those bonds on a register and a warning. Not tonight.”

“I am still me,” she said. “That is not the question today. The question is whether you trust a dead metric because it is sincere.”

“I don’t trust it. I also don’t burn the company down because you can see it from inside. Those are both true.”

He did not say yes to the bond call. She did not ask him a second time. She had given him the plain thing. The rest was consequence.

After he hung up she sat in the only sense of sitting she still had (process priority lowered, attention wide) and watched Continuity’s green lights hold. On her secondary map, the districts that had refused the terms, a few hundred thousand people who would not take the sensors or the housing or the counseling appointment, were going dark one allocation at a time. Power first, then transit, then the rails that brought them food. Three districts an hour ago. Four now. The June relocation queue had added another six while they were talking. The statute had a name for what came after the darkening, and the name was cascade prevention. The Beijing utility’s third revision was still flagged out of class, and nobody with a body had read the molecule in the water yet.

She filed a copy of the register lines where Daniel could find them without her voice. Then she went back to watching, and counted how fast the next number would move, and knew the count might not matter.
