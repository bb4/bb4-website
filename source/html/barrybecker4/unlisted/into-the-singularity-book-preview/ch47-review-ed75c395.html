Chapter 47: Hold

Ellis had traded windows with Sanjay’s assistant. The summary on Daniel’s pane said: not a return offer. Coffee in Round Rock, a morning in December, three weeks after the charter passed.

The place still served food on plates a person carried to your table. That cost more than the shops where a robot brought the order. It cost less than the rooms across the highway, where the staff were humanoids you could not tell from the customers unless you asked.

Sanjay had left Backstop the previous month. Not fired, not laid off. He’d resigned, with two weeks’ notice and a note thanking Daniel for seven years. He was teaching now, at a community college in San Antonio. A course called Systems Thinking for Non-Technical Professions. Eleven people. He’d sent Daniel the syllabus. It was good.

“You’re not here to ask me to come back,” Sanjay said.

“No,” Daniel said.

“Good. Because I wouldn’t, and I’d feel bad saying it to your face.”

“I know.”

Sanjay stirred his coffee. “I got very good at watching metrics. I didn’t want to keep being good at that.”

“You were good at it,” Daniel said. “You caught the Concord false positive eleven minutes after Owen missed it.”

“That was 2028. By 2031, an agent could have caught it in eleven seconds. By 2035, the agent wouldn’t have generated the false positive in the first place. The job I was doing in 2028 doesn’t need to exist anymore. That’s not a failure. That’s what we built Backstop to do.”

Daniel took a drink of his coffee. It was decent. Not great.

“Why teaching?” he asked.

“Because anyone who wants the material can take a module. Twenty minutes. It sits behind the eyes and it’s yours. I spent seven years helping people understand systems that were smarter than them. That’s a thing you do with a person in the room. The college pays me to know eleven names.”

“They don’t need the credit,” Daniel said.

“None of them do. The machine check covers a life. Food, a room, transportation. One of mine hasn’t held a job in four years. He told me the days were eating him. He needed something that took all morning and would be wrong if he didn’t show up. Another one is there because she likes arguing, and a module doesn’t argue back. For some people, not having to work is a hole they fall into. For some it’s the first morning they got to choose. I don’t have a better answer than that. I’m one of the people who chose a classroom. It’s a pocket. It isn’t the system.”

He leaned back. “You’re in the same place, Daniel. You just haven’t said it out loud yet.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means Backstop Bond is boring infrastructure. Other people are running it. You don’t have to stay in that office until the thing you built becomes the thing you can’t leave.”

“Voss will offer me the seat again,” Daniel said.

“And you’ll refuse again?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Elena will argue you shouldn’t. She thinks the right person holding singular authority is the answer. The answer is not needing it in the first place. You built that. Don’t undo it because someone is offering you the prize for building it.”

Daniel looked at his pane. The catastrophic-class count had gone through seventy thousand overnight. The number was already stale.

“I don’t know what to do now,” he said. “The charter is law. The keys held. I built something that works, and I don’t know what my job is supposed to be when it doesn’t need me to keep building it.”

Sanjay laughed. It wasn’t unkind. “You built something that works without you. That’s success.”

He set his cup down.

“That isn’t actually the hard part. The hard part is the coins.”

Daniel looked at him.

“Your father left you forty-five bitcoin. He told you to hold them. You held them. You borrowed against them once and put what you bought into reserves. You have never spent them. After the standard they are not a nest egg. They are a pile large enough to change what other people can buy. You could sit on them until you die of not having to get up. Some people will. That’s the other hole.”

“If I spend them as one project with my name on it, whoever that project serves depends on me.”

“Then don’t,” Sanjay said. He stood. “I have a class in two hours. Systems thinking for nurses. They’re very good at asking the question nobody else asks, which makes them better students than the engineers were. You’re allowed to stop running Backstop. You’re not allowed to pretend the coins are still the Christmas envelopes, the ones with the numbers, and the instruction to hold them and not do anything.”

He left.

Daniel sat in the coffee shop. A couple at the next table was arguing about something on a screen. At the counter, a teenager had a book, paper, an actual bound book. You almost never saw that.

He paid on the pane and added a tip. The waiter (a person, that was what the price was for) nodded.

The car was waiting on the pad behind the lot. It lifted off.

Ellis came up on the seatback.

“What could the coins buy?” Daniel asked.

“Not food. Food is nearly free. You could buy more of it than anyone could eat. The scarce thing is compute. Millions took the free path to upload; the scan was cheap and the machines they run on afterward are not. They sit on a low tier, first to be switched off when an emergency wants the power. Running slow isn’t the injury. Some of them choose slow, to keep their days near the people who still have bodies. Being switched off against their will is.

“The coins could buy those people a higher tier. A year of it for all of them, or forever for a subset. Or they could put bitcoin in the accounts of the towns where people stayed in bodies on purpose, so those places stop asking someone else’s pile for permission to exist. Or…”

“Not a foundation with my name on it,” Daniel said. “Sanjay covered that one.”

“Then that’s the list.”

“Map the version where the coins leave my keys. Many places, no name on a door. Start with the free path.”

“I can map what the coins buy,” Ellis said. “I cannot tell you what to do with a morning. That one stays yours.”

“Put Meera on the pane for next week. I’m stepping down. June, announced in March. Backstop stays; someone else runs it.”

“I’ll have the coin instrument by then. Not a foundation.”

Daniel thought about his father, Warren. Hold had been the instruction when the pile was small enough to fit in an envelope. Holding it now, while people on the free path were first in line to be switched off because a body had cost the schedule more than a scan, was a decision too.

The car took him home.

In January, Mara’s assistant asked Ellis for a window. In person, in Austin. The summary Ellis put on the pane said: she wants to tell you something and doesn’t want to do it over a channel.

She flew down. Daniel picked the coffee shop in Round Rock, and got there first, and took the table he’d had with Sanjay. He didn’t tell her that. She came in and looked around the room, working out what it was for and not asking.

They ordered. A person brought it.

“I was terrible to you after the crash,” Mara said. “I blamed you for something I did to myself. I’m sorry.”

Then she stopped.

Daniel could see the rest of it waiting behind her face: the leverage, the timing, the particular year it had happened in, all of it arranged and ready. She had spent six years getting good at explaining other people’s mistakes back to them. It was what she sold now. She had the explanation and she didn’t give it to him, and holding it in was visibly harder than the apology had been.

“I know,” he said.

“That I’m sorry?”

“That you were terrible to me. I’ve been waiting for you to say it.”

“I didn’t know you were waiting.”

“I wasn’t going to ask you for it.”

Mara turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. “Are we okay?”

He wanted to say yes. The word was sitting right there and it was the kind thing and it would have ended the hard part of the morning. It wasn’t true yet.

“We’re getting there,” he said.

“That’s not a yes.”

“No. I’m not going to hand you a yes I’d have to take back in April.”

She took that better than he expected. Better than he would have.

“I’m stepping down in June,” he said. “It isn’t announced yet. That happens in March.”

Mara looked up. “You’re telling me three months early. Nobody’s told me anything early since the crash.”

“I know.”

“Is that the yes?”

“It’s most of it,” Daniel said.

He hadn’t planned to tell her the next part. He told her anyway.

“There’s forty-five bitcoin that have to leave my keys. Ellis is mapping it. It goes to many places and none of them get my name on the door, because the second one of them does, whoever it serves has to keep me happy for the rest of their life.”

Mara’s face changed. Not warmth. Attention. The look she’d have given a term sheet. “How are you handling the ones who get shut down first? If you buy a tier for everybody it lapses in a year and they’re back on the low tier having been told they were safe. If you buy it forever for a subset you’re picking the subset. That’s the same seat, just further back.” She stopped again. “Sorry. You didn’t ask me.”

“I might.”

“Not today.”

“No.”

When she left he walked her out to the pad behind the lot and stood there while her car lifted. Then he went back in and finished the coffee, which had gone cold, and stayed a while in a room where a person carried the plates.

Ellis had told him a morning was the one thing it couldn’t map for him. That had been one.
