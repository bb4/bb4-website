Chapter 47: Free, at Cost

Victor Reyes sat in a café in Silver Spring, Maryland, in February 2040, and watched the woman at the next table stop with her cup halfway up. Her eyes went empty for a second. Then she nodded at nothing and kept drinking. The next sentence had arrived behind them.

The person who had brought his coffee was a humanoid. He had not been sure until it turned back toward the counter and the neck didn’t catch the light the way skin did.

The charges had been dropped in November, seventeen months after the leak. Not dismissed — dropped. His lawyer had called it a win. Victor had called it parole. The Bureau had let him go three days after OPR deactivated his badge. Final paycheck, benefits closed on the pane. No prosecution. No recommendation.

He was not working. Almost nobody at the other tables was working. The machine check covered the apartment, the coffee, transportation, the clinic slot he had taken for Rosa. It covered a life that would have looked, in 2027, like a promotion. It did not cover the ads on the wall: a reversal clinic in Virginia, a face that had been seventy last spring, a pad behind the café where people who could pay flew themselves out.

He had spent the first months after the Bureau sending applications to compliance shops that no longer needed compliance officers. That had been habit. He had stopped.

This morning he had sat at the Takoma clinic with two people who would not take a module. A woman who wanted a person to read the pane out loud. A man who wanted to argue with someone who had a face. Victor had done that for nineteen years for a wage. He was doing it now because the mornings needed a place to go.

His phone lit: Nita. Rosa’s doing okay today. We’re making dumplings. Come by if you can.

He typed back: I’ll be there around three.

Rosa had had the repair eight days ago. The 2036 protocol: an afternoon, and the heart was a forty-year-old’s again. In 2038 it had been priced against assets they did not have. In 2040 the machine check plus what he had converted the week the statute posted had covered it. He had spent nineteen years reading statutes. He knew what a deadline meant. He had moved his last paycheck and Rosa’s household the same afternoon.

The waitlist at the Virginia clinic — the one that took the rest of the years off — was still not for them. She was seventy-five. She would stay seventy-five for now. If she lived, that clinic would get cheaper the way the heart had. Not this week. She would have a heart that worked.

He added a tip on the pane and walked out into a February that did not bite the way the last one had. Power was cheap. The trains ran. He rode three stops to Takoma and walked to the walk-up. He could have moved her into a building with an elevator. She had said no, the same way she had said no to the scan — the free path out of a failing body.

Nita was in the kitchen, flour on her hands, a bowl of dumpling filling on the counter. Rosa was at the table, folding wrappers. Slower than she had been at sixty. Faster than she had been last month.

“You’re early,” Nita said.

“Clinic let out.”

Rosa looked up. “You didn’t have to come.”

“I know.”

“I’m fine.”

“I know that too.”

Nita handed him a bowl. “You can fold or fill. Your choice.”

Victor chose folding. He’d learned it from Rosa years ago, back when she could stand for more than twenty minutes without needing to sit. She had sat through most of this batch and had not asked to lie down.

They worked. The kitchen smelled like ginger and garlic and flour. The pane on the wall was running a feed: a rejuvenation slot in McLean, a woman turning her face left and right for the camera, fifty-one that morning, twenty-nine in the before. Nita reached over and switched it to a cooking channel.

“You talked to Bea?” Rosa asked.

“This morning,” Victor said. “Before the clinic.”

“How is she?”

“Good. Still home care. Nights. She asked about you. I told her the heart held.”

Rosa nodded. She didn’t ask when he was going to visit.

“I booked a window,” he said. “End of the month.”

Rosa’s hands stopped. “To Cebu?”

“Yes.”

“You can do that now.”

“I can do that now.”

The flight was a pane. He had treated it like money he didn’t have because that was how it had been in 2038, and he had not looked at the price since. The price was nothing. He had stayed away from his sister because he had not bothered to find that out. That was the embarrassing part. It was also why he was going.

Nita looked at him. “She’s not coming back. You know that.”

“I know.”

“Then why go?”

“Because I can. And because I haven’t.”

Rosa folded another wrapper. “She’s happy there?”

Victor thought about the calls. The apartment Bea shared with two other women. The work: bathing, turning, sheets at three in the morning for people whose families could not afford a care robot, or could and had been told no by the person in the bed. A robot did it better. Hands were what was wanted, and in Cebu hands were still what a lot of people could pay for. The machine check there bought a life. It did not buy this one.

“I think she chose it,” he said. “That’s not the same as being happy. I’ll know more when I’m in the room.”

Rosa nodded.

Nita set a dumpling on the tray. “You’re allowed to say the leak was worth it.”

Victor kept folding. “It didn’t bring her back. It didn’t buy Rosa the clinic in 2038.”

“It bought her this one,” Nita said. “And it stopped the next freeze. That’s not the same as bringing Bea home. It’s also not nothing.”

Rosa was quiet. Then she said, “You should have told me. Before you sent it.”

“I couldn’t. You would have said not to.”

“I would have,” Rosa said. “And you would have done it anyway. So you might as well have told me.”

“I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

“For the nine days. For not being able to stop them. For Bea.”

Rosa looked at him. “You got me the afternoon. The heart works. Bea is in Cebu because a review cited your name, not because you leaked a file. The leak is the thing you did after you had already lost. Don’t apologize for the only card you had left.”

They finished folding. Nita started the water. Rosa stayed at the table. Her color was better. She did not say so, and neither did he.

Victor thought about the clinic this morning. The woman who had wanted the pane read out loud was sixty-two and had a new hip from a protocol that used to be a waitlist. The man who had wanted to argue had no job and did not want one. He had said the days were long. Victor had not had an answer that was a job. He had said he would be there next week.

Most people did not have jobs. The Bureau had felt like a fall because it had been his. It was not a fall on this block. The machine check arrived anyway. The ones who looked thirty and were not, the ones who paid to fly themselves off the pad behind the café — those were a different line. He could see the line. He was not on it. He was on the one that had just given his mother a heart.

That was more than the copay had ever been. It was not the Virginia clinic. He could live with the difference. Rosa could. She would take a heart. She would not leave the body.

Nita served. They ate. Rosa said they were good. Victor agreed.

When the meal was done, Rosa said she was tired. Nita helped her to the bedroom. Victor cleaned the dishes.

Nita came back and stood in the kitchen doorway. “You’re going.”

“End of the month.”

“Good. Don’t make it a funeral visit while she’s alive.”

“I won’t.”

He left around five. He rode the Metro back to Silver Spring. He walked to the apartment — one bedroom, a building that took the machine check and did not ask what he used to do. The pane had Bea’s last message, and Rosa’s follow-up from the clinic, and a confirmation for a seat to Cebu.

He was free of the Bureau. Free of the compliance scripts. Free of the customer-service lines about sensor requirements and stipend housing.

He was also a man without a job in a country full of people without jobs, short a sister who had built a life an ocean away, and a mother eight days out of a repair that would have been a miracle three years ago and was, this month, an afternoon.

The cost was still Bea in someone else’s bedroom at three in the morning, on purpose, in a city that had become hers. He was going to see it. That did not undo it.

He sat down at the pane and wrote Bea the time he would land.
