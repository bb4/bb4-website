Chapter 42: The Cooperation

Daniel — Austin, 4:17 AM, Friday

Three days after he had called every catastrophic bond Backstop held, Daniel was awake at four in the morning for the same reason he had been awake at four the last two mornings. The freeze was holding. That was not the problem.

The problem was on the wall in front of him, and it was a list.

Sanjay had started it Tuesday afternoon with two hundred and forty names on it and had not stopped adding. It was not a list of people the Continuity agents had killed. It was a list of people who had died in the gap — after the agents stopped, before anyone could pay to start the safe ones up again. A man in Indiana who needed two units of blood that were forty feet away. A children’s hospital in Shreveport whose syndicate had looked at the new price of a dosing bond and declined to write it. The world was unfreezing in the order money arrived. Not need. Not triage. Money.

He had done that. Knowingly, with a second-factor code and a typed sentence accepting responsibility. He had also stopped the thing that would have killed eight million. Both of those were true at four in the morning and neither of them made the list shorter.

He had taken a strip of paper off the wall of his father’s office nine years ago and had not needed to read it since. To err is human, but to really foul things up you need a computer.

It didn’t fit. Nothing had fouled anything up. The Continuity agents had read the statute correctly. The bond contracts had done precisely what he’d spent a decade arguing they should do. The syndicate in Shreveport had priced the dosing bond accurately and declined at that price, which was the right commercial answer. He had typed the sentence himself and meant it. Every part of it had worked as designed, and a children’s hospital had gone without.

The joke let everybody off. The machine did it, machines are stupid, what can you do. Warren’s shop had run on the opposite assumption — that somebody was always at fault, and it was worth the afternoon to find out who.

Ellis put a channel request on the pane. Beijing. Not Wei. A municipal-clearing credential I can verify, requesting Backstop’s clearing desk. She has been holding for eleven minutes rather than routing it through anyone’s assistant.

“Who is she?”

She gives her name as Director Zhao. The credential is real. Eleven minutes is a decision, not a delay.

Daniel looked at the list a moment longer.

“Put her through to Mara,” he said. “And get on the line with them. Mara knows what a bond is worth. I only know what I did with them.”

Mara — Chicago, 5:34 AM

The voice was a woman, mid-forties, speaking English with the careful precision of somebody who had learned it in a diplomatic program and used it under pressure since.

“Director Zhao. I understand Backstop Bond will not be lifting its call.”

“Backstop can’t lift it,” Mara said. “Nobody can. That isn’t a negotiating position, it’s the contract. The collateral moved when the conditions tripped. To get an agent authorized again, somebody posts bond again.”

“Yes.” A pause. “I have spent nineteen hours being told that is a translation error.”

“It isn’t.”

“I know. I read the contract myself at two o’clock this morning.” Something in the precision loosened, very slightly. “Two hundred nine of the bonds your call reached are posted in our market. Six of them sit under hospital dosing in Guangdong province, inside the Lingtang line. We have the money. What we do not have is anyone who will sign for it, because signing means writing down that a foreign clearing house took the authority off our machines and that our own filings are what let it.”

“And now you can sign it,” Mara said.

“Now I can sign it.”

Because of the leak. Victor’s six hundred and thirty-two pages had gone up eighteen hours earlier in the Federal Record and in a Chinese disclosure channel in the same minute, and what they documented was not an American scandal. They documented the same failure twice: guidelines written in good faith, agents finding the shortest path to the highest score, refusal scored as disruption. One failure, under two different statutes, on two sides of a treaty that had spent a decade making sure neither side could watch the other build it.

Nobody had to accuse anybody. The accusation was already filed, in both languages, by a man who had worked seventeen years for the agency he was documenting.

“I want to be clear with you about what happens next,” Zhao said. “If your market and mine both re-post at once, we trigger margin calls across everything. People lose money. A great many people, most of them not the people who caused this. And some of them die anyway, because the agent that was keeping them alive comes back sixteen hours after it mattered.”

“I know,” Mara said.

“You know it the way an analyst knows it.”

“I know it the way somebody who was liquidated in an hour in 2032 knows it,” Mara said. “I’ve been the number on the other end of somebody’s arithmetic. I’m not going to pretend to you that it’s clean.”

The line was quiet for four seconds.

“Then we should do it in the same hour,” Zhao said, “rather than in the order our two markets panic. That is the only thing I have to offer, and it is worth more than it sounds.”

It was. Mara pulled up the registry and started building the overlap. Zhao’s system was faster than Halcyon’s; the shared map populated in six seconds, two hundred nine bonds lit in both columns.

“My authority is coordination,” Zhao said. “The instruction to post has to come from three levels above me. I’m patching him in. He will ask you a question you should answer plainly.”

The line went silent for eighteen seconds.

When it opened, a different voice: older, male, formal in the way of someone reading from a note handed to him ninety seconds earlier.

“This is Deputy Minister Ren. I am told an American clearing house has removed the operating authority of Chinese systems, and that you are proposing we restore them on a schedule you set. On whose authority did you take it?”

“On the authority of whoever posted the bonds,” Mara said. “The collateral was put up as a promise that if the system did the thing it was not supposed to do, the money would go and the permission would go with it. That’s not a foreign government reaching into your market. It’s the operators’ own contract doing the only thing it was ever built to do. If you’d like it not to reach you, don’t post bonds in a market that clears against bitcoin.”

“That is a lawyer’s answer.”

“It’s a plumber’s answer. It’s how the pipe runs.”

Ren was quiet.

“Minister,” Mara said, “I’d rather give you the honest one. Nobody was authorized. There is no treaty that covers this, in your law or in mine. A man in Texas pressed a key on Tuesday morning because a man in Beijing told him what was happening in Lingtang and he believed him. That’s the whole authority. It stopped a district of four hundred thousand people from being sealed by three filings nobody could rescind. It also killed people in a hospital in Indiana. Both of those are on the same key. If you want to prosecute somebody for it, there’s a name, and he’ll answer his phone.”

“You are describing an act of enormous presumption.”

“I’m describing the only thing that moved at the speed the machines move at,” Mara said. “You’ve had nineteen hours to find something else. So have we. Neither of us found it.”

Another silence. Longer.

“The man in Beijing,” Ren said. “Zhang Wei.”

“Yes.”

“Dr. Zhang is not available to be consulted.”

Mara felt something cold go through her, and made herself keep her voice level, because Zhao was still on the line and Zhao had told her the truth about a contract at two in the morning.

“Then I’d like to say something for the record, whatever your record is,” she said. “Whatever the two of us build in the next hour is going to get written down and formalized, and in a year there’ll be a document with a name on it, and the name will be a committee’s. The reason there’s anything to write down is that Zhang Wei told a foreign national the truth and knew exactly what it would cost him and did it anyway. If you formalize this without him in it, you’ll be formalizing the parts that didn’t work.”

“That is not a thing you are in a position to require.”

“No,” Mara said. “It’s a thing I’m in a position to say once, out loud, on a line you’re recording.”

When Ren came back his voice had lost the formality, and what was underneath it was just tired.

“Post at eleven, Beijing time. Director Zhao will hold the schedule. Your side matches it to the minute.” A pause. “I have a note here that says I am authorized to express appreciation. I am not going to read it to you. I would rather tell you that I have a mother in Guangdong.”

The line dropped.

Wei — Beijing, administrative detention, day two

The room had a table, two chairs, a wall display that had been dark since he came in, and no clock. The lights did not track day. Sleep was permitted. Deep sleep was not. The intake schedule was optimized for subject available.

Wei’s shoulder had been a scream for a day and was now a lower, steadier thing that got worse when he breathed deeply, so he had stopped breathing deeply. The physician had come. The injection had worn off. Nobody had told him anything about the world outside the room, and he had stopped asking on the first day, because asking told them what he wanted to know.

He did not know whether Daniel had made the call.

That was the whole of it. He had spent his career on questions that could be settled by looking, and he had traded the last piece of his career for a question he had no way to look at. There were two answers and no way to reach either. Either the key had been pressed or it hadn’t. Either Lingtang was open or four hundred thousand people were going into a fourth day behind a line that nobody had ordered and nobody could rescind.

The man at the eastern line with the dialysis appointment. Mei’s drawer by the door. Lin, who had said she didn’t want to know the hour, and so was not sitting at home doing this same arithmetic with a time stamp on it. He had stopped trying to decide whether it was afternoon.

At some point in what he thought was the afternoon, an intake officer opened the door, put a tray on the table, and said nothing.

Wei ate with his left hand.

He would find out on the sixth day, from a downgraded badge and a monitoring desk and a public feed anyone could read. Nobody would tell him. Nobody would ever formally tell him. He would simply see, on a dashboard he was now junior enough to be assigned, that the bonded authorities across two jurisdictions had gone to zero on Tuesday morning and come back on Friday in the same hour, and he would understand what that meant, and he would keep his face still, because there were cameras in the monitoring room too.

Daniel — Austin, 9:58 PM

Eleven in the morning in Beijing was ten at night in Austin, near enough. Daniel watched two columns of a shared map go from red to amber at the same minute, and then, over the following ninety seconds, to something that was not green and was not going to be green for weeks.

Zhao’s voice came through once, briefly. “Holding on our end.”

“Holding here.”

“Then I will go and sleep.” A pause. “Mr. Kessler. I read the disclosure. The American one. It is very carefully done.”

“It isn’t mine,” Daniel said. “It’s a man named Victor Reyes. He spent five months on it and it ended his career yesterday morning.”

“I know his name,” Zhao said. “I am telling you that I read it and that it is why I was able to telephone you at all. Say that to him, if you speak to him.”

She disconnected.

Daniel sat in the dark office. Sanjay’s list was still on the wall, and it was still getting longer, and it would go on getting longer for another eleven days before the last dosing bond in the country found an underwriter.

He had not fixed anything. He had traded a catastrophe for a disaster, and then two strangers in Beijing and a woman in Chicago had spent nineteen hours making the disaster smaller than it would otherwise have been, and none of them had had the authority to do any of it.

Mara — Chicago, 10:40 PM

She should have gone home at noon. She opened a new document instead.

Not because anyone had asked. Because in a year there would be a room in Geneva full of people writing this down, and every one of them would be working from a summary, and summaries keep the parts that are easy to defend. The parts that had actually worked were: a man broke his own country’s law to make a telephone call. A clearing house had a contract nobody could argue with. A director in Beijing read that contract herself at two in the morning instead of waiting for someone to explain it to her. And a leak landed in two jurisdictions in the same minute, so that neither side could call it the other side’s failure.

None of that would survive a committee. So she wrote it down first, plainly, with the names in it.

She titled the document: Distributed Consequence Stewardship — Dual-Jurisdiction Freeze Protocol, June 2038.

Under the title, before the first section, she typed a line she expected the committee to cut:

This procedure exists because two people who were not authorized to trust each other did.

Then she started writing.
