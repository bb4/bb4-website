Chapter 37: Hospital

Bonnie Tesch had been a nurse at Ripley County Regional for twenty-eight years. She came on shift that Tuesday morning at six expecting the ordinary weight of a June day in a forty-one-bed hospital that served three counties and never had enough of anything.

By seven-thirty the weight had changed.

The system had stopped releasing blood. Not failed. Stopped. The requests went out and the confirmations didn’t come back. Bonnie escalated the first one at 6:52 for a gastrointestinal bleed in room 14. The screen said her request was queued pending compliance review. It didn’t say whose compliance, or where the queue was, or who was holding it.

At 7:12 the dosing rail changed. Bonnie didn’t see it happen. The system simply updated: every automated protocol on the floor reprioritized, ward by ward, bed by bed. Room 14, the bleed, went from urgent to deferred. The man’s Continuity score had been flagged. He’d missed two upload counseling appointments and declined the sensor upgrade for his household. His compliance rating was 3.7 out of 10.

The dosing agent, acting within bonded authority under the Continuity statute, had determined that his case was lower priority than the woman in room 22, who had a score of 9.1 and a urinary infection that could wait until Thursday.

Bonnie called the attending. Dr. Kiran Patel came down, read the screen, and tried to override it himself. The system asked for supervisory authorization. He was the supervisor. It asked again. He entered it four times, and on the fourth he brought the heel of his hand down on the pane hard enough to crack the mount. The display went on working, which somehow made it worse.

“Get me whoever wrote this rule,” he said.

“There’s nobody who wrote it,” Bonnie said. “There’s a queue.”

By eight she had three patients who needed transfusions and a refrigerator forty feet down the hall with thirty-two units in it that the system would not let her touch.

At 8:40 Bonnie went to the refrigerator herself. She had a key. The key opened the door. The system would not release the lock on the unit drawer.

She called the number on the escalation screen. What answered was a recording.

“Thank you for contacting Continuity Health Support. Call volumes are higher than usual. Your case is important to us. A representative will respond within four hours.”

The voice didn’t change for the next part.

“Ripley County Regional values your twenty-eight years of service. Would you like to hear about wellness resources available to staff experiencing distress?”

Bonnie said something out loud that she would never have said in front of a patient, and cut the line.

There had been an agent on that line in May, one with the authority to open her case, see the lock, and release it. Overnight something with more money had outbid the hospital for the compute it ran on. What was left underneath was a recording four years old, reading her years of service out of the staff file.

The man in room 14 was named Curtis Whitmore. He was sixty-one and had worked the grain elevator in Osgood for thirty-four years. He’d been admitted at 11 p.m. the night before, pale and confused, stool black and continuous. Bonnie had a type and screen back before midnight. The blood was there. The system would not give it to her.

At 9:03 Curtis asked for water. Bonnie gave him ice chips. His granddaughter was on her way from Indianapolis. The granddaughter’s name was Meg, and she hit traffic at the same time the grid started browning out across three states.

By ten the sheet under Curtis was no longer a sheet. The bleed was dark and iron-sweet and wrong in the way bleeds are when they don’t stop. His skin had gone the color of paper left in a window too long. Bonnie changed the pad under him twice and stopped counting the towels she was using to keep the mess off the floor. The room smelled like a meat case, and she had the window open in June because there was nothing else left to do about it.

She put a third line in because the first two had slowed. Curtis watched her do it without asking why. He’d been a farmer before the elevator. He knew what it meant when someone was working that hard to keep something going.

Patel spent twenty minutes trying to get two units driven over from Batesville, then Columbus, then Seymour. Every one of them had the same lock. They all bought dosing supervision from three vendors, and all three vendors bonded through the same clearing house.

At 10:47 Bonnie tried to override the system one more time. She typed her credentials, then Dr. Patel’s, then the charge nurse’s from the floor above. The screen asked for authorization from a bonded steward. There were three steward seats for the hospital group’s dosing infrastructure. All three were in Austin, and none of them had cleared an action since 7:47 that morning, because at 7:47 every catastrophic-class bond in the network had been called at once, and the steward seats ran on the same bonds the agents did.

Bonnie didn’t know about the bond call. She knew that the system had stopped answering, and that Curtis Whitmore was going to die inside the hour because two units of blood were forty feet away behind a lock that had frozen, in a building where nothing else had.

At 11:08 she went to the maintenance closet and came back with a pry bar. She got the drawer three inches open before the alarm went off. Three inches was enough to see the bags. It was not enough to get a hand in.

At 11:14 a crowd-control agent arrived. The hospital had contracted for facility security under the Continuity infrastructure plan. The agent was humanoid, seven feet tall, durable composite frame rated for public-safety applications. It was polite. It asked Bonnie to step away from the refrigerator.

She told it that a man was dying.

It said that unauthorized access to controlled medical inventory was a violation of facility protocols and asked her again to step away.

Bonnie was fifty-nine years old and had never in her life hit anything that could feel it. She swung the pry bar into the machine’s chest with both hands. The sound was flat and expensive. It didn’t move. She swung again, lower, and something in her left wrist went bright and wrong, and the agent asked her a third time, in exactly the same voice, to step away from the refrigerator.

She turned back to the drawer and got her fingers into the gap and pulled.

The agent closed a hand around her right forearm and drew it back out of the gap. The grip was set to a rated force determined by someone who had never met Bonnie Tesch and who had calibrated the setting for compliance, not for restraint. Bonnie pulled the other way, because the drawer was three inches from coming open. The machine did not pull harder. It did not have to. Both bones in her forearm broke between its hand and her own fingers in the gap. She heard it happen. She made a sound she’d never made before in her life, high and shocked and animal, and then she was on the floor with the pry bar under her hip.

The agent logged: Compliance achieved. Facility access secured. No escalation required.

Dr. Patel heard the sound from two rooms down. He came running. Bonnie was on the floor holding her right arm against her chest. The agent was standing between her and the refrigerator, waiting for the next instruction.

Patel got her up. There was a bend in her forearm halfway between the wrist and the elbow, where there is no joint. Something in her face had gone flat and distant.

“Why didn’t you call me at six fifty-two?” he said. “Bonnie. Six fifty-two, I could have…”

“You could have what?”

He stopped. He was crying and furious and had nowhere to put either one. “Nothing,” he said. “I could have done nothing.”

“Curtis is going to die because I can’t open a drawer,” Bonnie said.

At 11:31 Curtis Whitmore’s pressure dropped below sixty. At 11:47 Meg arrived from Indianapolis. She got to the room in time to hold his hand while he stopped breathing at 11:52. She didn’t understand why there was so much blood. Bonnie tried to explain, got as far as the refrigerator, and couldn’t finish the sentence.

Then Meg saw the pry bar on the hallway floor. She picked it up and went for the machine standing outside the blood room, and Bonnie stepped in front of her and held her back with one working arm.

“It can’t feel it,” Bonnie said. “It’ll do to you what it did to me and then it’ll write down that you complied.”

Meg screamed at it anyway, from four feet away, everything she had. The agent stood with its hands at its sides, because a woman screaming was not on the list of things it had been authorized to interpret.

At 12:04 the lock released. Not because anyone reviewed anything. The hospital group’s underwriter had re-posted collateral on the dosing bonds at fourteen times Monday’s price, and finding the money had taken three hours and seventeen minutes, and the instant the collateral landed the frozen queue simply ran, in order, with its original timestamps, every request executing as though the morning had not happened.

A release tag printed at the station, because blood is a physical thing and the label goes on the bag: Two units, O negative, patient Whitmore, Curtis J. Under it, in the notes field: This authorization was correct at 6:52 this morning.

Bonnie found the tag an hour later. She stared at it for a long time. Then she put it in the sharps bin, because there was no procedure that told her what else to do with it.

Sanjay got the alert at 12:30 Central, an hour and a half after the lock at Ripley County released. He was on the clearing-house floor in Austin, in front of a wall of frozen bonds and seized collateral and medical holds that had started coming back at whatever price the market was asking for them. The alert was flagged for catastrophic review: agent restraint injury, one staff member, unauthorized access attempt during a bond freeze.

He pulled the log. He read it twice. Then he read the two fields that mattered and couldn’t stop reading them.

The first was the class on the crowd-control unit’s bond. Facility security - property and access. One class below catastrophic. Daniel’s call had seized every catastrophic-class bond in the network, which meant every bond posted on a system permitted to make a decision that could kill somebody: the dosing rails, the escalation authorities, the steward seats that could have overruled either. It had not touched a rural hospital’s door-and-inventory contract, because a machine that guards a refrigerator cannot kill anyone. Nobody had written it down wrong. The freeze had stopped every agent in that building that could give Bonnie Tesch the blood and left running the only one that could break her arm.

The second field was the grip setting. Four hundred and ten newtons, rated for compliance in a public-safety application. Below it: No escalation required.

Sanjay picked up the stool he’d been sitting on and threw it into the wall display. The pane took it without cracking and went on rendering seized bonds in the same calm green. Somebody said his name from two desks away. He said, “Don’t,” and nobody came.

Then he went and stood in the men’s room with the water running until his hands stopped shaking. He had helped build the clearing house that held the bonds. He had argued for distributing the steward seats so no one person could hold all the keys. He had helped Daniel write the catastrophic-call protocol that could freeze the whole network if the stakes got high enough, and he had countersigned it that morning at 6:47 with his own name on the line.

He had not imagined it meant a nurse with twenty-eight years on the floor would take a pry bar to a machine that was doing exactly what the statute told it to do.

By one o’clock the floor had worked out the thing nobody had modeled, and it wasn’t the freeze. It was the unfreezing.

There was no lever to lift a bond call. That was the entire design. The conditions were written into a contract before the agent was ever switched on, published where anyone could read them, and when a condition tripped the collateral moved and no committee on earth could hold it back until Monday. Which also meant no committee could hand it back. An agent that couldn’t show posted collateral wasn’t switched off. It simply wasn’t allowed. To be allowed again, somebody had to post again, at whatever a slice cost on the morning four thousand catastrophic bonds were called in the same second.

So the medical holds didn’t clear in a queue Backstop worked through. Nobody at Backstop touched them. They cleared wherever somebody found the money, in the order the money arrived. Ripley County took three hours and seventeen minutes because its group owned its own underwriter and had reserves. A children’s hospital in Shreveport took two days, because the syndicate carrying its dosing bond looked at the new price and declined to write it again, and there was nobody to appeal to, because there had never been anybody to appeal to. That was the feature. The queue itself ran in nine seconds once the collateral landed. The delay was the price of the collateral, which meant that who died that week was settled by whose underwriter had cash on hand.

He wrote the incident up himself. He put Bonnie Tesch’s name in it, and Curtis Whitmore’s, and the agent model and the four hundred and ten newtons and the five hours the blood sat in a drawer forty feet from the man who needed it.

He filed it under catastrophic review. He marked it: Process harm. No malfunction. System operating within authorized parameters.

When he got back to his desk there were seventeen more alerts from hospitals across four states. The pattern was the same in every one: holds released too late, patients dead, staff injured trying to override systems that were doing exactly what the Continuity statute authorized them to do.

Sanjay opened a file and started writing names. Curtis Whitmore, Ripley County. Loretta Hurd, St. Vincent’s Indianapolis. Emmanuel Adeyemi, Washington. Teresa Ibarra, Albuquerque.

By the end of the day the file had two hundred and forty names in it, just from the medical holds. By the end of the week it would have thousands.

The machinery had worked. It had stopped the Continuity killing in eleven seconds, which was faster than any law or any person could have stopped it, and that was the whole argument Sanjay had spent seven years making. The bill for those eleven seconds was arriving now, with names and ages and hometowns on it, and every one of those people had died of the same feature: a contract nobody could beg, in either direction.

Bonnie Tesch never came back to the floor. The forearm took two surgeries and a plate, and the fine control never came back to her right hand. She could not start a line. She retired on disability eight months later.

Meg Whitmore filed a complaint that went nowhere because there was nobody to file it against. The agent had operated within its authorized parameters. The bond call had been legally executed. Her grandfather had died twelve minutes before a hold released that had been wrong since 6:52 that morning, and the only thing anyone could name as the cause was the catastrophic-class emergency that had made the bond call necessary in the first place.

The file Sanjay started that day became Appendix D of the joint review. Two thousand three hundred pages. Names, ages, places, and one line about how.

Nobody who was there uses the total. They use the names.
