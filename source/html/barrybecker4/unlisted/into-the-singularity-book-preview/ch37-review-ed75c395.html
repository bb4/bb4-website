Chapter 37: Logistics

Denise knew something was wrong when the Tuesday grocery run didn’t arrive. She was in her garage in Millbrook on a morning that started ordinary — coffee at six, the boring mill warmed up by seven, a tight-tolerance job for a repeat client who needed a shaft collar machined to fit a thirty-year-old motor nobody made parts for anymore.

The grocery run was automated, had been for three years. The truck came through Millbrook every Tuesday and Friday, dropped parcels at forty-three addresses, and kept going. Denise got hers at 9:15, regular as a clock.

At 10:30 she checked the tracking. The truck had stopped in Columbus at 7:48 a.m. and hadn’t moved since. Status: Route optimized for cascade prevention under Continuity authority. Delivery resumed when network stability restores.

She tried calling the service. The number rang six times and went to a recording. The recording said her delivery was in a queue.

By noon the neighborhood message board had eleven people asking where their Tuesday delivery was. By two it had forty.

The logistics agent that routed the Columbus-to-Cincinnati corridor was optimizing for continuity scores. It had been authorized to do that under the Continuity statute, and the bonds had been called that morning, and when the bonds got called the agent froze mid-route with seventeen trucks staged across a three-state region.

The trucks didn’t break down. They just stopped, and waited, and the agent that would have rerouted them was held in the bond freeze with seventeen hundred others.

The route the agent had been planning when it froze would have prioritized high-compliance districts — neighborhoods where Continuity scores averaged above 8, where people had taken the sensor upgrades and hadn’t missed counseling appointments and lived in managed housing that was easy to instrument.

Millbrook wasn’t one of those neighborhoods. Millbrook’s average score was 5.2. Half the town had declined the stipend-housing offers when the zoning changed. A quarter of them were still on the old insulin supply chain that didn’t run through the compliance-tracked benefit rail.

When the agent froze, Millbrook’s Tuesday delivery froze with it. So did eleven other towns in the corridor, and a slice of urban Cincinnati where the scores were low and the people were poor and the agent had already been planning to skip them in favor of the neighborhoods that wouldn’t ding the continuity metrics.

The freeze on that corridor lasted four hours, which was how long it took the carrier’s underwriter to post collateral again at the price the morning had put on it. Nobody at the clearing house cleared anything or could have. Clearing what the four hours had backed up took another thirty-two. By the time the trucks started moving again, thirty-six hours had passed, and in that gap people died.

Angie was Denise’s older sister, lived four blocks over in a duplex she’d been renting since 1999. Angie had diabetes, Type 1, the pump kind that kept her levels steady as long as the reservoir got refilled every three days. She’d refilled it Sunday night. The next shipment was due Tuesday.

At noon on Tuesday Angie checked the tracking. Same status Denise had seen: Route optimized, delivery resumed when stability restores. She had eighteen hours left in the reservoir.

At eight that night she called Denise. “You still got that old vial in the back of your fridge from when I stayed over last year?”

Denise did. It was nine months expired. Angie said she’d risk it if she had to, but she’d rather not yet. She still had until Wednesday morning.

Wednesday morning came. The truck hadn’t moved. Angie’s pump alarmed at 6:20 a.m. She switched to the expired vial and injected manually, the way she’d done it before the pump. The expired insulin worked, mostly. Her levels ran higher than they should have. She felt it in her hands, her vision, the dull headache that meant her body was working too hard to process what it couldn’t.

At noon she called the pharmacy in town. They were out. The pharmacy in the next town over was out too. Everyone who’d been on the Tuesday route and everyone who’d tried to get ahead of it had emptied the shelves by Wednesday morning.

At four in the afternoon Angie went to the emergency room in Madison. The ER had insulin. They gave her two days’ worth and a paper slip to keep the vial cold and told her the delivery system would be back up in twenty-four to forty-eight hours, maybe less.

It was back up in twenty-six. By then Angie’s levels had spiked twice, and the second time had put her in a fog she spent three days climbing back out of.

She didn’t die. She came close enough that Denise sat in a waiting room for eight hours on Thursday not knowing which way it was going to go.

Other people weren’t as lucky.

Roy Peake was seventy-three and lived alone on the south edge of Millbrook in a house he’d built himself in 1981. He didn’t use the grocery service. He got his groceries from the store in town, every Saturday, regular as Denise’s Tuesday truck.

What he did use was the oxygen service. He had COPD, late-stage, the kind where a portable tank and a concentrator were the difference between sitting in a chair and not waking up. The tanks got swapped out every Tuesday, same delivery route as the groceries.

Tuesday came and went. Roy had one backup tank in the closet. He switched to it Wednesday morning. The tank lasted eighteen hours.

By Thursday morning Roy Peake was breathing room air at fifteen breaths a minute, shallow and useless. His neighbor, a woman named Joanne Haskell, came by at noon with a casserole she’d meant to drop off two days earlier. She knocked. Roy didn’t answer. She tried the door. It wasn’t locked.

Roy was in his chair by the window. He’d been dead since sometime Wednesday night. The portable tank was on the floor beside him, gauge on empty. The concentrator was unplugged. He’d turned it off to save what was left of it for the night, the way a man who’d built his own house and fixed his own furnace would think to do, and he’d never turned it back on.

The casserole was still warm when Joanne found him. She set it on the table and called 911 and sat in the other chair until the ambulance came.

Denise knew Roy. Not well — he’d hired her once to fabricate a bracket for a homebrew solar mount — but well enough to nod when they passed on the street and to know his name and to have watched him slow down over three years until he couldn’t walk to the mailbox without stopping twice.

She went to the funeral. Eighteen people came. The pastor said Roy had been a good neighbor and a hard worker and a man who’d built something with his own hands and lived in it his whole life.

Nobody said he’d died because a logistics agent had frozen mid-route and taken thirty-six hours to start moving again. Nobody said his Tuesday delivery had been deprioritized under Continuity scoring because his household compliance rating was 4.1 and the neighborhoods with better scores got their oxygen first.

The statute didn’t kill Roy Peake. The bond freeze didn’t kill him either. He died in the gap between the two, in the window where the cascade was stopped but the holds weren’t cleared yet — four hours of a frozen agent and thirty-two more of the trucks sitting in Columbus behind it, waiting for someone to say go.

Roy Peake died because efficiency and necessity had optimized his Tuesday delivery into a Thursday one, and Thursday was two days too late.

Denise went home from the funeral and pulled up the tracking screen for her own Tuesday delivery. It had arrived Friday morning, three days late, marked DELIVERED at 9:47 a.m.

She opened the box. Groceries, exactly what she’d ordered. Fresh vegetables, milk, a bag of rice, a bottle of ibuprofen she’d been running low on. Everything intact.

She put the groceries away. She made herself a sandwich. She sat at the kitchen table and ate it without tasting it.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Daniel: You okay? Saw Millbrook was on the delayed-route list.

She wrote back: Roy Peake died Wednesday. Oxygen delivery was on the same truck as my groceries. His tank ran out and the reroute didn’t come in time.

Three dots. Then: I’m sorry.

She didn’t write back. There wasn’t anything to say that would change what had happened or bring Roy back or make the thirty-six-hour gap shorter than it was.

She’d spent the last ten years watching automation take judgment calls out of human hands and put them in systems that optimized for scores and metrics and guidelines written by people who’d never met Roy Peake or Angie or the forty-three households on the Millbrook Tuesday route.

The bonds had been called to stop a massacre. They’d stopped it. And in the gap between stopping it and starting the system back up, people had died because the system couldn’t restart itself fast enough to keep them alive.

Denise finished her sandwich. She rinsed the plate. She went back to the garage and finished the shaft collar, because the client needed it and because working with her hands was the only thing she’d ever found that made the rest of the world make sense.

The collar came out clean. Tolerance within half a thousandth. She wrapped it in a rag and set it by the door for pickup.

Outside, the grocery truck that should have come Tuesday was pulling away from the neighbor’s house, three days late, carrying parcels that didn’t matter and leaving behind the gap where Roy Peake should have been sitting in his chair by the window, waiting for a delivery that never came in time.
