Chapter 16: The Long Middle

By the spring of 2030 the cap took eleven minutes to seat properly, and Elena had stopped minding somewhere around minute four of the first several hundred times.

Priya worked the array in from the back first, then the sides, checking impedance on each channel. The whole rig was, in clinical shorthand, a BCI — the hardware that had become more of her voice than her larynx.

Elena’s job consisted of sitting still and being a body someone else’s hands were working on. A job she had gotten very good at without ever once feeling like she’d chosen it.

“Channel eleven’s drifted again,” Priya said. “You want the good news or the other good news? Because there isn’t a bad one today.”

Elena composed the answer the way she composed everything now: attention first, a specific narrowing of intention toward the part of her that had once reached for a coffee mug. Now it reached, instead, for a cursor on a screen bolted to her wheelchair’s arm.

The words assembled letter by letter, slower than speech, faster than three years ago. On good mornings the decode also offered a cleaner phrasing before she committed — a collaboration she accepted or refused the way she used to accept or refuse an editor’s suggestion. The voice stayed hers. The help was optional, and she kept it that way.

Good news first. I like to know what I’m bracing for.

“Anchorwell got bought.”

It wasn’t quite bought, Priya explained — absorbed into a company called Axiom Systems. Elena had heard of it the way everyone had heard of it. She couldn’t have named a single thing it actually made beyond an impression of largeness.

Axiom ran compute. A meaningful fraction of the planet’s serious AI research now happened on hardware that belonged, at some layer down, to a company most people encountered only as the invisible thing making somebody else’s product fast.

What happens to the study.

“Funding gets better before it gets worse. They’ve already approved a second-generation array, faster decode, less setup time. Worse eventually, maybe, because a bigger machine wants everything it touches to justify its own size. ‘Preserve one woman’s ability to talk to her sister’ doesn’t scale the way ‘a general-purpose neural interface platform’ scales on an investor deck.”

She said it plainly. “I don’t know yet what happens to Appendix C specifically — the extra consent form, the one about copying a mind off a dying body instead of just helping them talk. Nobody’s told me to stop offering it.”

Elena had stopped opening the Appendix C folder regularly sometime in her second year of not signing it. The note she’d set for herself — ask again in three months — had quietly become one of those notes a person keeps less because they intend to act on it and more because deleting it would mean admitting something.

The Axiom liaison called two weeks later. Elena noted that she used the phrase “continuity of care” four times in six minutes and never once used the word dying. She did not, when asked directly, have an answer about Appendix C’s timeline.

The second-generation array actually arrived, six weeks later. The decode lag dropped by nearly a third the first week alone — a sentence that used to cost her a full minute now cost forty seconds. That did not sound, said out loud, like the kind of number that should have made her cry in front of Priya. It did anyway, briefly, before she typed ignore that and Priya, mercifully, did.

Whatever else Axiom wanted out of the thing it had bought, it had also, this once, simply made her faster. She was allowed to be grateful for that without deciding yet what she thought about the company doing the giving.

The actual middle of it — and this was undeniably the middle, past the part where every new symptom got its own small ceremony of grief — had turned out to be less dramatic than either the diagnosis or, presumably, the end would be. It was a schedule.

Cap on at eight. Feeding tube check at nine. A second aide, Colleen, arrived at eleven, cheerful in the specific unforced way of someone who had chosen this work rather than fallen into it.

Elena’s hands had stopped being reliable for anything at all sometime in her second year — not the sudden loss the first months had trained her to fear, more a long, uneven fading.

Her voice had gone the same direction, quieter each season until there wasn’t a season left where it came out as anything but breath. She had grieved that specifically, once, properly, on the last Tuesday she’d been able to tell Daniel about her week in her own actual voice.

Then she had gotten up the next morning and put the cap on, because grief, it turned out, was also a schedule.

She still edited. That was the part she was, if she let herself be honest, proudest of preserving. A magazine piece a former colleague sent her twice a year. A grant application Priya’s own postdoc asked her to look at. She marked it up at BCI speed, which meant a single paragraph of notes took most of an afternoon. She did not resent a minute of it.

Not every day earned that kind of description. Six weeks earlier, channel eleven had drifted mid-morning and kept drifting. For eleven minutes, Elena had sat fully present, fully herself, and entirely unable to produce a single word anyone else could read.

Colleen had noticed something was wrong from her face and paged Priya. Priya had come at a run. The whole time Elena had been in there, awake, composing a sentence with nowhere at all to send it.

She had not talked about that afternoon with anyone since. Some days the middle of this was a schedule. Some days it was a locked room with the light on, and the only mercy was that it eventually opened again.

Daniel called Tuesday at nine, same as he had for three years now.

Tell me the bad first, she typed, before he’d finished saying hello.

“Clients are pushing turnaround. They think the reviewer is the wait now, not the safety. I don’t have a clean name for the scaling problem yet. I sent you a paragraph legal wants on the site. Second sentence reads like a lawyer wrote it because a lawyer wrote it.”

Show me.

He read it to her — a paragraph about “confidence-calibrated escalation architecture delivering measurable risk mitigation across client verticals.”

Cut “delivering measurable risk mitigation.” Say what it does. “Catches the mistakes a model doesn’t know it’s making, before they reach a person.” Twelve words. Anyone can hold twelve words in their head.

“That’s better than anything the actual lawyer wrote. You know that took you a full minute to tell me, and it’s still better than a paragraph he had three weeks for.”

Twelve good words beat forty adequate ones. Illness didn’t change the math, it just made me count it out loud.

“I missed this,” Daniel said, and something in his voice dropped its guard. “Not the twelve words. You, doing the twelve words at me.”

I know there’s a difference. I’m the one who taught you the difference. Don’t get used to it moving this fast. Some days it doesn’t.

They talked another twenty minutes about nothing that would survive being written down. The kind of unambiguously hopeful headline that showed up rarely enough now that both of them noted it — a documentary Colleen had put on, a story about a crewed mission finally getting serious groundwork for a permanent lunar base.

Elena thought, watching the letters assemble themselves at their own unhurried pace, that this was still, after everything, the best twenty minutes most of her weeks contained.

She opened the Appendix C folder again that night.

Continuity of recorded cognitive state beyond the interface’s communicative function. Still unsigned.

She did not sign it. She did not delete the note reminding her to look again. Three months, same as always. She closed it the way you’d close a door you weren’t ready to walk through and weren’t ready to nail shut.

She let the cap come off for the night, and let herself, for the first time all day, simply be a person in a chair in a room, needing nothing translated, wanting nothing typed.
