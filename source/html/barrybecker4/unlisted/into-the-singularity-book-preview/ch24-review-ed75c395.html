Chapter 24: Second Hit

“Pull them out. You’re going to need them.”

The kid standing next to Warren’s boring-and-facing machine had his hands in his pockets the way people did when they wanted to touch something and had been told not to yet. Janelle had bolted the machine to a pad she’d poured herself, years earlier. The garage smelled the way a real shop smelled: coolant, old oil, the metal-dust that settled in the corners no matter how carefully you swept.

His name was Caleb. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. His father ran a small fab outfit two towns over that still sent Janelle the jobs TruePath’s cousins couldn’t finish clean: tight-tolerance bushings, hand-fit sleeves, the kind of one-off repair where the drawing lied and somebody’s fingers had to find the truth. Caleb had asked, three Saturdays running, if he could watch. This morning his father had texted: He’s going to ask to try. Say no if you want.

Janelle had said yes before she’d finished reading.

“Feel that,” she said, guiding his palm to the ways, the long ground tracks the carriage slides on. “Machine tells you when it’s happy. Not in words. In how the cut sounds and how the bar sits when you take your hand off. If you rush the setup to get to the cut, the cut will tell on you later.”

She walked him through the boring bar the way she’d walked herself through it a thousand times. Seat, indicate, listen for the harmonic that meant the bar was singing wrong.

“Here. Don’t force it. You’re listening with your fingers. If you push past what the metal wants to tell you, you’ll get a number that looks fine and a part that fails in the field.”

He got it on the third try, the small flinch of a person who had felt the soft place and recognized it as information.

“Again. Slower this time.”

Weekdays she did the other version of the same work. The weird ones, the kickbacks, the jobs a print still lied about. The garage paid less than Backstop had. She ate anyway. Fridays she still moved money into the wallet app before she let herself think about groceries, the same faith in a dead man it had been when she still had a paycheck.

It hadn’t been thirty dollars in two years. She’d raised it twice after the machine check went permanent, sixty, then a hundred and twenty, and both times the raise was stale by the time she’d gotten used to it. So she quit chasing the dollar with round numbers and made it a share instead: a fixed slice off the top of every invoice, before tax, before groceries, whatever the invoice happened to say. It was the difference between a dimension and a ratio, and she’d spent her working life on the side of the shop that knew which one held when the material moved. She still called it the thirty dollars. Eleven years of calling it that had worn the name in.

She had finally let herself look at the cumulative number the year inflation made looking feel less like greed. It was eleven years of Fridays that had cost her something every week and had never once been leveraged into anything she couldn’t afford to lose.

The email came on a Monday in April of 2035.

Janelle, we’ve appreciated the bushing work more than I can say. Price and turnaround on the new contractor are hard to ignore for the volume we’re looking at this quarter. Happy to keep the specialty one-offs with you if that still works. - Glen

Glen was Caleb’s father. The specialty one-offs were the jobs that didn’t pay enough to keep a garage warm through February. The volume was the reason she’d poured the pad and bolted the machine down and taught his kid the soft place in a fit.

She recognized every phrase from the year TruePath had taken the deciding out of Kessler. What was leaving was the thing she’d built the shop around as the one refuge the software couldn’t touch: a person’s hands, standing where a body stood, doing the fit a dial called fine and a finger called wrong.

She texted Glen back. Understood. Come by if you want me to walk the new shop through the soft-fit notes on the last batch. No charge. I’d rather the parts don’t fail because somebody skipped the hand pass.

His reply took twenty minutes. They’re doing a floor demo Thursday in Columbus. You should see it. I mean that as respect, not a sales pitch.

She almost said no. She drove.

The contractor’s floor was cleaner than any shop Janelle had ever worked in. White epoxy. Overhead LEDs that didn’t leave a shadow where you needed one. A row of humanoid units along the far wall in a charging cradle that looked like a locker room designed by somebody who’d never sweat.

“They’re running the bushing cell at two,” Glen said. “I asked them to use the same print you’ve been working from.”

The unit that stepped off the cradle moved the way a careful person moved when they knew they were being watched. Two arms. Hands with articulated fingers that closed on a fixture the way hers closed on a fixture.

It indicated the part. It seated the boring approach. It paused, for a fraction of a second that looked almost like listening, and then it found the soft place.

Janelle watched its fingers do the thing she had taught Caleb three Saturdays earlier. Not a camera on an arm. Not a toolpath generated from a print. A body, manufactured, standing where a body stood, fitting what her hands fitted. The dial on the screen called it in-tolerance. The hand, the machine’s hand, had already known.

“Cycle time’s about sixty percent of yours,” Glen said. “Reject rate’s lower on the last hundred. And they’re bonded. Backstop Bond. If a unit wrecks a batch, somebody’s coins pay for it. The insurance guy made that the whole pitch.”

“They’re right. That’s the point of bringing me.”

She watched a second unit deburr a mating face with a motion so close to Marisol’s old station rhythm that she had to look at the wall for a second.

She asked the floor lead, a tired woman named Keisha, whether the soft-fit pass was programmed or learned.

“Both. Base model comes with the dexterity stack. The fit intuition’s trained off recorded human passes, anonymized shop footage, mostly. Yours might be in there, for all I know. No way to tell. You want to put a hand on one? Some of the old machinists like to, once.”

She understood, all at once, that the question she’d been living inside since TruePath was the wrong question. She was still employable. Backstop proved that every morning. What she was watching was not a verdict on her wage. It was a body built to do what her body did. The refuge hadn’t been a refuge. It had been a pause.

“I’m good. Thanks for the demo.”

Glen walked her to the parking lot.

“I’ll still send the weird ones. The ones the cell kicks back.”

“Send them. I’ll do them.”

The radio on the drive back had three stories rotating. Autonomous freight had crossed ninety percent on the interstates that mattered. Fusion was contributing a real one percent to the grid somewhere out west. Between freight and fusion, a local spot reminded listeners the machine check hit accounts on the first.

She stopped at Kessler on the way home. The lot had Ray’s truck and nothing else that counted as a person. He came out of the office, not the floor, wiping a tablet instead of his hands.

“You saw one.”

“I saw one.” She nodded at the dark windows. “Yours are bonded through him too. I saw the logo on Glen’s contractor packet.”

Ray didn’t insult her by asking who him was. “You still got work.”

“The weird ones Glen can’t feed the cell yet. And the thirty dollars on Friday.”

“That enough.”

“It’s enough to eat. I’m asking a different question now.”

Ray didn’t ask what the question was. He had known Warren too long to need the translation.

Caleb was in the garage when she got home, sitting on the overturned bucket he’d claimed as a stool, waiting with the stubbornness of a kid who had decided a Saturday lesson wasn’t canceled by a Monday email.

“You still want to do the soft place.”

He looked at her like the question was a test. “Dad said you might not want company.”

“Your dad’s polite. I’m not canceling.” She hung her jacket, put her hands on the ways the way she always did first. “Come here. Slower than last time. You’re listening with your fingers.”

He came. She put his hand where hers had been. The soft place was still there, metal telling the truth to anyone patient enough to hear it. She had looked the replacement in the face. She was still here.

“Again. The machine isn’t going anywhere.”
