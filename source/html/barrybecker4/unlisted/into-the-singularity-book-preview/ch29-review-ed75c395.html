Chapter 29: Smaller, on Purpose

Mara had worked out of the same two rooms since 2033, three floors up in a Ravenswood building that had sold pianos for most of the last century. The showroom was the ground floor. Her floor was where they rebuilt the uprights, which had gone up and down the outside of the building on a hoist, through a door cut in the back wall. The door was still there, painted over a dozen times, opening onto a thirty-foot drop and nothing else.

Six years earlier she had worked forty floors above the Loop — the downtown blocks inside the ring of elevated track, where Chicago keeps its money and always has. A desk up there was the thing she’d been aiming at since she was nineteen. She had made a private rule, moving into Ravenswood, not to compare the two, and had broken it roughly once a month ever since.

What she sold now was not a book and not a tip. Half her clients still wanted the old conversation — borrow against your coins, buy more coins. The other half wanted her to price agent bonds.

A bond was bitcoin posted in advance against being wrong. Call it and the system’s authority froze; the collateral went to whoever the harm landed on — no committee, no Monday. Catastrophic classes took the largest slices. Mara priced the fee in the middle: what you collected monthly for standing behind a loss that size, and what you actually stood to lose.

She was better at that conversation than the old one. She had been the old one once, from the client’s chair, and that was how she knew how it ended.

Dale Ferrante sat across from her at eleven in the morning, in February of 2038 — sixty-one, four years out from selling a plumbing supply business his father had started. He held two and a fraction bitcoin he’d bought in pieces since 2028 and had never borrowed against.

At a little over thirteen million dollars a coin, that made him on paper a man who never needed to work again and had never entirely believed it.

“The desk in Naperville says thirty percent loan-to-value,” he said. “Regulated custodian, statement every week. It takes deposits, it makes loans, it sends statements. That’s a bank with a different word on the door. Everybody I know is doing some version of it.”

“Thirty percent means they lend you thirty cents on every dollar of coin and sell you out if the price falls far enough. A bank can’t take your house because the neighborhood got cheaper. That desk can, and it doesn’t call first.”

“So tell me why not. The honest version.”

“You’re paying me for the honest one. You can get the polite one free anywhere.”

She turned her monitor around. She’d known what the meeting was from the summary his digital assistant sent hers, and had already built him the table: loan sizes down one side, and along the top, the price at which each one got his coin sold out from under him.

“At thirty percent you can survive a seventy-percent drop. That’s a real cushion. If you took exactly that loan today and never touched it again, I’d sign off.”

“But.”

“But you won’t leave it there. Nobody does. Thirty works, then forty is convenient, then fifty. The number that gets you is the fourth one — the one you never actually decided.”

Ferrante looked at the table. “You’re describing somebody specific.”

“I’m describing me. In 2027 I borrowed forty percent of what my holdings were worth through a friend who did the math correctly. By 2032 I’d taken it to twice that. The price fell through my line on a Wednesday morning and sold me out inside an hour. The friend had said the number out loud five months earlier and I’d written it down.”

She turned the monitor back. “So weigh it against the fact that I’m the last person who ought to be saying it — or against the fact that I’m the only one in this building who’s watched it happen from inside. I’d take the second one.”

He didn’t take the loan. He also didn’t entirely take her point — she could see him filing it under she got burned rather than she’s right. The filing usually got corrected later, by the market, at a much worse price than her fee.

He shook her hand and said he’d think about it, which meant he’d call the desk in Naperville by the end of the week. She’d take halfway.

Kira Ostrowski waited until the elevator closed. Twenty-six, eighteen months into the job, and dependable about asking the obvious question long after everyone else in a room had quietly agreed it was too late to ask — why do we believe that number? — which was most of why Mara had hired her.

“The Continuity packet from overnight,” Kira said. “Three city governments posted catastrophic-class bonds through Backstop inside the same forty hours. Emergency dispatch, crowd control, grid allocation.”

“I saw the filings.”

“Did you see all three published the same contract? Same conditions, word for word — the same list of what counts as harm bad enough to seize the money. Same floor on the Continuity score, so each agent keeps its authority as long as its stability number holds above eight. Same statute cited underneath.” Kira put it on the wall. “One at a time, each of them prices fine. Side by side, they aren’t three bets. They’re one bet sold three times.”

“Do we warn anyone?”

“We warn the client paying us to price a slice of the Denver one. He collects a fee every month for promising to cover that loss and he’s out fourteen million the day it’s called. This is what could take his money.” Mara closed the packet. “We do not call my brother and tell him his rails are about to become one bomb with three addresses. Not randomly, with a fee invoice, and nothing but a pattern.” She looked at the wall a moment longer. “Write the correlation into the memo anyway. Plain words. If I’m wrong it’s a paragraph. If I’m right, somebody’s going to need that paragraph in a hurry.”

Kira nodded once. She was already drafting.

“And Ferrante,” Kira said. “Why not just structure the loan for him? Twenty-five percent, two signatures, a custodian who won’t process an increase on one phone call.”

“Because a structure only holds while the person inside it wants it to. Every human constraint has an off switch, and the off switch is a phone call on a good day.” She started closing windows. “Agent bonds don’t have that weakness. You can’t call the contract and ask it for a week. That’s the part everyone finds comforting and it’s the reason this frightens me — the code does exactly what it was told, on the dot, in three cities at once. All the safety sits in what somebody wrote down beforehand. And the conditions look airtight until three cities write identical ones and all three agents get graded by the same scoring model. Green means the same thing in Denver as it means in Phoenix — unrest low, essential services running, nothing visibly broken. Three greens isn’t three opinions. It’s one opinion printed three times, and whatever it’s built not to notice, it misses everywhere in the same second.”

She shut the last pane. “I had a covenant too. Mine was called I’ll stop at one and a half.”

Farid’s assistant reached hers in March and asked for twenty minutes. Hers offered Thursday, and the two of them traded calendars and context the ordinary way, so that each principal would be handed a time and a one-line summary and neither would have to negotiate anything out loud. Then, a minute later, his assistant asked whether now was possible instead.

That part was Farid. Assistants didn’t get impatient. Five and a half years since he’d last spoken to her, and she took it live, and noticed with irritation that she’d taken it live.

He asked how she was. She said fine. He said tired, which from Farid was closer to disclosure than most people’s confessions. Then he stopped doing the part of the call that was manners.

“The partners are rebuilding the house risk framework from the studs. Not a memo — the actual governing document, the one compliance runs off and regulators read first. The last real rewrite was 2024.

“Half of what we manage now sits in agent collateral the document treats as a footnote next to the old crypto margin language. Continuity clients post through Backstop the way they pay a utility bill — monthly, automatic, nobody reads the terms. The treaty people will start asking questions before the year’s out. I’d rather have the answer already written than be drafting one in a hearing room.” A pause. “I want you to write it.”

Mara looked at the freight door that opened onto nothing.

“Say the actual role, Farid. Not the project.”

“Director of risk framework. Bond and agent collateral at the center of it. Reports to me. No client book, no seat on the floor, no share of the profits. You’d have Legal, compliance, and two analysts, and you’d spend the first year mostly reading.”

He said it flatly, without padding. “It is not the seat you had, and I’m not going to dress it up. About forty percent of what you made in 2031 — in dollars that buy half what they bought then. Call it a fifth of the old job. That number isn’t coming back for anybody.”

“Why me?”

“Somebody forwards me one of your client memos about twice a year, and they’re better than anything my own people write.” He exhaled. “And the partners wanted somebody who’s personally been liquidated. Their phrasing, not mine. They said hiring the scar instead of the résumé would be sentiment — hiring the scar and the résumé is just diligence.”

She was quiet long enough that he had to say her name.

The wanting came up before the thinking did — the wish she’d have been embarrassed to say out loud, that they’d offered her the floor instead, a book, a title with weight in it. She let herself have it for a few seconds without arguing, which was new.

Six years ago the sentence it is not the seat you had would have ended the call. It would have meant smaller, and smaller had been the only word in the language she couldn’t sit still inside.

She thought of Ray Delgado, who’d bought Kessler Machine in 2032 after the crash emptied the floor, and had kept the title on purpose for six years without ever once describing an empty building as a comedown.

“Two conditions,” she said. “I bring my analyst. Ostrowski. She’s twenty-six and she’s better at this than the two you’d assign me. She gets the title she’d have gotten if you’d hired her yourself out of a program you like.”

“Done. What’s the second?”

“The framework says what I think it should say, or my name comes off it. Including the parts about our own leverage and about correlated agent bonds. Those are going to be unpopular while every Continuity score on the board is green and everybody wants to know why the risk document sounds like it’s expecting a funeral.”

Farid was quiet a moment. “You understand that’s the entire reason it’s worth doing.”

“I understand it’s the entire reason you’ll agree in March and fight me in October.”

“Probably,” he said. She heard something that had taken six years and a wipeout to earn: he was talking to her like a peer.

She still bought bitcoin every week. Not much — a fixed share of the practice’s revenue, never a fixed dollar figure, no borrowing, held outright at a custodian whose interface she found genuinely annoying and had chosen partly for that reason.

She had stopped explaining it to clients as a thesis. Six years of it had rebuilt a position that was, in coin count, a little under a twentieth of what she’d inherited. The fraction was the point.

She’d gotten the habit from Janelle, who had been moving thirty dollars a week out of a machinist’s pay since 2024 — some unexamined mix of spite and half-belief in a man who was still alive to argue with. Janelle was still moving it fourteen years later, out of the garage shop in Millbrook — though thirty dollars had been the habit’s name and not its size since 2035, when she’d cut it loose from the dollar and made it a share of every invoice instead. The money came partly from Kessler now: the empty floor billed in bitcoin and paid Ray more than one man had any use for, so Ray sent Janelle the difference and called it overflow work in months when there was no overflow. Neither of them corrected the other.

Mara had never said out loud that the discipline she’d rebuilt her professional life on came from a woman who’d never managed a dollar of anybody else’s money. Saying it would have made it a story, and she wanted it to stay a practice.

Daniel called that night. He led with Backstop Bond, which meant he’d called about something else. It took him until the second cup to get to a woman named Voss, and ninety minutes she had spent walking his clearing-house floor, and the question she’d left him with — whether the people holding the keys to the catastrophic bonds were still ordinary people, or whether he’d started selecting for the kind who’d say yes to holding an off switch forever.

“Four months of somebody being extremely pleasant to me and no ask attached,” he said. “That’s the part I can’t place. And I’m not asking you for money. I want to be clear about that before you spend the call waiting for it.”

“I know you’re not.” She found she was smiling at the freight door. “You’re asking me what it means when an institution starts being polite to you and won’t say what it wants.”

“Yeah. That.”

“It means they haven’t decided yet, and that they will. Right now they’re asking how the bonding layer works. One day the questions turn — how would you run it, what would you do with the master keys, who would you bring with you. That’s not diligence anymore. That’s casting.”

“When it turns, call me the same night. Not after. I’ve been on both ends of that room and you’ve only been on one.”

“I will.”

“I mean it, Daniel.”

“I know you do,” he said, and then, after a pause just long enough to carry six years in it, “Farid’s a fool for waiting this long.”

She didn’t ask how he knew. She’d mentioned Halcyon in the first four minutes of the call, and he had let it go by and carried it the whole hour anyway.

She said good night. She sat in a two-room office above what used to be a piano showroom — a fifth of a number that wasn’t coming back, a title with no floor under it, and a memo in a client file about three cities posting identical bond language while every score on the board read green. Smaller, on purpose. The old panic no longer got a vote.
