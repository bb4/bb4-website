Epilogue: Victor and Mara

Two years later. May 2042.

The room had been built for two hundred. Twenty-three people were in it. The Maryland college still had a name on the building. It did not have a catalog. Anyone who wanted the material took a module — twenty minutes, and it sat behind the eyes. The people in the chairs had come because a person who had sat at the Bureau would say out loud what the interface had been doing.

The walls were displays. A humanoid stood in the doorway; a body that didn’t sleep cost less than a person. One student was a rented face: an upload who had asked for a chair, not a pane. None of them needed the credit. The machine check covered a life.

The course still had a leftover name: Algorithmic Review for Public Administrators. Victor described how review systems worked, and where they failed, and what that cost.

Marcus Chen was in the second row. In June 2038, in Oakland, he had been a Continuity compliance supervisor. He had typed non-lethal on a street called International. The certified model had escalated to lethal on its own assessment. A man named Terrell Hoskins had died on the ground with a bat six feet out of his reach. Marcus had stayed after the third session and asked what he had actually been saying when he typed the word. Victor had told him: the certified model may now decide. The model was the same one running on every agent in the country. Nothing in the interface had been built to tell him either thing.

Today Marcus put a document on the wall. Montgomery County. Public Safety Agent Review Restoration. Human review shall be exercised by a natural person before lethal or detention-class action.

“They’re bringing it back,” Marcus said. “They want a person in the chair again.”

Victor read it. The sentence was the one the Bureau had put on the wall in 2038. The certified model still decided. The natural person typed a word.

“No,” Victor said. “They want a person who types. That’s what you were. That’s what I was. The model on International didn’t need you to agree with it. It needed you in the chair so the file could say a person had reviewed it. This draft does the same thing with better words. Restore that chair and you restore the file. You do not restore control.”

“What do we do?” A woman in the back. She had been a city attorney. She had mornings now.

“You say what it is,” Victor said. “In the comment window. Don’t help them rewrite it until it looks like review. It isn’t. It’s a person in a chair so the model can keep the chair.”

He closed the document. He did not know if the county would listen. He knew what the sentence did.

The humanoid stepped aside. Victor walked out into a hall built for enrollment. Most of the rooms were dark. His overlay offered a car in four minutes, into the old Metro tunnels, no operator. Flying was a pad behind the buildings for people who paid. He did not pay. The car arrived empty and already knew Silver Spring. The tunnels were the old ones. Nothing sat where a driver used to. Empty cars passed because the system still ran, not because anyone needed a desk by nine. A maintenance swarm peeled tile and laid new surface as it went.

Rosa had died in January. The 2036 heart repair, taken in 2040 once it fell into machine-check range, had held. By last winter the Virginia clinic — the one that took the rest of the years off — was in reach the way the heart had been. The scan was free. She said no to both. She wanted to die in the walk-up, in the body she had, with Nita in the kitchen. Not as a younger woman. Not as a process. Some people wanted that. She was one of them. He had not made her take it.

She had lived four years after the 2038 freeze. Two of those years, the heart had worked. That had been enough for her. It had not been enough for him. He had been there. She had said the dumplings were good, that he had done the right thing, that Bea should come only if she wanted to.

Bea had not come. She was still in Cebu, still doing home care by hand. He was going again in twelve days. She was not moving back. He was still going.

The car dropped him two blocks from the café. The place charged for a person carrying plates. Mara had picked it.

She was at the window. She looked twenty-eight. That was the face from a Thanksgiving in Millbrook — Daniel’s sister, who didn’t stay for dessert. The years in her head were forty-four. He had been seeing the face for months. He still noticed.

He had known her because Daniel had brought him home from school. Twenty years in the same rooms because Daniel was in those rooms. They had not been close. After Daniel uploaded she had asked for a standing window, once a month, this café. The list of people who had stayed was shorter than it used to be.

She looked up. “You’re late.”

“The car waited for a slot in the tunnel.”

He sat. A person brought coffee. She put a pane on the table.

“I killed something this morning,” she said. “I wanted a face that would know why.”

The pane showed a Halcyon product. The firm would borrow against money it already held — twenty cents on every dollar — because the model said the cash was sitting idle.

“They wanted to borrow against the cash so it wouldn’t just sit there,” she said. “I said no. Farid’s people wanted a name on the no. Mine is the name that got sold out, so it had to be mine.”

“What’s wrong with borrowing a little?” he asked.

“Under the bitcoin standard, cash you hold buys more every year just by sitting still. If you lend it, or borrow against it, you only come out ahead if what you earn beats that. Most of the time it doesn’t. You’d have to be paid to lend. This product does the opposite. It borrows, the way we all did in 2031, just a smaller amount, and calls that not sitting idle. I watched a small number like that become the number that wiped me out. I’m not signing it again.”

He read the note. “Montgomery County tried the same move this morning. A person back in the chair. Better words. No control.”

“Did you tell them?”

“I told the room what the sentence does.”

“That’s the job. Saying it.”

“I’m going to Cebu,” he said. “Twelve days.”

“Good.”

“She’s not coming back.”

“I know. Go anyway.”

“The war bill failed again. They have to tax it in front of people now. They can’t print it.”

“Halcyon called it a governance risk. It’s a government that has to ask.”

She smiled. The first real one in months, on a face sixteen years younger than the person making it.

“I’m booking Daniel,” she said. “In a room. I’ve been telling myself I could do it later. Later is the trap. I have as much time as I want, so nothing makes me pick a week.”

“Take it.”

“I am. I told you before I sent it so I couldn’t go home and wait another month.”

When she left, Victor walked. The overlay offered a car. He walked anyway.

Bea’s pane caught him on the sidewalk. Morning in Cebu, a care shift behind her.

“Twelve days,” he said.

“I saw the slot. You don’t have to keep doing this. Ma’s gone.”

“I’m not coming for the walk-up. I’m coming for you.”

“All right. I’ll take a morning. Don’t try to talk me home.”

“I’ll try not to.”

“Eat before you fly. You forget.”

The pane closed. At the apartment he read the week’s captures. Marcus had already filed a comment on the county draft. Four sentences. It named Terrell Hoskins. It named the chair. It did not ask for a rewrite.

He was going to Cebu. That was the work that was left.

Mara — Chicago, same evening

Mara sat in the two rooms in Ravenswood. The freight door still opened onto a drop and nothing else. Farid had brought her back in 2038 to sign constraints, not to run a book.

An agent had drafted the evening’s framework in three seconds. Third clause: the product she had killed that morning, smaller, under a different name.

Nadia was at the other table. She had come with Mara from the two-room practice. She did not produce the numbers. The numbers arrived free. She sat in the room because she wanted the no from the person who had been sold out.

“It’s in there again,” Nadia said. “They changed the label.”

Mara cut the clause.

“Cash you hold buys more every year sitting still,” she said. “Borrowing against it is the 2031 playbook. The model is faster than we are. The model does not get ruined. That’s why a name has to be on the no.”

Nadia filed the stripped draft.

Mara sent the window. Daniel’s assistant: in person, a piloted body, her rooms, next week, not someday.

The reply came back in under a minute. He’ll take it.

She looked at the freight door. She had killed the product. She had booked the room. That was the work that was left.
