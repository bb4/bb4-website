Chapter 11: The Same Week

Kessler Machine opened at 6 a.m. in the second week of November, same as it had every weekday for thirty-one years, and Denise was there at 5:50. The alarm panel still ran on a dead man’s ex-wife’s birthday because nobody had ever gotten around to changing it.

Ten months since the funeral — ten months since a camera on an arm had started watching Marisol’s station. Ten months of nothing else changing enough to make her regret the thirty dollars she still moved into her wallet app most Fridays.

Tyler had his phone out again before the coffee finished. “You seeing this? They’re saying it’s official now. AGI — artificial general intelligence. AI that can learn and handle most kinds of thinking as well as a person can. Not just that fusion lab from the summer. They’re saying several systems have crossed the line.”

“Saying who’s saying,” Purnell said.

“Everybody. There’s supposed to be some kind of joint statement today. And there’s already three companies putting out layoff numbers this morning that they’re blaming on it.”

Denise walked her station the way she always did, a hand on each thing in turn. She had the job traveler for a transmission housing clipped to her fixture — the work sheet that rode with each part — tolerances in a pencil hand that wasn’t Ray’s. Ray didn’t write the travelers himself anymore, hadn’t in four months, not since TruePath had gone in.

TruePath was not a camera on an arm. It read a design, decided how to set up the machines, chose the cutting speeds and tool changes, and scheduled the shop’s entire week. Those decisions used to be the skilled part of Denise’s job.

She had watched TruePath’s third revision correctly diagnose, on its own, a vibration in the boring bar she’d spent two days chasing the winter before.

Ray called the floor meeting at 6:45.

“I’m not going to make this a whole thing. Before my chance to buy the shop comes up for review in the spring, the trust wants me down to the number of workers TruePath leaves enough work for. I fought that number for three weeks. I lost the fight two days ago. Two positions. Effective end of the month.”

Nobody asked which two. Denise watched Purnell’s face close down — sixty-one, immovable, running the old hand mill TruePath couldn’t yet make sense of. She watched Tyler’s face do the opposite thing.

Marisol found her at the coffee machine after.

“I already know it’s not me. TruePath still can’t tell you why a part failed, just whether it did. Somebody still has to look at the ones it flags. That’s the whole reason I’m still on that floor.” She poured her coffee. “Go find out. Standing here guessing is worse than either answer.”

Denise kept walking, because Ray’s door was already open and waiting for her.

“Denise. Can I get a minute?”

It was hers.

“TruePath does the programming now. Not perfectly. Better than it did in July, worse than you some days, but well enough that if I keep your position in next spring’s budget, the trust won’t let me buy the shop.”

“I know you fought.”

“You’re allowed to be angrier at me than that.”

“I’m not angry at you, Ray. You didn’t build the thing that got better than me at my own job. You just had to be the one standing in the room holding the notice when it finished.” She almost stopped there. “I’ve got money from the will for a favor I never thought mattered. I’ve got more put away than anybody in this building would guess. So no. Save the angry-at-me for somebody who’s earned it more.”

“What’s the package?”

“Six weeks, plus insurance through February.” He stopped. “The trust’s approved letting go of a piece of equipment nobody’s used since TruePath took over the programming. Old boring-and-facing machine Warren bought used. I can put in a word that you get first crack at buying it for scrap value, if you ever want a shop of your own someday.”

She thought about that machine. She said she’d think about it.

She was halfway across the floor when Ray’s door opened again and Tyler came out of it.

He came out wrong — too fast, the look on his face the opposite of Purnell’s closing-down. Ray was a step behind him saying his name carefully.

Tyler turned and put both hands into Ray’s chest and shoved. Ray went back into the doorframe hard enough that the frame answered. Tyler’s travel clipboard left his hand. It hit the tool crib cage and came apart. A corner of the board caught Ray under the eye and opened a line there.

“Eleven years,” Tyler said, wrong on purpose. “You stood up there and you said you fought. You didn’t fight. You picked a fucking spreadsheet.”

“Tyler. Go home.”

“I am home. This is—” Tyler stopped. His eyes found Denise for half a second, registered that she was already holding the same news, and something in him flickered and went. He kicked the nearest trash can hard enough that it spilled metal shavings and paper cups, and then he walked out through the bay door without taking his jacket.

Cold air came in. Denise bent and picked up three of the traveler pages because her hands needed a job that wasn’t a fist. She set them on the crib counter in a stack that didn’t matter.

“You all right?”

“I’ll live. He will too. Eventually.” Ray pressed the heel of his hand under the eye. “You should go. Before this becomes a meeting.”

The radio in the truck had the AGI story running as the top segment. Phrases like coordinated framework and responsible transition period laid end to end without ever quite saying anything a person standing in a parking lot with a layoff letter could use.

Denise turned it off and drove the rest of the way north on 9, past the shuttered movie theater and the bank branch that had become a vape shop.

Angie called at eight, same as every week. “What do you actually need?”

“I don’t know yet. I might, in a few days.”

She got the can down that night out of habit and did the math in her head. One bitcoin. She let herself check its dollar value for the first time in weeks — closer to two hundred thousand than to one hundred and twenty thousand now, plus nearly four years of thirty-dollar Fridays growing into something on top of that.

It wasn’t nothing, but it could not fix the real problem. She had spent twelve years becoming good at work that no employer wanted to pay her to do anymore.

She put the can back and stood at the kitchen window looking out at a yard she’d need to figure out how to keep paying for.

She thought of Warren’s sentence — hold onto something you don’t need yet. Standing in her own dark kitchen on the night she’d lost the job, she understood that she had never actually needed the thing yet, not once, in going on four years. She needed it now.

That was the point of the instruction. She turned off the kitchen light and went upstairs. Tomorrow she would start finding out whether Warren had been wise, or only lucky.
