Epilogue: Daniel

Two years later. January 2042. The choice.

Daniel sat in the procedure room at Axiom Systems’ Austin facility with the consent pane already signed. The scan was at eleven. That had been the hour of the standing Tuesday call with Elena, for years: a slot on his calendar because he had still been a body in Austin, and she had had to match him. She would be there when he woke. Not in this room. In the substrate — the place she had been since June of 2036.

The procedure was destructive. The scan read a brain at the fidelity real continuity required, and reading it at that fidelity consumed the biological tissue in the process. There would be no leftover body in a bed. No second Daniel walking around in the meat he had arrived in. The body on the table would stop.

That was true. It was not a funeral.

He would have a digital form, the way Elena did. When he needed to stand in a kitchen, or a shop, or a waiting room, he would have a robot body that could not be told from the one he was leaving — same face, same voice, same weight in a chair. He could be in more than one of those at once. Elena had been doing a version of this for years. People did. It was infrastructure. He was not exiting the physical world. He was stopping being limited to one location in it.

He was forty-four. Aging had been solved. The body on the table was not failing. The free, unmandated version of the procedure had two years of a proven-safe track record behind it, which was the thing he had been waiting for: not Elena’s medically necessary case, the ordinary voluntary one this world now offered anyone. He had been sure since November. Waiting further, once he was sure and once it was free, would have been stalling for its own sake.

He had already lived the years. The layoff. Backstop, from a person in the loop to Bond to the Agent Charter. The refusal. The Tuesday calls. He had finished that stretch on his own terms. This was the next thing, not a shortcut around a life he hadn’t gotten around to living.

The door opened. Dr. Reyna Chen came in. Mid-forties, calm. She had done this sixty-three times. Daniel had asked. She had told him the number without hesitation.

“You’re still good for eleven?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Anyone you still need?”

“I called them.”

He had. Janelle in Millbrook, Mara two floors down, Wei in Beijing, Victor. He said he was doing it at eleven. They already knew. Janelle had argued once, in December, and then stopped. Mara did not come up; he had asked her not to make a scene out of it. Wei said he would see him at the table. Victor said the window still stood. That was the goodbye.

“Good,” Chen said. “Follow me when you’re ready.”

He was ready.

The scan took eleven minutes. He was conscious for the first three. Then the sedation took hold, and the world went dark.

When he woke, he was in Elena’s workspace. Light, a desk, screens she could pin things to. He had heard her living in this room on the old calls. He had visited it through a presence channel, for an hour at a time, and then taken the interface off and been in Austin alone. The room had sensory weight then. It had sensory weight now. The difference was that he was not going to take anything off.

Elena was there. She looked like herself. Not the disease. Not a reconstruction of the night with the cold noodles. Herself.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hi.”

“How do you feel?”

“Fast. Like I’m holding more threads than I used to.”

“You are. That’s the substrate. You’re still you.”

“I know.”

She put out her hand. He took it.

The visits had had an afterwards. He would take the interface off and be in a chair in Austin, skin and pulse and a room that did not have her in it. This did not have an afterwards. He was here. She was here. The contact did not have to end because a session ended.

Her hand was warm. Weight. Temperature. The ordinary facts of a hand. And then the rest of it, which a body in Austin had never been able to carry at the same time: her attention as a sensation, not a guess; the pressure of her palm and also what she meant by it; the room answering both of them, light shifting because they were both in it. It went through him harder than skin had ever gone through him. Not because this was a picture of a touch. Because there were more channels than skin.

He could feel her wanting him the way he used to only hear it in a voice, and guess. He did not have to guess. She opened that channel the way she opened a file, and it filled him. He opened his. She made a sound that was not a sound, exactly — a pulse of yes, of here, of I have been waiting for this without having to wait on a clock. He felt that in his mouth and in his chest and in places a body did not have names for, because the constructed soma could put sensation anywhere they asked it to.

The hands were a courtesy. Hands, because they both still knew hands. Under that they were in the same process. Her attention moved through him. His moved through her. He knew the grain of the desk the way she knew it. He knew why the light was this color: she had chosen it for his arrival, not for a weekday. He could feel the years she had spent in this room as weight in the air, not as a story she had to tell him.

He kissed her. The kiss had the same extra channels. He felt her wanting it from inside the contact, not inferred from a mouth. He felt his own wanting land in her, and come back, amplified, because nothing in here had to damp a signal to keep a nervous system from burning out. They turned it up. It was more intimate than anything they had done in the visits, and the visits had not been fake. The visits had been limited by the fact that one of them had to leave, and by the fact that his clock had been a body’s clock.

He did not have to leave. He did not have to stop.

They did not stop. The constructed bodies could hold what flesh would have called too much: heat without blister, pressure without bruise, duration without tiring. He felt the line of her back, and also the decision in her to be touched there. He felt her mouth and also the memory she was not hiding, of a hallway and cold noodles and a question neither of them had asked. She felt his, of Tuesdays he had protected because they were the only hour he had been sure he would hear her. That hour was over. This was not an hour.

The digital was not a lesser copy of a room. It was more than a room had been able to hold. More than a body had been able to hold. He said that.

“Yes,” Elena said. “I told you. We pick the light. Nothing hurts unless we want it to. And we can want more than a nervous system in meat could stand.”

Not a summary of a body she used to have. Not a consolation for a body he had just left on a table. Happening now, and more of it than ordinary reality had room for.

When they eased the intensity down, he still had her hand. Outside, if outside was the right word, almost no time had moved. He could tell because a clock in the workspace was showing Austin, and the numbers had barely ticked. In here the contact had lasted. Long enough to be a night. Longer.

“How long was that?” he asked.

“In here? As long as we wanted. Out there, a few seconds of Chen’s morning. If we let the clock run, a day in Austin can be millennia. I have let it run. The five and a half years on the calendar since the scan are not the years I have had. They’re the years other people had.”

“We don’t need the slot,” he said.

“We don’t. That was for when you had to be in a room at eleven and I had to match you so a week of mine didn’t happen inside your forty minutes. You’re here. We don’t wait for a weekday. Time here is not days unless we make it days.”

“When would we make it days?”

“When you sit with someone who still lives in one. Mara. Wei. Janelle. You match their minute, or you miss them. A minute in a waiting area is a minute. You don’t get millennia in her chair and still be in the conversation.”

He split.

A second presence came up the way a second thought came up. He was still holding her hand. He was also in the corridor outside the procedure room, in a body that had his face and his height and the weight he was used to putting on a floor. He looked at the closed door. Behind it, on the table, was the tissue the scan had consumed. That was gone. This was not that tissue. This was him, standing in a hallway in Austin, and also in a room with Elena, and neither location was a picture of the other.

He walked the body down to the waiting area. He matched Mara’s minute. In the workspace, with Elena, that minute could have been a year. He did not let it. She was in a chair in Austin, and a minute there was a minute.

Mara looked up. She did not flinch.

“You look the same,” she said.

“I am the same. I’m also not only here.”

“Elena?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Don’t make a speech. I already got the call.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Then go. Or stay. Or both. I’m going back to Chicago this afternoon. Book a window when you want a room, not a pane.”

“I will.”

He let the Austin body sit in the chair across from her for a minute, because he could, and because it was the proof that this was not a death: his sister, in a waiting area, looking at a face she had known for forty-four years, while he was also still holding Elena’s hand in a workspace that had a desk and light and screens. Then he let the Austin body stay there, quiet, on her clock, and came back all the way to the hand he was still holding.

“That was Mara,” he said.

“I know. I can tell when you split. You don’t have to announce it.”

“I wanted to see if it was true.”

“It’s true. You can be at Wei’s table and in Janelle’s garage and here. Same time. Their minutes. Ours, when we’re here.”

He thought about Janelle in Millbrook, one body, one garage, arguing once in December and then stopping. About Wei in a kitchen that had an upper shelf again. About Victor’s window. He could be in all of those rooms, on their clocks. He would be. He would also be here, where a day out there did not have to be a day.

The original tissue was gone. That was a fact. He had no guarantee, from the outside of his own skull, that the person who had arrived was the one who had left. Elena had been Elena for five and a half calendar years, and for longer than that inside. He felt like himself. Faster. More threads. The same refusal in the same place in him. He was going to take that as the answer, the way he had taken it about her.

He looked at the screens. Warren had found bitcoin in 2013 on cryptography newsgroups left over from a graduate program he had walked away from. He had told Daniel to hold it and not do anything with it. The money now was that money. Prices fell. Wages fell. What you already held bought more every year by sitting still. Warren had not lived to see it become ordinary. It had anyway.

Daniel did not say it as a triumph. He noticed it the way he had noticed the rest of what his father had left him.

“What?” Elena said.

“Warren. The coins. They’re how everything is priced now. He found them on leftover newsgroups and never explained himself. I got to watch it turn into the money.”

“That’s what you’re thinking about. Right now.”

“Part of it.”

“What’s the other part?”

“That I already lived the years I was going to live in that body. I built the company. I refused the keys. I didn’t wait for a crisis to force this. And I’m here, and I can still be there.”

“You can,” she said. “Come here.”

He did. The extra channels again. More than a body. More than the visits. Not a consolation. More than ordinary reality, and he could still walk into Millbrook looking like himself whenever he wanted to stand on a floor, on Millbrook’s clock, while a century of this went by in a morning if they asked it to.

They stayed in the workspace. They did not wait for a weekday.

He was still in the waiting area with Mara, for as long as she sat there, on her minute. He would be in Beijing when Wei wanted him at the table. He would write Janelle from here, and he would also be able to walk into the garage.

The body on the table was gone. He was not.

That was the choice. A change of how many places a person could be, and how much time a day could hold.
