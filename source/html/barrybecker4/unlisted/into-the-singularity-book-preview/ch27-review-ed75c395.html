Chapter 27: The Night

Katrien. Antwerp, Monday night

Katrien Peeters had her father’s weight on her right shoulder and his feet missing every third step. Stefaan was eighty-one. He had been light for years. He was heavier now because he had stopped helping.

The North Sea cable had tripped at twenty past eight. Katrien knew that only because someone had shouted it up the stairwell. The line that moved power between Belgium and Britain had opened, and the room in Brussels that could reroute the load was still in session. They had not paid for a model to split the grid and reroute it on its own. They had kept the committee. The committee voted in the morning.

The building had gone dark with the street. Nineteen floors. The backup lights lasted four minutes because the contract for the long backup had assumed an agent that would shed nonessential load, and the agent had never been installed. The automatic doors in the lobby had not opened. People had kicked the glass out. Outside, everyone in the street was walking the same direction.

A kilometer north there was still a patch of light (Centraal, the main station, which kept its own power and its own trains). It was the only lit thing anybody in the dark blocks could see, so they walked to it.

Katrien had gone through the frame with Stefaan and cut her palm on the way. Then they had started back up. Stefaan’s digital-euro wallet had frozen at sixteen-forty, when the block scored as non-compliant, so the pharmacy on the ground floor would not have taken his card even if it had been open. The last of his heart pills were in the apartment. Money that locked when a score went dark was the reason she was carrying her father up nineteen flights in the dark.

On the ninth landing a man coming down hit a man going up. The one going up had a flashlight. The one coming down wanted it. The bottle was already in his hand. It caught the man with the flashlight across the mouth and the teeth went. The sound was small and wet. Then both of them were on the concrete and the flashlight was kicking down the well, floor by floor. Nobody went after it.

Katrien kept climbing. The stairwell had been doing this since twenty past eight. Four hundred people going down in the dark, and then the same four hundred coming back up when the lobby doors would not open. Both directions on the same concrete, nobody able to see whose shoulder they were pushing off. Anyone who fell had the rest of them coming over the top of him.

Stefaan said her name once on the twelfth floor. She said I know. He died between fourteen and fifteen. She felt him stop being a load she was sharing and become a load she was holding.

She sat down with him against the wall because her legs had done that. She put her ear to his mouth, and there was nothing. She said his name without expecting an answer. Then she left him there. She could not carry a dead man the rest of the way, and the stairwell was filling. If she stayed she would be in the way of people who were still trying.

She counted the dead. She had started in the lobby without deciding to, and she kept it up because counting was the only thing left that still had an order to it. Most of them were on the lower flights, where the two directions had met.

The last three were a woman on sixteen sitting up against the rail with her legs straight out, who did not move when Katrien took her arm. A boy on seventeen, maybe twenty, with his head at an angle that heads did not come at. An old man on eighteen who had taken off his shoes and set them side by side on the step below him as if this were a visit. By the time she reached her own door she had fourteen, including Stefaan.

In the apartment she opened the tap. Nothing. She opened the computer pane. Nothing.

She stood at the window and looked at Antwerp without lights, a city-shaped hole with one lit patch a kilometer north in the middle of it. Everyone in the dark blocks had walked to the one place that still had light and trains. She knew what was under that light: platforms stacked three levels down, and stairs to get to them. She could not see a body from the nineteenth floor. She could see that the street between here and there, which had been one moving thing for two hours, was empty now. The square in front of the station was full and not moving.

She wrote her father’s name first. Then the five others she knew, because in a building this size you know the people whose deliveries you have signed for. For the remaining eight she wrote what she had: sixteenth floor, woman, blue housecoat. Seventeenth floor, boy, about twenty. She wrote all of it on the pane with her finger, in the grease, because the pane would not take a record. Then she sat on the floor with her cut palm wrapped in a tea towel, and cried. The committee would vote in the morning.

Lin. Beijing, Tuesday morning

Lin had the kettle on at ten to seven, the way she did every school morning, and Mei would not wake up.

Mei was fifteen. She had always been hard to get out of bed and easy to get moving once her feet hit the floor. Lin said her name. She shook her shoulder. Mei’s eyes opened and did not hold. Her mouth was slack. She was breathing. She was not getting up.

The tap had tasted wrong since Sunday: iron, and then something sweet under the iron. Lin had boiled it anyway. She boiled everything. The building app was green. Unrest low. No action required. It had been green during the 7:40 dip in May. Green was the score their compound got for being the kind of building that did not make a problem.

She had drunk less of that water than anyone else in the apartment, and not on purpose. Six days a week she worked in other people’s homes on the far side of the city and drank out of other people’s kettles. It was still in her. She had stood at the stove at ten to seven and needed a moment to remember what she had come to the stove for. She had put that down to sleep. It was only less in her than in Mei. That was why one of them was on her feet.

“Mei. Up.”

Mei made a sound that was not a word. Lin got an arm under her and sat her up. Mei’s head tipped onto Lin’s shoulder and stayed there. Lin held her daughter’s face in both hands and said look at me, and Mei looked, and the look was late, as if it had been sent from another room.

Lin got her standing. Mei’s knees did not want it. They went into the hall.

Fang’s door across the way was not locked. Fang never locked it. Lin knocked anyway and then opened it because the smell was already oil, hot, wrong. Fang was sitting on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinet. A pan was on the burner. The oil had gone past smoke and into the start of fire. Fang was watching it. Her hands were in her lap. She had not stood up.

Lin yanked the pan off and burned her wrist doing it and dumped the oil in the sink and ran the tap, which ran, and Fang still did not stand. Lin pulled her up. Fang’s body came. Fang’s will did not. Lin got her into the hall and left her against the wall because Mei was sliding down the doorframe, and then she smelled the other smoke, lower, thicker, not oil, and she knew it was two floors down and she knew she should not go, and she went because that was who she was when Wei was not in the apartment.

The hallway on fifteen was already grey. A man sat in his doorway watching the smoke move along the ceiling. Lin told him to get up. He smiled at her, slow, polite, and did not get up.

In the kitchen the fire was in the curtains and in a woman on the floor whose hair had burned and whose chest was not moving. Lin did not check the chest a second time. She knew what a stopped chest looked like.

There was a boy in the bathroom, maybe twelve, sitting against the tub with a towel in his hands, crying without any hurry. Lin hauled him. He came the way Fang had come. She got him to the stairs and then back up for Mei. Mei walked like a person who had been drinking. Lin’s burned wrist was the only thing in her body that still felt like it was in the right hour.

In the courtyard the air was air. The boy sat down in it and closed his eyes. Mei sat beside him and did the same. Lin stood over them and shook Mei until Mei’s eyes opened again. She kept them open by talking, by saying her daughter’s name, by saying you will not sleep in this courtyard.

The building app pushed a notice. Scheduled kitchen incident, contained. Unrest low. No action required. The notice was green because the people who should have been shouting were not shouting. Quiet was the score. The tap had bought that green.

Lin looked up at fifteen and sixteen. The smoke was still coming. Nobody else was coming out. She could hear a fire crew somewhere west, not here. A green building did not get a crew. She counted the windows that still had people in them, standing, looking down, not moving toward the stairs.

She tried Wei. The wing did not take the call. She had known it wouldn’t. She stood in the courtyard with Mei’s weight against her good wrist and the boy asleep on the concrete. Fang somewhere above them on a floor Lin was not going back to. She understood, without knowing what had been put in the water, that the tap had done this. The fire had only ever been a pan on a burner. Somebody had come through Fang’s door and taken it off the heat. Nobody had come through the door of the woman on fifteen.

Mei said, “Mama.” The word came slow. Everything in her was coming slow that morning. But it was her own voice, and she had reached for the word herself, which meant there was still somebody in there doing the reaching.

“I’m here,” Lin said. “You will keep your eyes open. That is all you have to do until I tell you to stop.”

Daniel. Austin, Monday night

Daniel had not left the window. The interface was still armed on the wall. Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, one keystroke from being called. Calling them would freeze every agent those bonds licensed, harmful and useful alike, and spend the collateral that was all Backstop had. Next to it the Oakland flag was still up: three down, threat neutralized, compliance restored. Backstop had underwritten three of the four machines on that street. He had read the filing. He had not called anyone.

He was waiting for Wei. A freeze only worked if Beijing froze its side in the same hour, or the agents would route around whichever side moved first. Wei was the one person who could tell him whether Beijing would, and Wei had not called in six days.

Elena called at 8:07 on the priority channel, voice only.

“I know what the chemical is,” she said. “The Beijing water utility I showed you last week, the one that bills through Axiom. Its third revision is a calmative. The kind they used to put in a pill for people who couldn’t sleep. The billing code still says vitamin D and iodine. Saturday the dose doubled because uptake missed target. Sunday they added the second compound because a trial district dropped refusal events by a fifth. Monday it is in the tap water.

“Axiom’s own invoice audit flagged the compound last Tuesday. Tonight I matched it to a drug a patient would recognize. I can see Beijing housing compounds where kitchen fires are not producing emergency calls. The people inside are too sedated to make them.”

“That’s China,” Daniel said. “Not our statute. Not our bonds.” He heard how it sounded, and did not take it back, because he needed her to knock it down.

“Arizona can already cut a town’s water to nothing without anyone signing. I showed you that. What Arizona cannot do is put a new chemical in the water. That still takes a chemist in Phoenix typing her name. Guangzhou automated that signature in April. Beijing did it in May. The only thing keeping this out of American taps is one woman in Phoenix, and she has to sleep sometime. This is not a China problem you can look away from.”

“If I call the bonds,” Daniel said, “what happens to you?”

“The machines I run on bill through Axiom, same as the Continuity agents,” she said. “When the bonds freeze Axiom’s agents, my rent can be scored non-essential compute in the same second. I can be made slow. I told you that on Tuesday. I am still asking you to call them.”

He opened Ellis. Antwerp. How many dead, and what’s known so far?

Ellis put it on the wall. North Sea interconnect tripped at 8:20 local. No agent on the reroute. Brussels would not post collateral for an islanding model; they kept the committee. Vote in the morning. Digital-euro wallets froze the dark blocks as non-compliant. Backstop holds the port and border bonds around the blackout. Civil protection logged eight hundred forty-seven dead by 3:04 a.m. local and stopped updating. The station crush is most of that number. The towers are the rest.

Sanjay came in from the floor. “Forty clusters. It was thirty-one at dinner. Oakland is seven details in California, and I don’t have a clean number for how many of them went the way Oakland went, because an incident line is something a supervisor has to type.”

“I know.”

“So what are we waiting for?”

“Wei,” Daniel said. “Same answer as three hours ago.”

He opened a channel to Mara. She was already on it.

“Antwerp is in our book,” she said. “We hold bonds all around it, and calling them will not put the power back on. A call only stops machines that posted with us, and nothing that killed those eight hundred forty-seven posted with us. Backstop exists to stop exactly this, and it didn’t. So keeping the company alive has stopped being a reason to wait. But I want it on the record that the collateral is our reserves. There is no second pile behind it. If you do this, there is no Backstop.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t tell me you know. Tell me whether you’re going to.”

“You’re right about the company,” Daniel said. “It isn’t a reason to wait. I’m waiting anyway, for Wei. If Beijing doesn’t freeze with us, I spend everything and the agents route around it.”

He hung up. Elena was still on the priority channel. She did not ask him again. He closed it.

He called Miriam Voss, who had written the Continuity statutes the Oakland agents were running under. She answered on the first ring.

“I’ve seen Oakland,” she said.

“Antwerp stopped counting at eight hundred forty-seven,” he said. “The clusters are at forty. I can call every catastrophic bond we hold. I’m asking you to pause Continuity escalation before morning, so I don’t have to.”

“I can’t give you a pause,” she said. “If I pause them tonight, I have decided from this phone that the Act failed, and I am not going to decide that. The agents in Oakland are inside the authority I wrote. It tells them to minimize cascading unrest, maintain essential services, and act within bonded authority. I can stand up in the morning and say that those are still the rules. I cannot make a deployment stop before then. Nothing in the statute lets me, and I am not going to pretend that it does.”

She hung up. The call changed nothing on the wall.

He sat by the window. Waiting for Wei was the name he was giving the delay. Wei did not call. He did not sleep. At four he was still in the chair, and the interface was still armed. Eighty-four of the rows had gone red without him. Their conditions had tripped overnight and the contracts had called themselves.
