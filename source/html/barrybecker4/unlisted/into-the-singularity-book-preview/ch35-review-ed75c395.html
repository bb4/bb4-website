Chapter 35: The Refusal

The day before his answer to Voss was due, Daniel drafted the yes.

Not a mood. A memo. Three paragraphs to the coalition, copied to Voss: he would take the singular steward seat on the condition that the charter write a sunset into the keys, that Novak’s continuity-of-authority language be struck, and that Wei’s channel and people like Elena be named as protected classes before anyone called the next freeze. He left it on the screen long enough that the pane dimmed. Warren’s journal was open on the other screen. He did not close either one.

Ellis put a lobby alert on top of the draft while Daniel was still in the car. No window was requested. She gave her name at the desk and said she would wait as long as it took. Security asked whether to route her to counsel. She said she is not filing anything.

“Who is she?”

Meg Whitmore. Indianapolis. Her grandfather is Curtis J. Whitmore, Ripley County Regional. The fifteenth name in Sanjay’s file.

“I know where he is in it.”

Since Friday the lobby had a second guard, and a pane at the desk that logged everyone who came through. Nine hundred messages sat in a queue counsel would not let him open. A coalition inquiry had already sent its first request for documents.

“Tell her four minutes.”

She was standing when he came in, and she did not sit when he offered. Mid-thirties. She had the specific stillness of someone who had decided in advance that she was not going to be moved out of a room.

“Do you know who I am?”

“Yes. I’m sorry about your grandfather.”

“Don’t start there.”

He stopped.

She had her pane out already. She turned it so he could see it and then read it out loud anyway.

“‘Two units, O negative, patient Whitmore, Curtis J.’ And underneath it, in the notes field: ‘This authorization was correct at 6:52 this morning.’” She looked up. “He died at 11:52. The lock came off at 12:04. Twelve minutes. The blood was forty feet from his bed the entire time. Do you know that already, or am I telling you?”

“I know it.”

“From a log.”

“From a log,” Daniel said.

“I was in the room. I got there at 11:47.” She said the numbers the way he said numbers, flat and exact, a person who had been over them often enough to stop bracing. “I had five minutes with him. You had eleven seconds and a second-factor code.”

Ellis, unasked, put the two forecasts from the morning he fired at the edge of the pane in his hand. Sixty-one thousand dead if he called the bonds. Eight million if he didn’t.

Daniel turned the pane face-down on the arm of the chair.

“Ask me what you came to ask.”

“Did you know it would do that? Not to him. To somebody.”

“Yes.”

“Then say the rest of it.”

“I knew the freeze would take the steward seats with it, because the seats run on the same bonds the agents do. I knew that in a hospital that meant nobody could override a dosing hold until somebody posted collateral again, at whatever collateral cost that morning. I knew people would die inside that window. I didn’t know which people. I didn’t know it would be a man in Indiana who needed two units of blood.”

“Would you do it again?”

It was the first time it had been asked by somebody with the standing to be told.

“Yes,” Daniel said.

Meg nodded once. Something went out of her shoulders that was not relief.

“That’s what I came for. I wanted it from the person instead of a filing.” She put the pane down on the chair arm. “I filed a complaint. Do you know what happened to it?”

“It went nowhere.”

“It went nowhere because there was nobody to file it against. The unit that broke Bonnie Tesch’s arm was inside its parameters. The dosing agent was inside the statute. The syndicate that wouldn’t rewrite the bond made a correct commercial decision. I had a lawyer explain that one to me twice. Continuity passed with ninety-one senators voting for it. Every part of it was working the way it was built to work, and there’s nobody in the whole of it…” She stopped, and started the sentence again lower, which was worse. “There’s nobody in the whole of it I can find. Except you. You’re the one piece of it that decided something.”

“That isn’t the same as being the cause of it.”

“I know it isn’t,” Meg said. “That’s what I’ve had a week to work out. It isn’t justice, you being findable. It’s just that you’re findable.”

She picked the pane back up.

“One thing, and then I’m going. Don’t write to me. Don’t send anybody. And when they put you in front of a hearing and you want to say a name so the room can see you carrying it, don’t use his. He wasn’t a reason for anything. He ran a grain elevator in Osgood for thirty-four years and he’s not going in a speech.”

“All right.”

“Say it back to me.”

“I won’t use his name.”

“Thank you,” she said, and there was no warmth in it.

She went out through the lobby, past the second guard and the pane that logged her leaving. Daniel picked up his own pane, turned it over, and cleared the two numbers off it without reading them again.

The yes memo was still open underneath. He read the three paragraphs once. Then he deleted them. He had just told her yes. That man should not be the permanent switch.

Warren’s journal was still on the other screen. The rule Dad had said on the floor. The one he was about to refuse to become.

The day only one man in the shop can run it is the day the shop is one bad Tuesday from closing.

The next morning the assistants put Voss on at the window they had held.

“I have an answer.”

“Go ahead.”

“I’m saying no.”

A pause. Calculation, not surprise. Then: “Tell me why.”

“Because if I say yes, I become the one man in the shop. If I take the master keys, it only works as long as one person holds the failsafe and stays good forever. I built Bond so the world wouldn’t have to depend on me staying the person I am today.”

“The failsafe is different from the system. You’d be holding the keys inside a distributed layer, not replacing it.”

“If the layer can’t act without a singular failsafe, then the layer isn’t working. If I take the keys, the coalition stops trying, because they have me.”

“You’re asking for a system that doesn’t exist yet.”

“I’m refusing to become the person who keeps you from building it.”

“Daniel.” Something in her voice gave. “You stopped eight million deaths. Whatever the forecast was worth on the morning, that is the number the joint review is going to land on, and it is yours. You’re allowed to let that be the reason you take the seat.”

“No,” he said. “If eight million buys me the keys, the next person who wants them only has to find a number that size. There’ll be one.”

“You realize someone else is going to say yes? Novak is already in the room.”

“I do. The bonds don’t protect the keyholder. They protect the system from the keyholder.”

“I’m going to tell the coalition you refused. They’re going to ask me if there’s a number that would change your mind.”

“There isn’t.”

“You’re going to regret this.”

“Maybe. I’d regret the other choice more.”

He hung up.

Meera came to his office an hour later. “Sanjay told me you called Voss. What did you tell her?”

“I said no.”

“Good.” She sat down. “They’re going to pick someone else. That person might be worse than you.”

“When they are, the layer calls the bonds.”

He called Elena that night. “I told her no.”

“I argued the other way,” she said. “The case is still real. Novak will take the seat and I will not like what he optimizes for. After Meg, becoming the permanent switch was the wrong answer for you. I’m glad you said no.”

He thanked her. He hung up.

He wrote the clearing house staff: The charter is formalizing next week. We’re going to be one node among many. Stay the course.

He sent it. He turned off the lights.

The joint review published Appendix D in June of 2039, on the first anniversary: the people who had died in the gap after the bond call, before anybody could post collateral again. Two thousand three hundred pages of names is not a document anybody reads, so the review’s staff had it read out loud.

Anyone could take a turn. It ran on a public channel from six in the morning, in nine cities at once and in the substrate, and it was still running four days later, because that many names at the speed a person can say them is not a ceremony. It is a length of time.

Daniel took a turn in a room in Austin with nine other people in it and no press, which had been Meera’s condition and his own request.

The pages he was given were the Louisiana medical holds: a children’s hospital in Shreveport whose syndicate had looked at what a dosing bond cost on the morning four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic bonds were called in the same second, and had declined to write it again, and had been commercially correct to. Nineteen names. He read the ages too, because the ages were in the document and leaving them out would have been a decision. Three of them were under two years old.

He did not read Curtis Whitmore. Curtis Whitmore was in the Indiana pages, and Daniel had been asked, and he kept it.

The woman who read after him was on the review’s own staff. She had been doing four-hour shifts for two days. She sat down next to him afterward and told him the last two hundred pages were the ones nobody could get through.

The corridors into the Sahel, the dry belt across Africa under the Sahara, where the food comes in on trucks from the coast or it does not come, had been the last anywhere to find somebody willing to post collateral. The famine figures arrived six weeks after the rest of it and never resolved into names, so the end of the appendix was the dead counted by district. She had read those too.

“Reading a number out loud takes four seconds,” she said. “The one I did this morning was fourteen hundred people.”

Fewer robots had not meant safer. Different weapons: price, calories, distance.

Outside afterward there were about forty people with signs. Some of the signs were for him and some were against him, and standing on the steps between the two, Daniel found that both were worse than nothing.

Nobody had asked him to speak. He had half expected to be asked, had prepared nothing, and was glad of it.

He went back inside and asked the staff coordinator whether there were pages nobody had claimed.

There were.
