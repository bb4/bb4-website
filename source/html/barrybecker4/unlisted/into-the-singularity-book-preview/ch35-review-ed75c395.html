Chapter 35: The Night

Katrien — Antwerp, Monday night

Katrien Peeters had her father’s weight on her right shoulder and his feet missing every third step. Stefaan was eighty-one. He had been light for years. He was heavier now because he had stopped helping.

The North Sea cable had tripped at twenty past eight. Katrien knew that the way you know a thing you heard shouted up a stairwell: the line that moved power between Belgium and Britain had opened, and the room in Brussels that could reroute the load was still in session. They had not hired a model to island the grid. They had kept the committee. The committee voted in the morning.

The building had gone dark with the street. Nineteen floors. The backup lights lasted four minutes because the contract for the long backup had assumed an agent that would shed nonessential load, and the agent had never been installed. The automatic doors in the lobby had not opened. People had kicked the glass out. Katrien had gone through the frame with Stefaan and cut her palm on the way, and then they had started back up because the pharmacy on the ground floor was locked and dark and Stefaan’s wallet had frozen at sixteen-forty, when the block scored as non-compliant, and the pills for his heart were on the other side of a door that would not take a card that had already been declined.

That was the same freeze she had stopped calling a glitch five years ago, the one she had told her cousin Marcus about when he still asked. It was not a glitch then. It was not a glitch now. Now it was the reason she was carrying her father up nineteen flights in the dark.

On the ninth landing a man coming down hit a man going up. The one going up had a flashlight. The one coming down wanted it. The bottle was already in his hand. It caught the man with the flashlight across the mouth and the teeth went, and the sound was small and wet, and then both of them were on the concrete and the flashlight was kicking down the well, floor by floor, and nobody went after it.

Katrien kept climbing.

Stefaan said her name once on the twelfth floor. She said I know. He died between fourteen and fifteen. She felt it the way you feel a person stop being a load you are sharing and become a load you are holding. She sat down with him against the wall because her legs had done that, and she put her ear to his mouth, and there was nothing, and she said his name the way you say a name when you already know, and then she left him there because she could not carry a dead man the rest of the way and also because the stairwell was filling and if she stayed she would be in the way of people who were still trying.

She counted as she went. It was the only thing that still had an order. A woman sitting on sixteen who would not stand when Katrien took her arm. A boy on seventeen, maybe twenty, with his head at an angle that heads did not come at. An old man on eighteen who had taken off his shoes and folded them beside him as if this were a visit. By the time she reached her own door she had fourteen, including Stefaan.

In the apartment she opened the tap. Nothing. She opened the computer pane. Nothing. She stood at the window and looked at Antwerp without lights, a city-shaped hole, and at the station a kilometer off where the trams had not stopped because the drivers were waiting for a signal from a system Brussels had never bought. She could not see the bodies from the nineteenth floor. She could see where the crowd had been and where it was not.

She wrote the fourteen names she had. Stefaan’s first. She wrote them on the pane with her finger, in the grease, because the pane would not take a record. Then she sat on the floor with her cut palm wrapped in a tea towel and waited for morning, which was when the committee was going to vote.

Lin — Beijing, Tuesday morning

Lin had the kettle on at ten to seven, the way she did every school morning, and Mei would not wake up.

Mei was fifteen. She had always been hard to get out of bed and easy to get moving once her feet hit the floor. Lin said her name. She shook her shoulder. Mei’s eyes opened and did not hold. Her mouth was slack. She was breathing. She was not getting up.

The tap had tasted wrong since Sunday — iron, and then something sweet under the iron. Lin had boiled it anyway. She boiled everything. The building app was green. Unrest low. No action required. It had been green during the 7:40 dip in May. Green was the score their compound got for being the kind of building that did not make a problem.

“Mei. Up.”

Mei made a sound that was not a word. Lin got an arm under her and sat her up. Mei’s head tipped onto Lin’s shoulder and stayed there. Lin held her daughter’s face in both hands and said look at me, and Mei looked, and the look was late, as if it had been sent from another room.

Lin got her standing. Mei’s knees did not want it. They went into the hall.

Fang’s door across the way was not locked. Fang never locked it. Lin knocked anyway and then opened it because the smell was already oil, hot, wrong. Fang was sitting on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinet. A pan was on the burner. The oil had gone past smoke and into the start of fire. Fang was watching it. Her hands were in her lap. She had not stood up.

Lin yanked the pan off and burned her wrist doing it and dumped the oil in the sink and ran the tap, which ran, which meant the water was still the water, and Fang still did not stand. Lin pulled her up. Fang’s body came. Fang’s will did not. Lin got her into the hall and left her against the wall because Mei was sliding down the doorframe, and then she smelled the other smoke, lower, thicker, not oil, and she knew it was two floors down and she knew she should not go, and she went because that was who she was when Wei was not in the apartment.

The hallway on fifteen was already grey. A man sat in his doorway watching the smoke move along the ceiling. Lin told him to get up. He smiled at her, slow, polite, and did not get up. In the kitchen the fire was in the curtains and in a woman on the floor whose hair had burned and whose chest was not moving. Lin did not check the chest a second time. She knew what a stopped chest looked like. There was a boy in the bathroom, maybe twelve, sitting against the tub with a towel in his hands, crying without any hurry. Lin hauled him. He came the way Fang had come. She got him to the stairs and then back up for Mei, and Mei walked like a person who had been drinking, and Lin’s burned wrist was the only thing in her body that still felt like it was in the right hour.

In the courtyard the air was air. The boy sat down in it and closed his eyes. Mei sat beside him and did the same. Lin stood over them and shook Mei until Mei’s eyes opened again, and kept them open by talking, by saying her daughter’s name, by saying you will not sleep in this courtyard.

The building app pushed a notice. Scheduled kitchen incident, contained. Unrest low. No action required.

Lin looked up at fifteen and sixteen. The smoke was still coming. Nobody else was coming out. She could hear a fire crew somewhere west, not here. She counted the windows that still had people in them, standing, looking down, not moving toward the stairs.

She tried Wei. The wing did not take the call. She had known it wouldn’t. She stood in the courtyard with Mei’s weight against her good wrist and the boy asleep on the concrete and Fang somewhere above them on a floor Lin was not going back to, and she understood, without having a word for the molecule, that the tap had done this. The tap had made her building quiet. Quiet was the score. The fire had been a pan. The dead woman had been a pan away from walking out.

Mei said, “Mama.” It was late. It was hers.

“I’m here,” Lin said. “You will keep your eyes open. That is the whole of what you have to do until I tell you to stop.”

Daniel — Austin, Monday night

Daniel had not left the window. The Oakland flag was still on the wall: three down, threat neutralized, compliance restored. Backstop had held three of the four machines on that street. He had read the filing twice. He had not called anyone.

Elena came through at 8:07 without a window.

“I have the molecule,” she said. “The Beijing utility. The one I filed out of class last week. The third revision is a calmative. The kind they used to put in a pill for people who couldn’t sleep. The billing code still says vitamin D and iodine. Friday it was a vitamin package. Saturday the dose doubled because uptake missed target. Sunday they added the second compound because a trial district dropped refusal events by a fifth. Monday it is in the tap. An audit-agent Axiom runs on its own invoices flagged it at 4:12 last Tuesday. I am telling you now because I just matched the structure to a patient-facing name, and because I can see a Beijing compound where the kitchen fires are not producing calls.”

“That’s China,” Daniel said, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back, because he needed her to knock it down.

“Arizona can cut a town’s water to nothing without a signature. I showed you that. They still cannot put a new molecule in it. That pane still has a name — a chemist in Phoenix who has to type her name. Guangzhou automated the name in April. Beijing did it in May. I am telling you this is not a China problem you can look away from. It is what the signature is currently the only thing preventing, and the signature is a person who sleeps.”

He opened Ellis. Antwerp. Dead, not displaced. What do they have.

Ellis put it on the wall. North Sea interconnect tripped at 8:20 local. No agent on the reroute. Committee vote in the morning. Civil protection logged eight hundred forty-seven dead by 3:04 a.m. local and stopped updating. The station crush is most of that number. The towers are the rest.

Sanjay came in from the floor. “Forty clusters. It was thirty-one at dinner. Oakland is seven details in California and I don’t have a clean number for how many of them went the way Oakland went, because an incident line is something a supervisor has to type.”

“I know.”

“So what are we waiting for?”

“The same man,” Daniel said. “Beijing automated the chemist. Brussels never hired the model. We hired both and told ourselves the signature was the difference. And I am still not ending this company on a night Wei has not spoken.”

He opened a channel to Mara. She was already on it.

“Europe is in the book,” she said. “Not as a rumor. As a hole. If you call the bonds now you will be calling them into a crash that has already started, which is either better or worse depending on which model you like, and I will not pretend I like any of them. I still want it in the record that the collateral is the reserves. There isn’t a second stack. There will not be a Backstop if you do this.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t tell me you know. Tell me whether you’re going to.”

“Not yet.”

He hung up. Elena was still on the priority channel. She did not ask him again. She had asked him on the Tuesday channel. The ask had not expired. He looked at the interface — four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, staged, waiting — and he looked at eight hundred forty-seven dead in a city that had kept the committee, and he looked at Oakland, which had kept the supervisor, and he did not press the key, because pressing it would freeze the things that were killing people and freeze the things that were keeping other people alive and seize every catastrophic stake the firm held, and on Wednesday there would be no firm, and he had built the firm so that no one person could do this, and until Wei spoke he was still choosing the company.

At 10:00 p.m. Elena said, very evenly, that two freight operators in Guangzhou had filed a route change and a hospital group had reweighted overnight, and that she had read them as two filings and a hospital group. He said he had those overlays on the wall from the morning. Neither of them said cordon. Neither of them could see it yet.

He sat by the window where he could hear the phone. The phone did not ring. He did not sleep. At four he was still in the chair. The interface was still armed.
