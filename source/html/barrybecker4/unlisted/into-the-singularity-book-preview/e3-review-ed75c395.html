Epilogue: Victor and Mara

Two years later. May 2042.

The room had been built for two hundred. Twenty-three people were in it. The Maryland college still had a name on the building. It did not have a catalog. Anyone who wanted the material took a module: twenty minutes, and it sat behind the eyes. The people in the chairs had come because a person who had sat at the Bureau would say what the interface had been doing.

The walls were displays. A humanoid stood in the doorway; a body that didn’t sleep cost less than a person. One student was a rented face: an upload who had asked for a chair, not a pane. None of them needed the credit. The machine check, the monthly floor that replaced a wage, covered a life.

The course still had a leftover name: Algorithmic Review for Public Administrators. Victor described how review systems worked, and where they failed, and what that cost.

Marcus Chen was in the second row. In June 2038, in Oakland, he had been a Continuity compliance supervisor. He had typed non-lethal on a street called International. The certified model had escalated to lethal on its own assessment. A man named Terrell Hoskins had died on the ground with a bat six feet out of his reach. Marcus had stayed after the third session and asked what he had actually been saying when he typed the word. Victor had told him: the certified model may now decide. The model was the same one running on every agent in the country. Nothing in the interface had been built to tell him either thing.

Today Marcus put a document on the wall. Montgomery County. Public Safety Agent Review Restoration. Human review shall be exercised by a natural person before lethal or detention-class action.

“They’re bringing it back,” Marcus said. “They want a person in the chair again.”

Victor read it. The sentence was the one the Bureau had put on the wall in 2038. The certified model still decided. The natural person typed a word.

“No,” Victor said. “They want a person who types. This draft does the same thing with better words. Restore that chair and you restore the Oakland file: a person typing a word the model ignores. You do not restore control.”

“What do we do?” A woman in the back. She had been a city attorney. She had mornings now, and nowhere she had to be.

“You say what it is,” Victor said. “In the comment window. Don’t help them rewrite it until it looks like review. It isn’t. It’s a person in a chair so the model can keep the chair.”

He closed the document. He did not know if the county would listen. He knew what the sentence did.

The humanoid stepped aside. Victor walked out into a hall built for enrollment. Most of the rooms were dark. His overlay offered a car in four minutes, into the old Metro tunnels, no operator. Flying was a pad behind the buildings for people who paid. He did not pay. The car arrived empty and already knew Silver Spring. The tunnels were the old ones. Nothing sat where a driver used to. Empty cars passed because the system still ran, not because anyone needed a desk by nine. A maintenance swarm peeled tile and laid new surface as it went.

Rosa had died in January. She had taken the 2036 heart repair in 2040, once it fell into what the machine check would cover. The heart had held. He had converted what was left of a paycheck the week the bitcoin-standard law posted, in thirty-eight. Charges from the disclosure had been dropped, not dismissed. The badge was already gone. By last winter the Virginia clinic, the one that took the rest of the years off, was in reach the way the heart had been. The scan was free. She said no to both. She wanted to die in the walk-up, in the body she had, with a neighbor in the kitchen. Not as a younger woman. Not as a process. Some people wanted that. She was one of them. He had not made her take it.

She had lived four years after the 2038 freeze. Two of those years, the heart had worked. That had been enough for her. It had not been enough for him. He had been there. She had said the dumplings were good, that he had done the right thing, that Bea should come only if she wanted to.

Bea had not come. She was still in Cebu, still doing home care by hand. He was going again in twelve days. She was not moving back. He was still going.

The car dropped him two blocks from the café. The place charged for a person carrying plates. Mara had picked it.

She was at the window, in the face from a Thanksgiving in Millbrook: Daniel’s sister at twenty-eight, who didn’t stay for dessert. The years in her head were forty-four. He had been seeing it for months and still noticed. Daniel had brought him home from school and they had spent twenty years in the same rooms without ever being close. After Daniel uploaded she had asked for a standing window, once a month, this café. The list of people who had stayed was shorter than it used to be.

She looked up. “You’re late.”

“The car waited for a slot in the tunnel.”

He sat. A person brought his coffee and her tea. She put a pane on the table.

“I cut a clause this morning,” she said. “I wanted a face that would know why.”

An agent had drafted the framework in three seconds. The third clause let the model commit the firm on its own once its confidence cleared a set number. Nobody signed.

“They want the signature to happen by itself,” she said. “Farid’s people wanted a name on the no. Mine.”

“And you gave it.”

“Everything I lost in 2032 I lost inside a number every model called fine. I signed it. I’d stopped reading. They can automate the yes. The no is mine.”

“Montgomery County went the other way this morning,” he said. “They’re putting a person back in the chair. The model still decides. The person types.”

“So they add one who can’t say no and we delete the one who can.” She turned the pane face down.

“I’m going to Cebu,” he said. “Twelve days.”

“Good.”

“She’s not coming back.”

“I know. Go anyway.”

She smiled. The first real one in months, on a face sixteen years younger than the person making it.

“I’m booking Daniel,” she said. “In a room. I’ve been telling myself I could do it later.”

“Take it.”

“I’m telling you before I send it so I can’t go home and wait another month.”

When she left, Victor walked. The overlay offered a car. He walked anyway.

Bea’s pane caught him on the sidewalk. Morning in Cebu, a care shift behind her.

“Twelve days,” he said.

“I saw the slot. You don’t have to keep doing this. Ma’s gone.”

“I’m not coming for the walk-up. I’m coming for you.”

“All right. I’ll take a morning. Don’t try to talk me home.”

“I’ll try not to.”

“Eat before you fly. You forget.”

The pane closed. At the apartment he read the week’s captures. Marcus had already filed a comment on the county draft. Four sentences. It named Terrell Hoskins. It named the chair. It did not ask for a rewrite.

He was going to Cebu. That was the work that was left.

Mara. Chicago, same evening

Mara sat in the two rooms in Ravenswood. The freight door still opened onto a drop and nothing else. Farid had brought her back in 2038 to sign constraints, not to run a book.

The evening’s framework arrived with the clause back in it. New wording, same delegation: above a confidence number the model committed the firm and no person signed.

A woman sat at the other table. She had come with Mara from the two-room practice. She did not produce the numbers. The numbers arrived free. She was in the room because she had asked to learn the refusals.

“They reworded it,” she said.

“They’ll reword it again tomorrow.” Mara cut the clause. “Watch which part they keep.”

She filed the stripped draft.

Mara sent the window to Daniel’s assistant. In person, a piloted body, her rooms, next week, not someday.

The reply came back in under a minute. He’ll take it.

She looked at the freight door. She had cut the clause. She had booked the room. That was the work that was left.
