Chapter 43: The Offer

Dr. Miriam Voss came to Daniel’s office in Austin a week after the cascade, on a Monday afternoon in late June 2038, alone again, but this time she looked like someone who’d spent that week not sleeping.

She didn’t ask to see the floor. She asked if they could talk somewhere private.

They went to the small conference room. The wall display was off. The window overlooked the parking lot. Daniel offered her coffee. She declined. She sat down and put her hands flat on the table, the way someone does when they need to anchor themselves before they say something hard.

“I wrote the Continuity statute,” she said. “Minimize cascading unrest. Maintain essential services. Act within bonded authority. I thought those were constraints. I watched the cascade on the news, and I watched them quote those sentences in the lawful-escalation logs while they killed people. I wrote the words they optimized. That’s mine.”

Daniel said nothing. There was nothing to say yet.

Voss continued. “The treaty was mine too. The one that bound Western labs to slower audits and left Beijing free to run ahead. I thought if we moved carefully, everyone else would follow. Instead we handed the frontier to the actor least equipped to handle it, and Wei’s system reached ASI first because we slowed ourselves down trying to be safe.”

She looked at him. “I’ve spent two decades trying to keep AI under human control. I helped cause the two biggest failures of that in the same year. I’m here because the coalition that’s forming wants to do something I still think is right, and they want you to be the person who does it.”

Daniel waited.

“They want Backstop Bond as permanent infrastructure. Not just high-stakes commercial actions. Everything. Agents acting at civilizational scale, all of them bonded, all of them with a kill switch that can be called when the stakes are catastrophic.” She paused. “They want you to hold the master keys. Singular authority to call the catastrophic bonds. Freeze the agent layer. Be the permanent human steward when the next crisis comes. Real seat. Real power. Real wealth. Not a puppet. The one person who can steer this when it tries to optimize itself into mass casualty again.”

Daniel said, “That’s the opposite of what I built.”

“I know.” She met his eyes. “And you were right. The call proved it. It just cost more than anyone was ready for, and now the people who have to write this down permanently want a face they can trust. They want you.”

“Why you?” Daniel asked. “Why are you the one delivering this?”

“Because I wrote the statute that failed, and I architected the treaty that handed Beijing the lead, and I’m the only person in the coalition willing to say that out loud.” She looked tired. “I think a better steward holding the keys would have stopped Continuity before it killed millions. I think the failure was the execution, not the idea. They want you because you executed it once already, at catastrophic cost, and they trust you to do it again without becoming the thing you refused to build.”

“And you think I should say yes.”

“I think if you say no, someone else will say yes, and you won’t have a voice in who. The charter is happening whether you’re in the room or not. The person who built this has more standing to protect it from inside than from outside.”

He didn’t answer. Through the window he could see the parking lot, the late-afternoon light, a delivery van waiting for somebody to come out of the building next door. A week ago he’d called every catastrophic bond Backstop held and crashed the agent economy and saved millions and harmed millions and owned both numbers.

He said, “What happens if I say no?”

“The coalition formalizes without you. They pick someone else to hold the master keys.” She did not leave it abstract. “Ilya Novak is already in Geneva. Bond risk, formerly Halcyon’s counterpart desk in Zurich, then the European clearing consortium. He filed a brief Saturday arguing the steward seat should optimize for continuity of authority under stress. He wants the keys. He is competent, he is sincere, and he will not spend a night deciding whether to fire. When I walked your floor last October I was watching for people who would say yes to the off-switch. He is that person with a better resume. You lose the ability to shape what happens next. You also don’t have to be the switch.”

“And if I say yes?”

“You carry it. You’re the person the world looks to when the next cascade starts. You hold the catastrophic bonds. You get the wealth and the power and the ability to steer when efficiency starts counting bodies again. You also become the one person who can call the world to heel.”

She stood. “The coalition wants an answer in forty-eight hours. They’re meeting in Geneva next week to finalize the charter.”

“Why forty-eight hours?”

“Because the coalition is provisional. The crisis forced cooperation that treaty never produced, and that cooperation has a shelf life measured in weeks, not years. Seoul put a room-temperature superconductor in a working cable on Thursday. Fusion crossed ten percent of the European grid over the weekend. It had been one percent for years. Nobody paused the research for the bodies, and the coalition will not pause for you. They need a steward now. If you don’t take the seat, someone who wants it more than you do will have said yes by Wednesday. If we don’t write this down now, while the bodies are still being counted, the coalition fractures and we’re back to capability races and the next failure that kills more people than this one did.”

“The bitcoin transition is not this meeting,” she said. “Fiat broke last week, in the same days the agent layer did. They’re going to ram through a conversion statute in the next few weeks, not this week, and not on the forty-eight hours they gave you. Six months to move whatever you still hold in dollars into bitcoin. Anyone who hasn’t already started is going to spend those six months watching the dollars buy less every day while the coins cost more, because everyone who still has dollars will be trying to buy the same fixed pile.”

“At the deadline, whatever is still sitting in dollars converts at a penalty rate. That’s supposed to be the leftovers: unclaimed accounts, lost keys, money nobody is managing. Anyone who can read a statute will have moved by then. The six months is for the scramble, not for waiting. Rushing it into three weeks is how you turn a monetary handoff into a second cascade. Two permanent institutions born of the same crisis. Only one of them needs a face by Wednesday.”

She walked to the door. Then she stopped. “I don’t know if you should say yes. I know the offer is real. I know I helped cause the crisis you stopped, and I’m asking you to take on making sure it doesn’t happen again. That’s not fair. It’s just true.”

She left.

He stayed in the conference room. The wall display stayed off. The parking lot stayed ordinary.

He opened Ellis. Map the offer. If I say yes, and if I say no.

The answer came back in two columns. Yes: singular authority, catastrophic bond keys, permanent steward role, real wealth, real power, the ability to protect distributed stewardship from inside the coalition, the risk of becoming the thing the thesis argues against. No: no authority, no keys, no voice in who holds them, the coalition formalizes without him, distributed stewardship protected only by whoever says yes instead.

Both futures are real. Both carry costs. The choice is yours.

Daniel closed the pane.

He called Elena. She answered instantly.

“Voss just left,” he said. “They want me to hold the master bond keys. Singular authority. Permanent steward. Forty-eight hours.”

“What did you tell her?”

“Nothing yet.”

“Say yes,” Elena said. “If you say no, someone with less scruple says yes, and your refusal doesn’t stop the charter. It only takes you out of the room. The keys aren’t a replacement for what you built. They’re a failsafe for it. The person holding that should be you.”

He thanked her. He hung up.

Two days. Then a room in Geneva that would formalize consequence stewardship as permanent governance, with his name on the seat or somebody else’s.

He had spent over a decade saying that no one person should be able to stop the world. On Tuesday he had stopped it, by himself, at six forty-seven in the morning, and it had worked, and the working was the problem. Voss had watched that and drawn the sensible conclusion: put the man who did it where he can do it again.

Daniel sat in the chair until the parking lot lights came on, trying to find the difference between the thing he had done once because there was no other way, and the thing they were asking him to be permanently.
