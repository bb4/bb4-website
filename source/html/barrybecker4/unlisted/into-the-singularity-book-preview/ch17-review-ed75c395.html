Chapter 17: Reassignment

The notice came in the spring of 2030, in the same flat, over-translated register as every other notice Wei had read since the move to the new building: Congratulations on your selection for advancement to Tier Three research status. It offered no mention of what Tier Three actually meant — just a badge color and a wing of the building Wei had never had a reason to walk past.

His director delivered the news in person, which was itself a kind of tell. Good news traveled by email. The news that required watching your face traveled by a person sent specifically to watch it.

“You should be proud,” the director said, in the tone of a man who had delivered this exact sentence to this exact kind of employee more than once. “Very few people get asked. It reflects well on your work, and on the department that trained you.”

“What does the work involve?”

“You’ll be briefed on-site, after the paperwork clears.” A pause, the specific pause of a man choosing which true thing to hand over. “I can tell you it matters. I can tell you the institute is proud to be doing it here instead of somewhere slower. That’s most of what I’m cleared to say myself.”

Wei signed what he was given to sign — a longer document than the one he’d signed four years ago, denser, with a clause near the bottom requiring him to report, within forty-eight hours, any contact from a foreign national that touched on the substance of his work.

He read that sentence three times before he let himself keep going, because he had a person in his life that sentence was obviously, structurally aimed at. He was fairly sure the institute had no idea who Daniel was. He was equally sure that not knowing wasn’t the same thing as it not mattering.

The Tier Three badge came with a desk assistant the institute issued and logged — watched, he assumed. He used it for scheduling and literature pulls and nothing that touched Daniel’s name. Friendship was not a query he was going to run through a machine that reported home.

There was a second signature line under a section titled Family Notification and Household Disclosure. It listed everyone he lived with by name and asked him to confirm, in writing, that they understood he could no longer discuss the specifics of his work — not the project, not the building, not the names of anyone he worked alongside.

Lin’s name was already typed in the field when the document arrived. That meant someone, somewhere, had already looked her up before Wei had said a word to her about any of it. That fact held his attention longer than anything else in the document. Then he signed it, because the alternative to signing it had never actually been presented as an alternative.

He was not only signing a secret. Tier Three was how you stayed in the district school that still had a seat, and on the housing list the institute ran. The NDA was the smaller document.

The new wing had its own cafeteria, better than the one Wei had described to Daniel as merely “bigger,” and its own badge readers, and its own particular quiet. Not silence, exactly. More the specific hush of a building full of people who had each individually decided to stop asking each other what they were working on.

Fourteen people at the orientation had known each other from somewhere before this room. Nobody said so. Nobody introduced themselves by their previous project. Nobody asked.

Wei’s own project, once he was finally briefed on it, turned out to be exactly interesting enough to justify everything the paperwork had cost him: a training architecture two generations past anything he’d touched before, scaling faster than any system he’d helped build. The actual frontier instead of a respectable distance behind it.

He understood, sitting through the briefing, why the institute called this an honor and meant it. He also understood that the awe and the fact that he now knew slightly too much to sleep were going to have to share the same body.

His own doctorate had taken seven years and produced one narrow fact about a class of optimization problems that a few dozen people in the world had ever needed. He had been proud of it. He was still proud of it.

Two years ago a machine had proved the Riemann hypothesis, the problem every mathematician of his generation had been raised to think of as permanent. Two more of the famous ones had gone since, and the second had been a smaller story than the first. The one he had stopped to read properly was Yang–Mills — the missing existence proof for the field theory the whole standard model had been built on, which physicists had used for decades to predict experiments that came out right every time, with nothing rigorous underneath it. The proof arrived now. A foundation delivered to a building that had been occupied since before he was born, and the building had never once waited for it.

Nobody would spend seven years on a narrow fact again. There was no version of that apprenticeship left: you handed the system a class of problem and it came back with forty candidates the same afternoon. Wei did not think of this as a theft, and he did not think the beauty had gone out of it either. He had only noticed, sometime in the last two years, that the part of his training he had been proudest of was the part that was now free, and he did not yet know what that left him with.

The state media framing, when he caught it that same week on the train home, used a phrase that had been showing up more often lately: The world’s caution has become our opportunity.

Western labs were throttled by their own governments’ new safety accords, export controls, alignment review boards that could pause a training run for months. All of it was framed, on the state broadcast, not as prudence but as fear. The kind of fear a confident nation didn’t need to indulge.

Wei had read the actual treaty text once, out of professional curiosity, through a workplace relay that logged the query. He would not open it again. It hadn’t struck him as fear. It had struck him as people trying, imperfectly, to build a guardrail before the car reached the curve. He did not say this out loud to anyone, including himself, more than once.

He saw Han at the Tier Three elevator bank on his second week, badge already scanned, three people ahead of him in the line. Han had lost weight. He had the face of a man who slept in the building. Han’s badge was blue, the same as Wei’s new one, which told Wei more in half a second than four months of silence had told him.

Han saw him. Something crossed his face — recognition, then something that closed over it almost as fast, a door shutting itself without a hand on it.

“Han.”

“Wei.” Han said it like a name he was required to say, polite, without the warmth two desks’ worth of shared coffee runs should have earned. “Congratulations on the tier.”

“You too, apparently.” Wei kept his voice light. “I looked for you. Nobody would say where you’d gone.”

“I’m sure they wouldn’t.” Han glanced at the badge reader, at the small clustered knot of people who were technically not listening and absolutely were. “It’s a good building. Better cafeteria.”

He said it with no irony at all, which was somehow worse than if he’d meant it as a joke. He stepped into the next car alone. The doors closed on whatever the rest of that sentence might have been, and Wei stood there a moment before he remembered he was also waiting for an elevator.

He did not tell Lin about it that night. He understood, not telling her, that he was doing the exact thing he’d once caught himself doing to Daniel — trimming an answer down before he’d consciously decided to.

Han was not gone. Han was not fired, not disappeared. Han had simply been promoted into the same quiet that was now Wei’s own address. That was, in its way, a worse answer than the ones Wei had spent four months not letting himself imagine, because it meant the quiet didn’t lose people. It absorbed them.

Lin noticed something at dinner that night, the way she noticed most things about him lately without saying so directly.

“You’ve been somewhere else since Tuesday,” she said, not a question, sliding the rice toward him.

“New project. Still figuring it out.”

“That’s the sentence you used for the last one too.” She said it lightly, not an accusation. “I signed a form with my own name on it last week that a courier brought to the door. Nobody’s ever explained to me what I actually agreed to.”

“You agreed that I won’t tell you things, and that you’ll be fine with that.” He heard how it sounded the moment it left him — worse, spoken plainly, than it had looked typed into a form. “I’m sorry. It’s not a good trade. I didn’t design it.”

“I know you didn’t.” She let it go there, the way she let most things go these days, filing it instead of fighting it. Then, as if it were the same form: “And he doesn’t come here. You know that.”

She meant Daniel. “I know.”

Marriages absorbed things too, apparently. He was learning that from more than one direction lately.

Mei needed her school bag packed for a field trip the next morning, an errand Wei had claimed for himself specifically because it required nothing of him but his hands and his daughter’s running commentary.

“Bao Yu says the museum has a real dinosaur, and I said no it doesn’t, it has a fake dinosaur that’s shaped like a real one, and he said that’s the same thing and I said it is not the same thing. Baba, is it the same thing?”

“It is extremely not the same thing.” He folded her rain jacket into the bag the way she liked it, rolled instead of flat, a preference she had held with total conviction since she was five and had never once explained.

Bao Yu’s mother had put the boy on a private cognitive-prep waitlist that required a deposit equal to three months of Wei’s old salary — brain training sold as a product, for families who could pay. Mei had absorbed that fact the way she absorbed museum arguments, as evidence the world had rules that did not apply evenly.

“A fake dinosaur that’s shaped like a real one is called a model. A real dinosaur is called a fossil, and there aren’t very many of those left. That’s exactly why the museum uses a model.”

“That’s what I said.” She said it with the specific satisfaction of a seven-year-old whose entire worldview had just been externally ratified. “Bao Yu is going to be so annoyed.”

“Try not to enjoy that part too much.”

“I’m going to enjoy it a normal amount.” She zipped the bag herself, insisting. She looked up at him with the specific, unbothered directness of a child who had not yet learned that a question could be dangerous. “Why do you look tired today?”

“Work was interesting today. Interesting is tiring sometimes.”

“That’s a weird kind of tired.”

“It is,” he agreed, and did not say anything else about it. She seemed satisfied with that the way she was satisfied with most of his non-answers these days. Still young enough to trust that a parent’s silence meant nothing important rather than something I can’t hand you.

He zipped the outer pocket for her, on principle, checking the water bottle was actually sealed this time.

The go board was still on the table from the night before. Wood. Two bowls of stones. Her black stones in a crooked line on the left.

“I packed,” she said. “I get a game.”

He sat. She put a stone in the corner he had left empty. He had been adding stones on the left. She hadn’t. He lost the corner. He laughed.

“Again tomorrow,” she said, and carried the bag to the door for morning.

He thought that this was the actual correct proportion of his day — a dinosaur argument and a lost corner against nine hours of a building that had eaten a friend’s whole personality and called it an honor. He was under no illusion the ratio would hold much longer.

The traffic log came across his new terminal near the end of that same week, a routine anomaly report he’d been assigned to triage as part of his onboarding, mostly busywork meant to get him used to the tooling before the real work started.

Most of the flagged entries were nothing — misconfigured caching, a partner lab’s test environment pinging somewhere it shouldn’t. One line further down listed an unfamiliar vendor name attached to an unusually large block of outbound compute requests, routed through commercial channels rather than the institute’s own dedicated lines: Axiom Systems.

Wei had never heard of it. He filed it under probably nothing, flag if it recurs, and moved on to the next line. There were forty more entries in the queue and no version of this job where a name he didn’t recognize got to slow him down for longer than it took to write four words about it.

Daniel called that Tuesday, same as always, and asked how the new building was — he’d remembered the word building, not tier, because Wei had never given him the second word to remember. “Bigger wing this time,” Wei said. “Better badge. Same parking.”

Daniel laughed at the joke about the parking and did not ask what the badge was. He had stopped filling the holes. He moved on to something about his own week, a client who wanted the reviewers faster and a queue that didn’t sleep. Wei let himself listen to the whole thing properly, grateful, in a way he didn’t examine too hard, for a friend whose week he was still allowed to hear about in full even on a week when his own had stopped being available to anyone.
