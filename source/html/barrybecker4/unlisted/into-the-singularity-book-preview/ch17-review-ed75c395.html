Chapter 17: The Bill Comes Due

Millbrook, Ohio

Janelle stood in the detached garage with the boring-and-facing machine, Warren’s machine, the one she’d bought at scrap value after Ray let her go. She wiped the sweat off her face and told the empty garage that the order from Glen’s shop was done early. The part was inside the tolerance.

Angie had texted that morning. Rent letter came. He’s calling it a market adjustment. Twenty-two percent higher than last year.

Janelle checked her own balance and did not send it. Her own small stack, nine years of thirty-dollar Fridays, and the inherited coin she’d never touched. Same coin count as last spring. Different purchasing power. She had read Warren’s note the week Ray closed, the one that said the coin was a down payment on the shop, and she had not used it.

The crash hadn’t touched her. She had never borrowed against any of it, so there was no margin call to lose it to. And inflation, the thing eating Angie’s rent and everyone’s groceries, couldn’t eat something with a fixed supply.

She’d quit telling Angie to buy some years ago. Coming from her, a woman who already had savings, it had only ever sounded like a lecture, which is what it was. What she texted instead was Send me the new number, I’ve got overflow this month. It was not true yet, but would be by Friday. Angie would take it the way she took it every time, neither of them calling it help.

Thursday Angie had to re-verify a transit subsidy in person in Baltimore. The window had moved up without notice. Janelle had said she would drive her. She went back to the mill. Tyler was late with a part. The machine didn’t care.

Washington, D.C.

Victor Reyes picked up on a weeknight. Rosa, his mother.

“The copay is sixty-eight,” she said. “Not forty-eight. Sixty-eight.”

Victor already had the benefit file open before she finished. The subsidy tier hadn’t changed. The copay formula had been adjusted for “stabilized pricing environment,” which was the Bureau’s phrase for inflation that isn’t technically hyperinflation but isn’t going away either.

“It’s the new adjustment model,” he said, and heard how hollow it sounded. “They recalibrate it quarterly now instead of annually.”

“I know what it is. I’m asking if it’s going to be ninety next time.”

His own grocery receipt from Sunday sat in his wallet: twenty-nine dollars more than October for the same cart. Congress had made the emergency relief permanent: a monthly share of what the machines earned, paid on the same rail his office sat on. People called it the machine check because Congress never agreed on a real name. Food was back. Rent was survivable. The gap between what things cost and what benefits covered kept widening in ways the check didn’t catch.

“I’ll cover the difference this month.”

“You covered it last month. And the month before.” Her voice was tired, not angry. “The machine check is real money, Victor. It shows up. It helps. But my copay went up twenty dollars in six months and the check went up eleven. I’m not asking you to fix it. I’m asking you to tell me if this is what stable looks like now.”

He thought about the phrase he’d seen in a briefing that morning: soft landing. Inflation hadn’t spiraled into hyperinflation. The relief had worked. The bottom fifty percent were mostly better off than they’d been in 2028.

But Rosa was paying sixty-eight instead of forty-eight. His sister Bea’s rent had climbed faster than her paycheck. And the people Victor saw in his queue, the ones who’d gotten the machine check, who’d survived the crash, who weren’t starving, were angrier now than they’d been when things were worse.

“It’s stable,” he said. “For a version of stable nobody’s used to yet.”

“You sound like the letter they sent me.”

“I know,” he said.

He used to be the person who could clear a flagged payment because he was in the building. Nobody sat behind the copay formula. It was a number, and his badge could not touch it.

The protest was in Baltimore on a Thursday, a municipal services building. A line of people had been told their transit subsidy required in-person re-verification, and the window had moved up without notice. Local news had been calling it civil unrest since noon. By three the scanner traffic was calling it something else.

Victor stood in the Bureau blazer with the badge clipped visible, doing the job the expanded mandate had turned into. Verify identities, cross-check duplicate claims, route edge cases to the compliance queue that had quadrupled in size since the relief went permanent.

By 3:20 someone threw a bottle at the glass door. It shattered. Baltimore PD moved in a line that had been staged for exactly this.

Janelle was twelve feet back with Angie’s wrist in her hand, pulling, trying to get her sister clear of the crush. She had driven most of a day from Millbrook so Angie could re-verify a transit subsidy in person. The window had moved up without notice. The building was locked. The appointment system was frozen.

The same week’s news feed carried a second story, below the fold: a defense appropriations bill tripled the materiel commitment to the Kesengo Corridor. A helmet-camera clip had leaked, a private contractor, mid-twenties, femoral wound, wet sounds of a man trying to hold his own leg together. He’d bled out on camera. The clip had forty million views.

Victor thought: The machine check is the one line on the page paid for out of something real, the levy on what the machines earn. The war and Rosa’s subsidy are paid for with new money, and that new money is why her copay climbs every year.

Then a man near the door swung at him.

His fist caught Victor in the mouth. Tooth against lip, the taste of metal, a corner of the tooth gone. Victor hit back before he had decided to. Knuckles into cheekbone, the give under the skin. The man dropped to one knee and came up with a shard of the broken bottle.

Theo, who had the next desk over from Victor at the Bureau, was shouting Victor’s name. The glass edge found Victor’s forearm through the blazer and the shirt and skin.

A woman next to Janelle stumbled. Janelle went down with her, concrete against palms, skin torn. She tasted blood in her mouth from the elbow she’d taken across the face getting up.

Victor was bleeding from the arm and swallowing his own blood from the split inside his mouth. Janelle was bleeding from her palm and her lip. Neither of them knew the other’s name. They would not meet for years.

By the time the parking lot cleared, twenty-three people had injuries requiring hospital care. Angie’s re-verification window had lapsed. The building stayed closed pending security review, which meant the people who had done everything right lost their window anyway.

On the ride back, Victor thought about Rosa’s sixty-eight-dollar copay. About the grocery receipt with blood on one corner now. The relief had worked exactly as designed and the bill for it was arriving in everyday purchases: groceries, transit passes, pharmacy counters.

He’d helped deliver the relief. He was helping collect the bill now.

His tongue kept going back to the chipped tooth, the new edge of it, the copper that wouldn’t rinse away. Fixing it would cost him, on Monday, forty percent more than the same work had cost in 2031.

His sister called that night, with the flight already booked.

“It’s your waiver rate,” Bea said, before he could ask. “That’s what the letter says. Not my file. My file’s clean. They flagged your hardship waivers and pulled my residency into elevated review because my name is on your household record.”

“Appeal it. I’ll write the…”

“Appeal is eighteen to thirty months with no work, Vic. I asked.” She had already decided; he could hear it. “I’m going to Lolo’s. Tita Luz says there are families in Cebu who still want a person’s hands on their mother instead of a machine’s. Bathing, turning, sheets at three in the morning. It pays badly. Nobody there is waiting on a Bureau form.”

He started to say he was sorry and she stopped him.

“Don’t. You kept your name off Ma’s case too. Look what that bought.”

Rosa’s heart medication had frozen for six days that month on an address mismatch, while Victor kept his name off her case so nobody could call it a favor. She had recovered. The cardiologist said manageable.

He had a message to Daniel open, the same short draft he had written and deleted since 2029: hey, saw something, you good? It said nothing Daniel could act on. He almost deleted it again.

He sent it anyway, with nothing from work in it. After that line he added: Bea’s going to Cebu. Compliance didn’t buy her anything. Ma’s okay enough. I don’t know what I’m asking.

Daniel wrote back in under an hour. I’m sorry. Gerald’s still on that landing. Call me if you want a person and not a pane.

Victor did not call. A vague message would not get him fired. A warning Daniel could use would. So there was nothing Daniel could do with any of it. He opened a plain text file on his own laptop and wrote one line that was about himself: Compliance is not a transaction. It never was.

Chicago

Mara watched Bitcoin recover on a screen she no longer had a position in. The Moon base had been crewed for nine months. Headline markets had recovered.

The number had bottomed at four hundred and fifty thousand in the spring. It climbed back through six hundred thousand, then seven hundred, then kept going. Fixed supply. Debasing currency. She had been right about the asset and catastrophically wrong about the borrowing, and now the chart showed both.

She had no leverage. What she had was a two-room practice in Ravenswood, fee-based work, conservative allocation memos for clients who’d survived the crash the same way Janelle had: by not borrowing.

She’d started buying again, spot, unleveraged, every week, because Janelle’s habit had survived everything hers hadn’t, and there was a lesson in that she didn’t need to be told twice. Not thirty dollars; she wasn’t a machinist on a shop wage. She took a fixed share off every fee that cleared, which came to something north of four hundred a week now and would go up as the practice brought in more. What she’d copied was the rule and not the number: every week, no borrowing, no timing, no exception for a week when she happened to have a view. Having a view was what had cost her the first position. The number climbing did not give her the position back.

Mara closed the screen.
