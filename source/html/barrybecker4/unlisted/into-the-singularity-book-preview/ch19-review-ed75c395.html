Chapter 19: Euphoria

By the fall of 2031, Mara’s forty-five inherited bitcoin, untouched in coin count since the will, were worth just under thirty-eight million dollars at the current price of eight hundred thirty-eight thousand dollars each.

She’d borrowed against them once in 2027, a conservative one-and-a-half-times bet Devon had talked her through. It would have needed the price to fall sixty percent before anyone touched her collateral. She’d let the loan grow with the collateral in 2029, same ratio, bigger base. Since then she’d pushed the ratio itself higher twice more.

The account Devon’s desk carried for her now sat at just over seventy-five million dollars, roughly twice what the coins alone were worth, nearly seven times what their father’s entire estate had been appraised at the week Carol Weiss read the will.

The vote came out of Buenos Aires a little after eight that evening. Argentina’s congress, after a year and a half of currency crisis, declared bitcoin legal tender alongside the peso, wages and taxes and contracts all payable either way.

Argentina was a G20 economy with a real central bank. When a country that size did it, nobody could call it a curiosity anymore.

The market didn’t step this time. It ran. Mara watched the number climb from her kitchen counter, the same counter, the same mug of tea going cold, and felt something she recognized as disappointment at how little the feeling had left to surprise her with.

She texted Devon anyway.

Saw it, he wrote back. You want the hour again, or are we past that part.

We’re past that part.

Give me twenty minutes. I want the actual liquidation math in front of me before I say yes to anything.

When he called back, it wasn’t the dry voice she remembered from Brazil. It was flatter, a man reading figures off a screen he didn’t like the look of.

“Tell me the actual number. Out loud.”

“Seventy-five million dollars. A little over.”

“Say the leverage, too.”

“Two times, roughly.”

“At one-and-a-half times, you needed the price to fall sixty percent before I’d ever call you about margin. At two times, it’s thirty. That’s not a small difference. That’s most of your cushion.”

“I heard the number, Devon.”

“Then hear the rest of it. Every cycle falls less than the one before. The crashes I started out watching took seventy, eighty percent off the top; the last one barely got past thirty. And it’s true, the asset’s too big now to move the way it used to. I believe it. So does every client I have, which is why every one of them is levered the way you are. Three years ago it gave back a third of itself between the ’28 top and the spring you rolled the loan bigger. At one-and-a-half times that was nothing to you. At two times I’d have sold you out of it.”

She thought of a sentence her father had written to a man named Russ a decade before any of this: the money that scares you into a bad decision isn’t money, it’s a leash.

“Noted,” she told Devon. “I’m not doing anything different tonight.”

“I know you’re not. I’m calling so that when the drop comes and it doesn’t look like much, you remember I said thirty percent.”

Farid wanted her in his office by nine the next morning. He had the Argentina story up on one screen and a slide deck open on another.

“The partners want a real recommendation. What percentage of a client’s portfolio should actually be in bitcoin, at what risk tolerance? We’ve run a two-paragraph disclaimer since I got here. After last night that’s not going to hold up as policy anymore. They want a real memo by Thursday. They want your name on the first draft.”

“My name.”

“You’ve been saying some version of this thesis at every review for three years.”

He didn’t apologize for the jokes at those reviews, but something in his face acknowledged they had aged badly.

She should have felt something bright about this, three years of being the correct one in a room that hadn’t wanted her to be. Instead she felt the same thin flatness from the night before. Being right too early had been the wound. Being right on schedule didn’t heal it so much as make it harder to point at.

“I’ll have a draft Thursday,” she said.

“Whatever you’re personally carrying in this space, keep the disclosure current. I don’t need to know the size. I need the paperwork to already exist.”

“It’s current,” she said.

The Fort Knox bill she’d been half-tracking since the spring finally cleared committee that same week. Congress voted to revalue the gold sitting on the Treasury’s books, carried at forty-two dollars an ounce since Nixon. Gold traded that week at over nine thousand. Congress marked the difference to market and routed the room it opened up, without issuing a dollar of new debt, into buying bitcoin for the strategic reserve.

The reserve itself had been sitting there since 2026, seized coins the Justice Department had never gotten around to auctioning, parked on the books and defended by nobody. This was the first time the government had voted to actually buy any. Two years after Brazil, which was the only reason the bill had the votes at all.

Nobody outside three financial trade publications ran it as a headline. Mara thought this was the actual story and Argentina was the spectacle.

Then she opened a blank document for Thursday’s memo. The desk AI offered a draft in under a minute, clean, cited. She read it once, closed it, and wrote her own from the top. She trusted it with the numbers. She did not yet trust it with the sentence that would have her name on it.

Inflation hadn’t really gone anywhere since the mid-2020s. The Fed kept announcing it was fighting the thing, and kept being right about that in exactly the sense a man announcing he’s fighting the tide is right.

It could, in principle, raise rates high enough to actually kill inflation. It could not do it at the debt-to-GDP level the country had been carrying without turning the interest on the debt into a second, faster fire.

A system built this way could survive inflation indefinitely. What it could not survive was real deflation, prices falling, because falling prices made every dollar of that debt heavier.

So a crash, whenever one came, would never be allowed to finish being a crash. It would get printed away, every time. That was the actual bet underneath her own bet, the sentence she’d never said out loud because spoken aloud it sounded less like a thesis than a prophecy.

She texted Daniel that night.

Saw Argentina. Assume you did too.

Saw it. How’s the capital conversation.

Closer to just next year now. When it happens, you’re still first call.

Don’t let Sanjay get too far ahead of himself. Currencies still have bad months.

Look at you, being the responsible one.

Don’t get used to it.

Daniel called Thursday morning before the markets opened.

“Quick update. Backstop Bond went live yesterday. We don’t review calls anymore. We make the company post bitcoin in escrow before a system is allowed to act unsupervised. If it does harm, the money pays the people it landed on. The pivot’s done.”

“Bond. Not review.”

“Not review. We underwrite the call now. It’s live.”

She thought about that. Her brother’s company, rebuilt, now holding bitcoin as reserves. Collateral. Not a bet. The opposite of what she’d done to the same coins.

“Congratulations,” she said. “A year and change to pivot the whole thesis. That’s real.”

“Had to. The old model died in 2030 and took the reviewer bench with it.” He paused. “I’m not calling about capital. The premiums fund the reserves. I don’t need a round.”

“We’ll see what my seat looks like next year,” she said, and meant it as generously as she could. “Glad it’s live.”

She hung up. He’d rebuilt Backstop as surety, money standing behind a machine being wrong, while she’d been riding a bull market at two-times leverage. In 2029 he’d talked like he might need her. Now he didn’t. The relief came with something smaller underneath it: he was using her asset the way their father had, and she was using it the other way, and only one of those would survive a bad month.

That night the number on her screen was still climbing. Devon’s thirty percent was underneath it, the distance between her and a call she didn’t want to get.

She went to bed and left the number running the way it had run every night for two years. She did not think about her father’s laptop, or a leash, or a dead man’s specific caution about the difference between conviction and fear.
