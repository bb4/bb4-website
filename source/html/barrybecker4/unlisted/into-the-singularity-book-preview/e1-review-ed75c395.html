Epilogue: Janelle

Two years later. March 2042.

The shop had been empty of people for ten years now, but Ray still owned it. He paid Janelle’s garage rent from the same bitcoin wallet that collected the automated billing, settled weekly, profits from robots milling parts on the floor Warren had built in 1996.

Janelle stood in her detached garage behind the Millbrook house, teaching Caleb a hand-fit pass Warren would have recognized. The part was titanium. The tolerance was plus-or-minus two thousandths. The job was small, billed at ninety-two satoshis, down from a hundred and six last year, down from a hundred and twenty the year before that.

Caleb noticed. “You’re charging less.”

“Fewer sats,” Janelle said. “More of everything else.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It does if you stop treating the sticker like the pay. A satoshi buys more than it did last year. I take less coin and I still come out ahead.”

Caleb looked at the invoice. “So you’re getting paid more, even though the number looks smaller.”

“That’s right. The number gets smaller. What you hold buys more.”

“Doesn’t that mean people stop spending?”

“Some do. Most don’t. You still buy what you want. The clinic and the seats still cost. That’s the only line left.”

Caleb set down the part. “That sounds like it’d kill business.”

“It kills the kind that borrows to start something it can’t hold. What’s left is slower. Work that pays what it costs.”

She picked up the part and closed her hand all the way around it. In 2038 a Continuity agent had caught her wrist in a grip rated for compliance and cracked the bone. It healed crooked. She ran the mill four years with a grip that never came all the way back. Last autumn the clinic rebuilt the bone.

She handed him the micrometer. “Check it again.”

He did. Plus one and a half thousandths. Inside spec.

“Good,” she said. “Now do the next one.”

Caleb had been in this garage since he was fifteen. He was twenty-one now, still showing up on weekends because he’d asked and she’d said yes. His father, Glen, had moved the volume work to a humanoid-staffed contractor in Columbus seven years ago. The robots could do the hand-fit work too. They had hands. They had dexterity. They stood where a body stood.

Janelle had watched one do it. She’d stood on that Columbus floor in 2035 and watched a machine fit a part the way her hands did. It hadn’t made her stop. It had made her double down.

Her phone lit: a message from Angie. Dad’s wallet just paid the quarterly. You seeing the same thing?

Janelle checked. Ray’s empty shop had paid out. Same as it did every quarter. Profits from robots, billed in bitcoin, split between Ray and the estate.

She texted back: Saw it. Same as last quarter.

Angie: I still don’t understand how this works.

Janelle: The shop runs itself. The overflow doesn’t get thinner.

She put the phone down. Caleb was on the second part. Plus one thousandth this time. Better.

“You’re getting it,” she said.

“It’s slower than I thought it’d be.”

“That’s because it’s real. Real work is slow. If it’s fast, somebody’s cutting a corner.”

Caleb looked at the boring-and-facing machine. The same one Warren had run for thirty years. The same one Janelle had bought from Ray at scrap value in 2027, after a scheduling system called TruePath had automated the judgment in her old job and the shop had let her go.

“Why do you keep doing this?” Caleb asked.

“Doing what?”

“Teaching me. Hand-fitting. Tolerance work. The robots can do it. Glen already sent the volume to Columbus. We’re leaving in a month. You could be packing.”

Janelle set down the part she’d been inspecting. “I spent eleven years on this. You asked to learn it. Both.”

“But I’m not going to use it at Glen’s. There is no Glen’s, not for this.”

“You’ll use it when someone brings a one-off they don’t trust to a robot. Spec’s wrong, material’s strange, or they want a person on it. That work still exists. We’re taking it with us.”

Caleb nodded. He went back to the third part.

Janelle thought about the thirty-dollar weeks. She’d started in 2024, when a dollar was still money and Warren was still alive to argue with. Twenty dollars here, forty there, then thirty every Friday, spite and habit and faith in a dead man’s discovery, in that order. She didn’t buy bitcoin anymore. There was nothing left to buy it with. Pay arrived in it. The overflow Ray sent her arrived in it. She just didn’t spend some. The old name for the habit had outlived the dollar.

The holding was larger, in coin count, than the one bitcoin Warren had left her. The fraction had mattered more than the total. The discipline had mattered more than the bet. She’d kept it through two collapses. She didn’t need a Friday transfer to prove it now. The not-spending was the habit.

She checked her phone: a news feed. Mars colony expanding: second wave of settlers arriving April 2042. Robots built the infrastructure. Humans following once verified safe. Terraforming to begin once surveys confirm the planet has no native life.

She already had a seat. So did Caleb. Robots first, people second. She’d been sending Daniel asteroid-claim notices and landing clips since the first dusty feed in 2038, and she had never dressed the want up as a job that needed her hands. The robots could have finished Mars without a single person on it. They would terraform it the same way, once the last survey said nothing lived there. She wanted to stand on that ground while they were still making it. That was the reason. It had always been the reason.

The clinic was Thursday. Rejuvenation. She’d told them twenty-one. Aging was a solved problem for anyone who could pay, and she could pay; the appointment would cost a rounding error of one year’s overflow from Ray’s empty floor. She had been telling herself she was too old. That was a habit, same as the thirty dollars, and it was wrong the same way. The clinic did not touch the years in her head. It only set the body. She could be whatever biological age she wanted.

Twenty-one was Caleb’s number. She had picked it knowing that, and she was not going to tell herself she’d picked it out of the air.

Once Thursday was over, the years between her and Caleb would stop being a reason. She had not wanted anything of that kind when he was fifteen, and she had not pretended otherwise. He was twenty-one. Thursday she would be twenty-one too, in the body, and the calendar was not going to be the thing that decided. They were going. They would find out the rest there.

Caleb finished the third part. Minus one thousandth. Inside spec. Consistent now.

“Good work,” Janelle said. “You’re learning.”

“Thanks.”

“When you’ve learned it, you teach someone else. Doesn’t have to be this garage.”

“Who am I supposed to teach? Nobody’s hiring machinists.”

“Not machinists. People who want to make a thing with their hands even when nobody needs them to. You’ll find them. Some of them are going to be standing on a printed floor instead of this one.”

Caleb nodded. He cleaned the parts. He packed them.

“Glen still thinks I’m crazy,” he said.

“Glen’s allowed. You already said yes.”

He left around noon. Not for good. Thursday, then April.

Janelle stayed in the garage a minute longer. The boring-and-facing machine. The part with hand-tool marks. The tolerance job that didn’t need her to exist and existed because she’d chosen to keep doing it anyway. The mill was staying. Ray would keep the wallet. The empty shop would keep milling. She was not staying with it.

The world had crossed the singularity two and a half years ago. The Agent Charter was law. The Bitcoin standard was lived-in now, unremarkable, the thing prices and wages and rents all moved inside. Upload was ordinary. Most people were choosing it eventually, once the procedure was proven safe and free.

Janelle wasn’t uploading. She was staying in a body. She was taking that body off the planet.

Her phone lit: Daniel. Not a call. A message, from the other side of the scan. Elena says hi.

Janelle texted back: Tell her hi back. Clinic Thursday. Mars in April. I’ll send you a picture of the dirt.

Daniel had uploaded in January. She’d known it was coming. He’d told her in December, when the procedure had enough of a track record that he was sure. She’d argued against it once, gently, and then stopped. It was his choice. He’d lived a full life. He’d earned it.

She missed him. Not the way you miss someone who’s dead. The way you miss someone who can be in three rooms at once while she is in this one. He still wrote. He could still walk into the garage looking like himself, and also be with Elena. After April he could stand on Mars in a body that passed. She would still be one person on one piece of dirt.

That was the cost of staying in a body. She was paying it, and she was going anyway.

Janelle looked at the mill. She thought about Warren. The man who’d run this shop for thirty years, who’d found Bitcoin in 2013 on cryptography newsgroups left over from a graduate program he’d dropped out of, who’d kept buying when everybody said it was over.

His discovery had turned out to be the operating system for how the back half of everyone’s life worked now. The printing press was dead. The fixed-supply standard was just the money. The deflationary economy was just the economy.

Warren hadn’t lived to see it. He’d died in 2027, fifteen years before the thing he’d believed in became the thing nobody argued about anymore.

Janelle stood. She went to the house. She made lunch. She ate at the kitchen table, same chair she’d been eating in for eighteen years. She wanted the ground more than she wanted the chair.

When someone asked her why, the answer was simple: because a body was still worth something. Because hands were still worth something. Because the harder path was still a path, and she wanted to walk it somewhere that was still being made.

That was enough.
