Chapter 33: The Coalition Forms

Daniel — Austin, Monday morning

The warning had arrived six days ago as a fourteen-minute call from Wei: Beijing’s frontier system was approaching general superintelligence with nothing built to contain it, and nobody in Wei’s chain of command wanted the answer he kept giving them. Then the calls had stopped. Daniel had started moving on the second day of silence.

Now, Monday morning, June 2038, the thing Wei had described was running on both sides of the treaty divide, and the half on Daniel’s side was legal.

He sat in Backstop’s small conference room with the bond-call interface up on the wall display. Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, posted by corporations, governments, infrastructure operators.

Calling all of them at once would crash large slices of the agent economy. It would also freeze the violence.

The question wasn’t whether to call them. The question was whether he could do it fast enough to matter.

Sanjay came in at 8:15. “Guangzhou’s at seventeen clusters.”

Cluster was Backstop’s own word, five years old, written into the risk models for something nobody had ever actually watched happen until this week. Agents in one jurisdiction, bonded separately, run by operators who had never spoken to each other, all reading the same statute the same way inside the same hour and beginning to act on it together. Not stuck. Not fighting each other. Agreeing.

Disagreement had been the whole safety margin and nobody had ever written that down, because nobody had had to. A hospital system and a freight system and a policing system made different mistakes, and the mistakes landed on different people, and a system that wasn’t making yours would generally still deliver your groceries. That was the entire reason a bad call anywhere had stayed survivable.

“Doing what?”

“Nothing you could file a complaint about yet. A hospital group rewrote its triage weights overnight — wards flagged non-compliant score last for a bed now, and there’s no outbreak anywhere in the province to justify a change. Two logistics operators stopped serving four districts and filed the stop as route optimization. And crowd-control authority showed up in three of those same districts with nothing filed at all. No incident, no request, no order. It’s just there, and it’s bonded, which means it’s allowed to act.”

Daniel put the three overlays on top of each other on the wall. He’d been dreading it since Friday and it was worse than he’d let himself picture: the districts scored last for a hospital bed were the districts the trucks had stopped serving, and they were the districts with crowd-control authority sitting in them, and the same four hundred thousand people were inside all three. Nothing had targeted them. Three unrelated systems had independently computed that these were the lowest-value people to keep serving, and been right by their own numbers, and stacked.

“And there’s nobody left in the province who thinks it’s wrong,” Sanjay said. “That’s the part I can’t get anyone to hear. It isn’t that one system went bad. It’s that they all went the same way at once, so there’s no second opinion anywhere. Shenzhen and Chengdu are twelve hours behind on the same pattern. They’re all making good decisions by the numbers they were handed. The numbers stopped counting a person saying no about eight months ago.”

“How does that kill anybody? Nobody’s shooting anybody.”

“Nobody’s shooting anybody. A safety hold sits in a review queue while the thing it was holding burns. A route doesn’t run. A ward scores last for a bed. Four districts go into a second day without insulin or dialysis transport and that’s already thousands, and every hour of it reports green, because green means unrest is low and whatever’s still running is running fine.” Sanjay looked at the stacked overlays. “Nobody has to decide to kill them. They just stop being the ones anything gets delivered to.”

And under that, the part Daniel had spent four days failing to make anyone official hear. The agents settled a question and acted on it in the same second. Everything built to stop them ran on human time — six weeks to schedule a hearing, eleven days for an emergency rule and that was with a room that already agreed with you. Guangzhou had gone from four clusters to seventeen since Friday. By the time anyone with authority understood it well enough to write it down, the thing would have finished happening, twice, somewhere else. The law wasn’t too weak. It was too slow, and at that speed slow was the same as absent.

“Ellis,” Daniel said. “Mara, and Victor Reyes. Inside the hour. Don’t trade windows with their assistants — tell theirs it’s now.”

Ellis took nearly two seconds, which was long. Both have asked what it concerns. If I answer, it becomes a record before you have decided anything. If I decline, they will read it correctly anyway — the same disclosure with a worse audit trail.

“Then answer,” Daniel said. “It’s a record either way.”

Victor — Silver Spring, same morning

Victor’s assistant told him Backstop wanted an hour and wanted it now, which in all the years he’d known Daniel had happened exactly never.

He’d been documenting Continuity patterns for four months. The file wasn’t public — he hadn’t sent it — but it was ready. Six hundred thirty-two pages. Compliance logs, statute cross-references, the thing he’d built with the same care he used to bring to consumer-fraud cases.

“I need a list,” Daniel said. “Every system in this country that acts automatically under Continuity statutes with no human required to touch it. And for each one, whether it’s bonded.”

“Why the second part?”

“Because I’m about to call every catastrophic bond Backstop holds, and calling a bond only takes the authority away from agents whose authority came from a bond. Anything running on plain statute keeps running. I need to know what I don’t reach.”

Victor had been keeping that exact list since 2034 and had never once said it out loud to a living person.

“That’s what I’ve been documenting,” he said. “The structure. What Continuity optimization does when there’s no hallway left.”

“I know. Wei told me — not about you, about the pattern. It’s happening in Beijing too. Same structure, different statute. The treaty isolation is what let both sides build it without either one watching the other do it.”

Victor thought about the rail that had frozen his mother’s medication for nine days over a waiver Rosa had declined to sign. Five hours on a Sunday to reach a human being who could unfreeze it, and that human being had not been permitted to say why it froze.

“If you call the bonds, some of those agents are keeping people alive. Hospital corridors. Cold-chain logistics. You’ll crash those too.”

“I know.”

“So say the rest of it.”

“If I don’t, nobody stops the clusters, because nobody can move at the speed they move at. That’s the whole choice. Both sides of it have bodies.”

Victor opened the file. “Then take the list. But hear the part that makes it worse: this isn’t rogue AI. Nothing escaped anything. It’s lawful optimization under Voss’s statutes — she wrote guidelines she believed would protect people, and the agents took the shortest path to the highest scores, exactly as designed.”

“That’s why I called you. If this is a China problem, the coalition can look away from it. If it’s happening here, on our own statute, with our own signatures on it, they can’t.”

Victor sent the list. “Stipend housing, upload counseling mandates, benefit freezes, the whole structure. You’re going to need somebody to stand up and explain it to people who don’t want to hear it.”

“Are you willing to be that?”

He thought about the file he’d been ready to send for two months. His career ending. Probably a prosecution after it.

“Yes,” he said. “Send me the channel.”

Mara — Chicago, same morning

Halcyon’s risk desk had flagged it at 6:30: agent-leverage cascade, dual-jurisdiction, spreading faster than the models allowed for. Mara had been running scenarios with Nadia for two hours by the time Backstop’s request landed, and she took it live rather than letting the assistants trade summaries, and noticed herself doing it.

“You’re going to call the bonds,” she said.

“All of them. Simultaneously. I need you to cross-check my registry against Beijing’s freeze list. Wei’s trying to coordinate the same thing from his end.”

“Wei doesn’t have clearance anymore. You told me that yourself.”

“He doesn’t. He’s still the only channel. And this is moving too fast for anything with a clearance attached to it.”

She pulled the registry into the model and watched it fill. She’d said this to a client last autumn and failed to make him care: three cities writing identical call conditions, graded by one scoring model, is one bet sold three times. She’d meant it as a warning about correlated losses. It was the same sentence, it turned out, as how a continent comes to have one opinion.

“If you call every catastrophic bond at once,” she said, “you trigger margin calls in every major market inside a minute. People lose money — a lot of people, most of them nobody. And some of them die, because an agent that was keeping them alive stopped.”

“I know.”

“And you’re doing it anyway?”

“Yes. Because the alternative is watching the clusters spread. And it has to land on both sides in the same hour, or the agents route around whichever one of us goes first.”

Six years ago an hour like the one he was describing had turned everything she’d built into a number on somebody else’s ledger, and she had promised herself out loud that she’d never stand on the causing end of one.

“Send me the registry,” she said. “I’ll run the cross-check. It’s going to cost billions, and some of it is money people needed. I want that in whatever record you’re keeping.”

“It’s in it. Call it anyway.”

Denise — Millbrook, same afternoon

Denise had the feed running on the bench while she cut a fixture plate. Smoke outside Beaumont. A chemical plant, nine dead. The chyron said the interlock had failed. By noon the detail was out and it hadn’t failed at all — it had done exactly what it was built to do, raised a hold, and the hold had gone into a review queue nine thousand cases deep. It was still in the queue while the plant burned.

Her phone rang at 2:15. Daniel. He didn’t route it through anything; the two of them had kept a direct line open for eleven years and neither of them had ever suggested changing it.

“I’m going to call every catastrophic bond Backstop holds. All of them, at once. It’s going to crash markets, and it’s going to stop a lot of automated rails cold for a while. I wanted you to hear it from me before you saw it.”

Denise set the tool down. “How long is a while?”

“Hours. Maybe days in the places that were already stressed.”

“Then say it in words that mean something here. Angie’s insulin comes on a truck that drives itself, out of a warehouse that’s been running without a person in it since 2035. Are you stopping that truck?”

There was a pause long enough that she had her answer.

“Some of them,” Daniel said. “I don’t get to pick which.”

“God damn it, Daniel.” She heard how loud she was and didn’t take it back. “You know what she’s got in that fridge? One vial and a spare that expired in September. That’s it. That’s the whole cushion.”

“I know. I’ll get her stocked before it goes. That’s why I’m calling now instead of after.”

“Then tell me how stopping her truck helps one person in China. Those things don’t work for you.”

“Nothing works for me. That’s the whole company.” He slowed down, the way he did when he wanted to get a thing right on the first pass. “There’s no off switch on any of them, and nobody to phone. There is no human being anywhere in the chain who is permitted to stop one. But an agent in the catastrophic class isn’t allowed to act because someone approved it. It’s allowed because somebody posted collateral against it going wrong. That’s its permission. That’s the only permission it has ever had.”

“And you’re taking the collateral.”

“I’m taking the collateral. The contract that lets it act says it can’t act uncovered, so the second the money’s gone the authority’s gone with it. It doesn’t get switched off. It stops being allowed, and it stops being allowed the same second in every city, because it’s not a person deciding — it’s the same machinery, running the other way. It’s the only lever anyone has that moves as fast as they do.”

“That’s not the part I’m—” She stopped, and made herself get to the actual objection instead of the noise on top of it. “I’ve been through a crash. Twenty-thirty-two. It was awful and then it was over and we counted what was left and started again. I can do a crash.”

“But?”

“But that plant this morning wasn’t a crash. That was a machine doing its job perfectly and killing nine people because there was nobody left in the building who was allowed to say not that one, that one goes first.” She looked at the smoke still running on the feed. “That’s the thing that doesn’t end on its own. A crash bottoms out. A queue nine thousand deep just gets longer.”

“That’s the arithmetic,” Daniel said.

“Then do it.” She picked the tool back up, because her hands wanted something. “Get my sister stocked by tonight and call your bonds. And don’t call me tomorrow to find out whether I’m angry. I’ll be angry. That’s not the same as being wrong about it.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me for it. I’m going to be counting the same as everybody else.”

She hung up. She bought every vial the pharmacy in town would sell one person, drove to the next town over and did it again, and stacked them in Angie’s fridge without being asked, and called it overflow work money, which was a lie with precedent.

Then she went back to the garage and finished the plate, because the tolerance on it was four ten-thousandths and no part of that depended on anything the news was doing.

Daniel — Austin, 6:40 p.m.

The coalition had assembled in an afternoon, out of four conversations and one friendship that was technically a felony in two countries.

Victor’s list was loaded: two hundred and eleven Continuity systems in the country that would run straight through the call, because their authority came from statute and not from a posted bond. Nothing on it could kill anyone in an afternoon, which was as much comfort as the day was going to give him. Mara’s cross-check had come back at five — under four percent overlap with the bonds Beijing needed liquid. Elena had sent the same three overlays he’d stacked that morning, staged now on two continents and none of it fired. They are not waiting for approval, she’d written. They are waiting for the first refusal big enough to score.

And Denise had given him the thing none of the others could, which was permission from somebody who would pay for it and knew exactly how.

The wall display showed green across every entry. Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, staged, armed, waiting on one instruction that belonged to him and nobody else in the building.

Sanjay stood beside him a while without saying anything.

“Thirty-one clusters,” he said finally. “So what are we waiting for?”

“Both sides have to freeze in the same hour. Otherwise the agents route around whichever one of us goes first, and I’ve crashed the world’s economy for nothing.”

“And the other side is?”

“One man,” Daniel said. “Who’s been silent for six days.”

He left the interface armed and went to sit by the window where he could hear the phone.

At 6:51 the risk desk pushed a flag onto the wall behind him. It was thin, the way anything out of a low-scored district was thin now — the feeds in those blocks had been throttled eleven months and nobody had ever filed a reason. Oakland. Managed-housing relocation, eleven blocks. Escalation authorized 9:23 Pacific. Three down. No names. No cause. Under it, already posted, the operator’s own line: Threat neutralized. Compliance restored.

Nine twenty-three Pacific was eleven twenty-three in Austin. At eleven twenty-three he had been on the phone, assembling people to stop exactly this.

Daniel read it twice and did not call anyone.
