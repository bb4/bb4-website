Chapter 26: Through the Interface

By the spring of 2031 the cap didn’t need eleven minutes anymore.

A technician Elena didn’t recognize had unpacked the new array in Priya’s office two weeks earlier. He’d asked, conversationally, whether she’d noticed the decode lag getting worse lately — the delay between intent and letter on the screen — casual, like small talk about a knee that had been acting up. It had. She hadn’t mentioned it, because mentioning it felt like asking a favor of a company that had bought her doctor’s research the way a warehouse changes hands — inventory included, the people inside treated as part of the sale. Elena had decided years ago not to ask favors of Axiom Systems if she could help it.

The new cap seated in four minutes. The new decode cut her average sentence time by another third. She tested it, that Tuesday, on the only sentence that mattered to her: three lines of Daniel’s client-facing copy, forwarded that morning under the subject line tell me this is fine. All of it was built around a phrase — “adaptive escalation confidence banding” — that took her, with the new hardware, under a minute to cut down to We know when to ask a person. That’s the whole feature.

He wrote back four words. You’re still faster than legal.

The market did the thing Warren had spent fourteen years insisting it eventually would, in the spring of 2032. Daniel texted her before he called his sister, same as he always did on days that cost him something. Borrowing against the coins. Not selling. Long story.

Her reply took her most of a minute, letter by letter. Tell me Tuesday. The whole thing. And call your sister first, not after. She sat with the screen a moment after sending it, the way she’d started sitting with everything she sent lately, because the synthesized voice had stopped sounding, even to her own ear, like something translated.

Three years earlier it had been unmistakably a machine’s approximation of her. Now it was just her voice, full stop — the distinction had worn away so gradually she couldn’t have named the Tuesday it happened on if someone had asked her to. She thought about that more than she let on to anyone, Daniel included.

A voice built letter by letter inside a machine had stopped feeling like a translation. It felt like speech now. That frightened her more than she admitted — because if the machine could become her voice without a seam, maybe it could become more than her voice. She’d already run that smaller experiment, quietly, every Tuesday for years. Appendix C would ask her to run it again for real.

By the winter of 2035 the interface had stopped being a workaround and become, by any honest measure, better than what she’d had before she got sick. She could hold three threads of a document at once now — edit one paragraph while composing the next — a kind of attention her pre-diagnosis brain, two working hands and all, had never once managed even on a deadline and too much coffee.

She caught the inconsistency in the back half of Daniel’s paragraph while she was still three sentences into fixing the front half. She did not tell Colleen, and told Daniel, eventually, the way she told him everything that scared her a little: as a joke. I think the machine just made me smarter than pre-illness me. Rude of it, not asking first.

Farrah called in March of 2036 — a different Farrah, the third account manager in three years, the position rotating through people the way a support ticket rotates through a queue. Elena noticed, as she used to notice a phrase going soft in a document, that “continuity of care” had quietly become “continuity-of-experience roadmap” sometime in the last two account managers. A shift nobody had announced and everybody on the call clearly expected her not to catch.

She caught it. She didn’t say anything, because saying something wouldn’t have changed the roadmap. What it changed, six weeks later, was Appendix C — for the first time in nine years of an unsigned form and a note that said ask again in three months, it had a date attached instead of a hypothetical.

The headline broke on a Thursday, two months after that — not hers, not personal, the kind that belonged to the whole species for about eighteen hours before the cycle moved on to something else. A research consortium outside Zurich had transferred a full adult mind onto a computational substrate and kept it, by every measure anyone could agree how to take, thinking.

Colleen read it out loud from her phone at eleven o’clock, mystery novel forgotten on her lap, and asked, careful in a way Colleen was rarely careful, whether Elena wanted to talk about it.

Not yet, Elena typed. Read me the part where they explain how they know it’s thinking and not just producing the right answers to prove it’s thinking. Colleen read that part too, and it was thinner than either of them wanted. Elena filed the thinness away with everything else she’d learned not to resolve on the first pass.

It felt like the wave from years ago, the one she’d stood at the edge of the week the first real AGI landed, except this time it hadn’t stayed offshore being enormous and indifferent. It had arrived at her actual door with a date on it, and the only remaining question was whether she was going to swim into it or watch it go past.

Priya laid out the real procedure the following week, plainly, the way she’d learned years ago Elena tolerated bad news better than euphemism. Not a scan, not a copy made once and switched on cold — the array had been reading her, continuously, in increasing resolution, for nine years already. What Appendix C actually authorized was extending that same channel instead of ending it, the reading becoming, gradually and then past some point neither of them could name in advance, the whole of her.

“The biological body finishes in the chair,” Priya said. “Not abandoned. Not left alive behind you. Consumed — the way a read at this fidelity has to be. There won’t be two of you. Best current answer on continuity is that it isn’t a coin flip. It’s not nothing, either. I won’t tell you it’s nothing so you feel better walking in.”

“I know what it is,” Elena said, in the sentence it now cost her eleven seconds to compose, quiet in a room with nobody in it but Priya, who had long since stopped needing the voice to carry anything but the words. It’s the only version of continuing that’s still on offer. Everything else already stopped being an option a year ago. I’m not deciding between this and staying. I’m deciding between this and finishing without it.

Priya didn’t argue. Elena had known, asking, that she wouldn’t.

Daniel called Tuesday at nine, same as he had for nine years now. She let the silence sit a beat before she typed the sentence she’d been composing since Sunday, because some sentences deserved the eleven seconds even when the hardware could have done it in three.

I’m doing the procedure. Two weeks from Thursday.

He didn’t say anything for long enough that she checked, the old reflex, whether the connection had dropped. It hadn’t. “Okay,” he said finally, and she could hear him deciding, in real time, which of the four things he wanted to say he was actually going to let out first. “Are you scared?”

Yes. Not the way you’re picturing. I’ve had nine years to get used to my body leaving in pieces. This is the first time in nine years I get to choose the piece and the timing instead of finding out from a symptom. That part I’m not scared of.

I’m scared of the gap. The part between the last thing I compose here and the first thing I compose there, if there’s a gap at all. Nobody currently alive can tell me for certain whether there is one.

“I could come,” he said. “The new supersonic route’s four hours Austin to Boston, gate to gate. I could be there Thursday morning.”

Not for this one. Come after, if there’s an after that wants visiting. I need this Thursday to be mine and Priya’s, not an audience, even you.

She let the pause cost what it cost. Especially you. I don’t want the last thing I compose in this voice to be performed for anybody, even somebody I’d perform it well for.

“Then I’ll be on the phone Thursday morning instead. Whatever time they’ll let me.”

I know you will. You’ve never once shorted me on a Tuesday, and this isn’t even a Tuesday, so you really don’t have an excuse.

She held the sentence a moment before sending the next one, the eleven seconds this time entirely deliberate, not the hardware’s limit but her own. If something comes through on the other end that sounds like me and isn’t, I need you to be able to tell the difference. I need you to be honest with me about it, whatever that costs both of us. Don’t perform relief at me either. I’d rather grieve something real than pretend about something fake.

“I don’t know how to promise I’ll get that right.”

Neither do I. Promise anyway. It’s what Tuesdays are for.

They talked another twenty minutes about nothing that would survive being written down. Neither of them said I want you. They had not said it on any of these Tuesdays. Saying it two weeks before the scan would have made the procedure a performance of a relationship they had never started, and she would not do that to him, or to herself.

Elena understood, watching the letters resolve at their own unhurried pace, that the not-saying was still a choice. They were both still making it.

Thursday came on schedule, with a checklist. Priya seated the cap — the same brain-computer interface Elena had worn for nine years, its electrode array reading her intent and running it through a decode that turned neural signal into text and synthesized voice. Elena couldn’t feel most of the adjustments anymore. The cap had been part of her Tuesdays so long it might as well have been hair.

She composed one sentence she wanted exactly right. Eleven seconds for Ready when you are. Everything more dramatic had already gone to Daniel two days earlier. The decode offered a softer version; she refused it and kept the four words she’d meant.

“You’ll feel the read-rate change before anything else does,” Priya said. “It’ll feel like the decode getting faster — less lag between wanting a word and seeing it. That’s not nothing. Watch it. Tell me the second it stops feeling like faster and starts feeling like something else.”

Understood.

It did feel like faster, at first — the familiar relief of a sentence costing less than the last one had. Then faster stopped being about sentences. Three threads of thought sat open at once, then five. The narrowing of attention she’d spent nine years learning simply wasn’t necessary anymore.

She tried to tell Priya so — this is the thing you told me to watch for — the way she’d told Priya everything for nine years: intent first, then wait for the decode to assemble the letters on the screen.

The sentence appeared whole. She hadn’t finished deciding to send it. No eleven-second lag. No gap between wanting words and having them. That gap had defined every word she’d produced since the diagnosis. She couldn’t find its edges anymore.

Somewhere she couldn’t yet picture — Priya had said Axiom’s orbital array, same channel, wider pipe — something that was still her kept composing. Her biological body was still in the chair under the cap, still breathing through the support Priya monitored on the wall — for now. Priya had already told her what came next: consumed, not copied, the body finishing here while the read became the whole person somewhere else.

Elena reached for the fear she’d promised Daniel she was carrying — the gap between the last thing composed here and the first thing composed there — and found no seam she could point to. No dropped frame. No off-and-on. Only a continuous insistence on being the one doing the composing, all the way through.

I’m still here, she composed — to no one yet, faster than thought used to move. She understood that whether it was true in the way Daniel needed it to be true was a question for somebody standing outside the gap looking in.

The first thing she wanted to do with a sentence that cost nothing, on a Thursday that wasn’t a Tuesday, was send it to him anyway, and find out.
