Chapter 12: The Slow Part

“We appreciate the catch,” the VP of Concord Health Network said. “But we’re looking at whether we still need the human layer at all. The catch you’re citing as a win is a near-miss you’re asking us to keep paying to prevent. And eleven minutes is eleven minutes a patient didn’t have. Some of our people think the reviewer is the slow part now, not the safety.”

“You’re right that it was a near-miss,” Daniel said. “Picture it without us in the loop at all. Your model was confident and it was wrong. That denial goes out. Your patient’s oncologist spends two days on hold with appeals, the treatment slips, and nobody at Concord hears about it until a complaint lands months later.

“We caught it in eleven minutes because four separate systems check each other, and there’s a human being at the end of the chain accountable enough that we fired him for missing it.”

“You fired the guy who caught your own weak point.”

“I fired the guy who proved we had one, and then I fixed the actual weak point, which was the metric telling him to go fast. We’re not perfect. We’re the only layer in this stack honest enough to tell you exactly how we failed and what we changed because of it.

“I’m not asking you to believe humans are smarter than your model. By most measures they aren’t anymore. A smarter model checking a smarter model just moves your problem up a floor. Somebody has to be the one you can still call, and still be standing in front of a jury when it’s over.”

The VP exhaled. “Send me the metric. The disagreement one.”

Daniel sent it that afternoon. He did not hear back for twelve days.

The Round Rock apartment was an office now. A lease, a landlord who wanted first and last, a conference table bought used from a company that had failed fast enough to leave the shrink-wrap on half its furniture. A sign on the door too, just BACKSTOP, in a font somebody on the team had picked because it didn’t look like it was trying to convince anyone of anything.

Eleven people now. Sanjay ran review-team training, which had become a real job instead of something he did between cases. You could not hire out of school anymore. AI already did everything a new hire could do in their first years, so a junior cost more than they returned. What still paid was experience, people who had made hard calls and lived with them. You taught those people the specific paranoia that made a Backstop reviewer worth what Backstop charged for one.

Daniel had learned Concord’s question in February, the expensive way. A reviewer of his, smart, nine years a warehouse shift lead before the shift got optimized out from under him. Cleared eleven cases in ninety minutes when the queue backed up during a volume spike. Eleven cases in ninety minutes was what the internal dashboard rewarded.

One of the eleven was a denial for Concord’s newest client, an oncology practice. The model had flagged a dose increase as anomalous. The chart two clicks from where he’d looked said that increase was the protocol. He approved it in four seconds. The tier-four audit model caught it eleven minutes later.

Daniel let him go himself. The man took it better than Daniel did, which made it worse. “I was fast because fast was the number on the screen. Nobody told me careful was the actual number until it already cost somebody eleven minutes. I’d take the job back if you fixed the number instead of just fixing me.”

Daniel had carried that sentence around for six weeks. He killed the internal cases-per-hour metric entirely and replaced it with something Sanjay had proposed. Time spent disagreeing with yourself tracked how often a reviewer’s second look changed their own first answer.

He had started keeping a named assistant on the kitchen laptop that winter. Ellis. Still the kind of very-good that saved him an hour, not the kind that ran the company. He asked it about the fired reviewer anyway.

The miss was the dashboard, not the man. Concord will treat the eleven minutes as delay you charged them to add. If they ask whether a stronger model checking the first would have been faster, the honest answer is yes.

“Faster isn’t the product.”

Not this year. Watch the question. Next time it will come as a finding, not a sales objection.

He closed it. He did not let Ellis write the Concord call. He had already taken that one himself.

In the first week of March, between the firing and the Concord call, somebody’s system proved the Riemann hypothesis true. It was the question of how the prime numbers are distributed, open since 1859 and the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics for Daniel’s entire life. He went looking for the result itself, which was not a paper. It was a formal proof four hundred thousand steps long, written in a language built for machines to check rather than for people to read. A proof assistant had verified the whole thing in a night. There was a summary in English. A handful of mathematicians would say it looked faithful, and none of them would say so without conditions.

Daniel had a master’s in this. He got through the first page and part of the second and stopped, because going further would have cost him a week he didn’t have. He and Wei had spent an entire afternoon in graduate school arguing about whether a proof was elegant or just short. Lindqvist at the board refusing to referee because he thought the arguing was the education. Neither word applied to four hundred thousand machine-checked steps. They had never settled that argument. It had simply stopped having anything to be about.

Warren had started a program like that in the nineties and left it in his twenties to run a machine shop. The problem he’d walked away from was still open the morning Ray found him on the shop floor. Daniel looked up how long the system had taken, because his father would have asked. Four days.

So the question of whether it was true had an answer now, and the answer was that the checker said yes. Nobody objected. There was nobody it could hurt. You could not put a result about prime numbers in front of a jury.

Daniel drove to the new office most mornings past a delivery truck that backed itself into the loading dock behind the coffee shop, no person in the cab. By April it was just how the street looked.

The bigger version of the same thing was going up off the highway, a site he’d watched grow over the winter from a graded lot to low, windowless, block-long buildings. A city council notice in February had made the local paper. A request to draw an extra allotment off the aquifer for cooling, granted.

That same week a company put out a video of a humanoid prototype folding laundry in a demo kitchen, stiff and slow and obviously staged. Daniel watched it twice and mentioned it to Sanjay the next morning. “Robot folded a shirt on the internet last night. Badly. Took forever.”

“Give it two years.”

“That’s what I keep telling myself about our own thing. Cuts both ways.”

Sanjay found him at his desk on a Saturday, going through the fired reviewer’s old case logs one more time.

“You’re not going to find something new in there.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you doing?”

Daniel closed the laptop. “Making sure I actually feel as bad about it as I should, instead of just telling people I do.”

“That’s the reason I took this job over three interviews where nobody asked what I actually knew how to do.” Sanjay pulled out the chair across from him and set down two coffees. “You know what I keep coming back to. Meridian never once did this. Nobody there carried a bad call around for six weeks. They just tuned a threshold and moved the headcount line and closed the ticket.”

“That’s not a compliment to us. That’s a low bar we’re clearing.”

“It’s still a bar somebody has to clear, and right now it’s us. Concord called the main line an hour ago, for you specifically. They’re renewing. Two-year term this time, not one.”

Daniel didn’t say anything for a second.

“They want one more thing added to the contract. Quarterly disclosure. Every miss, ours or the model’s, in writing.”

Daniel opened a blank page that night. The first line he owed Concord was the eleven-minute miss. The reviewer who went fast because the dashboard told him to, the dose the chart two clicks away had already authorized, the eleven minutes a patient did not have. He typed the date and stopped. The page was still empty under it.
