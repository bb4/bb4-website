Chapter 7: The Idea

Six weeks into unemployment, Daniel had developed a routine he was not especially proud of. Coffee, then the job boards for exactly as long as it took to feel the specific dread of reading his own resume as if a stranger had written it. Then whatever task he could find that had a beginning, a middle, and an end short enough to finish before lunch.

He applied to four jobs. He had wanted none of them.

He did not want to go be good at the same kind of job again, for the same kind of company, until the same kind of dashboard flagged him again in another four years.

Nadia called on a Tuesday. His father had died on one. He’d gotten laid off the Friday after, but the calendar invite had landed on a Thursday — close enough to count. He answered the phone anyway.

“There’s an edge case in the software that assigns shipments to loading docks. Multi-dock handoffs, the thing you used to call ‘the Tuesday problem’ because it only ever showed up—”

“On Tuesdays. I remember.” Six weeks unemployed and his brain still filed that particular piece of logic like it mattered. “You’re telling me it’s still happening.”

“I’m telling you it’s happening more. Since the model update in March. Someone raised the confidence threshold — the score that decides when the AI has to ask a person for help. The AI coding system has proposed four patches since Monday. All plausible. None come with a reason anybody trusts, and the last one took two docks at Reno down for forty minutes.

“So now we’ve got three guys taking turns babysitting it manually — reviewing whatever the agent proposes overnight, approving what looks reasonable, rolling back what doesn’t. Nobody who actually understands why it breaks.”

Daniel turned that over. Three people, rotating, approving or rejecting an agent’s homework without being able to grade it — re-inventing him, badly, in triplicate.

“Send me the redacted log. And whatever the agent’s already tried.”

The log arrived twenty minutes later. Along with it came the agent’s four rejected fixes, each with a confidence score and a paragraph of reasoning. The agent was like a very good student who’d done every page of the reading but hadn’t been in the room when the professor explained why the exam question existed.

It took him ninety minutes, and none of it was spent writing code — the agent could write circles around anything he’d have typed. It took him ninety minutes to remember why the system had two separate scores for how sure it was — one for the zone, one for the dock — and why those two scores had never been allowed to use the same rule.

His login had been shut off the afternoon of the call with Talia Brandt, and every design doc he’d ever written was on the other side of it. It didn’t matter. The reason had never been in the docs. It was a decision he’d made under deadline pressure eighteen months ago, in an afternoon, and never fully written down anywhere an agent could have read it.

None of the four candidates had gotten that part right. The second one had come close. He wrote four sentences explaining why it was the right one and sent them to Nadia’s personal email.

She wrote back within the hour. You just saved us God knows how many hours of babysitting. Also — does it bother you that we needed you for this and you don’t work here anymore?

He looked at that question longer than the fix had taken him.

It didn’t bother him that Meridian needed him and couldn’t have him. What troubled him was the workaround itself. Three people, rotating, pressing approve or reject on homework they couldn’t grade. Nobody had solved the problem, just relocated it.

When the model wasn’t sure, a person was still supposed to decide. And nobody had bothered making sure the person doing the deciding was allowed to understand what they were deciding.

Meridian’s engineering group had maybe a fifth as many people as when he’d started. The agents now did the work of the other four-fifths. What they lacked was the judgment of someone who had made a hard call under pressure and lived with what happened next. That knowledge came from experience, and nobody had figured out how to mass-produce it.

He didn’t decide to build anything that night. What happened was smaller: he kept thinking about it.

He thought about it when a recruiter emailed him a job description that was the same job he’d just been fired from. A logistics company with the same load-bearing sentence buried in paragraph four: drive automation of manual exception handling. Meaning: come build the thing that replaces you again.

He didn’t reply. He opened a blank document instead and typed: When the model isn’t sure, a person decides. Not three guys rotating shifts and hoping. An actual person, who understands why the model broke and gets to be responsible for the call.

Underneath it, half a joke to himself: Backstop.

The word had come from nowhere he could trace. Baseball, maybe. The thing standing behind the thing that was supposed to catch it.

Two nights later he opened the wallet app for the first time since the reading. The inheritance could pay his living expenses while he built the company. Eight weeks of leftover pay was no longer his only deadline.

He closed the app before the number could turn into anything specific. There was still a laptop in Millbrook, locked in a drawer, that he had not gone back for.

He called Mara because he wanted to say it out loud to someone who’d known him long enough to tell him if he was being an idiot.

“Walk me through the business, not the feeling. Who pays for this?”

“Companies. Anybody running a model that makes decisions expensive enough to get wrong. They plug into a review layer — real people, vetted, paid per case — for whatever the model flags as low-confidence or high-stakes. It’s not replacing anything. It’s the thing that catches what the model already admits it isn’t sure about.”

“And the humans get paid how?”

“Per case. Fast. I haven’t worked out the rails yet.” He meant the payment system that would move money from a hospital to a reviewer quickly and securely.

“That’s the whole business, though. Not the humans. The rails. You have money now you didn’t have three months ago. I need you to say out loud that you know the money and the idea are two separate questions.”

“I know they’re separate.”

“Do you? Because you buried Dad and got fired in the same week, six weeks ago. And now you want to quit looking for a real job and spend the number in your account on a company named after a baseball position, based on one bug you fixed for free for people who don’t pay you anymore. Say that back to me.”

“When you put it that way.”

“I’m not saying don’t build it. I’m saying don’t let it be about proving Meridian wrong, or outrunning the grief. Build something that survives you being right about all of it.”

He didn’t have a clean answer. “What are you doing with your half?”

“Going harder into bitcoin than is probably wise. Somebody in this family has to be the one taking the aggressive side of the bet.”

“Building a company from scratch during a personal crisis isn’t the sensible one, Mara.”

“It is next to what I’m about to do.”

He texted Denise two days later, mostly as an excuse. He told himself he was checking on the shop. He also wanted to say the idea out loud to somebody who worked with her hands for a living.

Building something, he wrote. Company that pays humans to catch what AI models get wrong. Working name Backstop.

The reply took a while. So a very expensive way of hoping somebody still needs people.

He laughed once, alone in his kitchen, at how precisely she’d hit on the exact sentence he’d been afraid was true.

Not saying that’s bad, she added. Just — hope isn’t usually something you can put in a business plan. You’ll have to figure out how to make it look like something else on paper.

He typed thanks, that’s genuinely useful and meant it.

He went back to the document. Under the name, under the sentence about a person deciding, he started a list: what it does. who pays. what it isn’t.

The cursor blinked for a long time before he started writing. When he finally did, he felt as he had at 6:48 on Tuesday mornings at Meridian: a problem in front of him and his hands already halfway to solving it.
