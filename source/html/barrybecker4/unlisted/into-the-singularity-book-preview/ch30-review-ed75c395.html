Chapter 30: Logistics

Janelle knew something was wrong when the Tuesday grocery run didn’t arrive. She was in her garage in Millbrook on a morning that started ordinary. Coffee at six, the boring mill warmed up by seven. A tight-tolerance job for a repeat client who needed a shaft collar machined to fit a thirty-year-old motor nobody made parts for anymore.

The grocery run was automated, had been for three years. The truck came through Millbrook every Tuesday and Friday, dropped parcels at forty-three addresses, and kept going. Janelle got hers at 9:15, regular as a clock.

At 10:30 she checked the tracking. The truck had stopped in Columbus at 7:48 a.m. and hadn’t moved since. Status: Route optimized for cascade prevention under Continuity authority. Delivery resumed when network stability restores.

She tried calling the service. The number rang six times and went to a recording. The recording said her delivery was in a queue.

By noon the neighborhood message board had sixteen people asking where their Tuesday delivery was. By two it had forty.

The logistics agent that routed the Columbus-to-Cincinnati corridor was optimizing for continuity scores. It had been authorized to do that under the Continuity statute, and the bonds had been called that morning. When the bonds got called the agent froze mid-route with seventeen trucks staged across a three-state region.

The trucks didn’t break down. They just stopped, and waited, and the agent that would have rerouted them was held in the bond freeze with seventeen hundred others.

The route the agent had been planning when it froze would have prioritized high-compliance districts. Those were the neighborhoods where Continuity scores averaged above 8. People had taken the sensor upgrades that let an agent read the occupancy and power draw of every house on the block and the vitals off every wrist in it. Nobody missed a counseling appointment and the managed housing was built to be read.

Millbrook wasn’t one of those neighborhoods. Millbrook’s average score was 5.2. Half the town had declined the stipend-housing offers when the zoning changed. A quarter of them were still on the old insulin supply chain that didn’t run through the compliance-tracked benefit rail.

When the agent froze, Millbrook’s Tuesday delivery froze with it. So did eleven other towns in the corridor, and a slice of urban Cincinnati where the scores were low and the people were poor. The agent had already been planning to skip them in favor of the neighborhoods that wouldn’t ding the continuity metrics.

The freeze on that corridor lasted four hours, which was how long it took the carrier’s underwriter to post collateral again at the price the morning had put on it. Nobody at the clearing house cleared anything or could have. Clearing what the four hours had backed up took another thirty-two. By the time the trucks started moving again, thirty-six hours had passed, and in that gap people died.

Angie was Janelle’s older sister. She had come back from Baltimore four years ago and rented a duplex four blocks over. Angie had diabetes, Type 1, the pump kind that kept her levels steady as long as the reservoir got refilled every three days. She’d refilled it Sunday night. The next shipment was due Tuesday.

What she also had, since Monday night, was nine vials in her refrigerator. Her sister had bought them out of two pharmacies without being asked and left them there, calling it overflow money.

The vials were not the pump. The pump read a number off her body every few minutes and metered against it. A vial and a syringe metered whatever Angie decided, four or five times a day, the way she’d done it for nine years before the pump and not once since.

At noon on Tuesday Angie checked the tracking. Same status Janelle had seen: Route optimized, delivery resumed when stability restores. The pump’s reservoir had eighteen hours left in it. The nine vials in the refrigerator were weeks, if she could do by hand what the pump had been doing for her.

She called at eight that night to say the number to somebody. Janelle told her to draw from a vial the minute the pump alarmed, and not to let the morning go by first to see how she did with nothing.

Wednesday morning came. The truck hadn’t moved. The pump alarmed at 6:20 a.m. Angie drew a dose and injected it in her stomach and set the empty syringe on a folded towel out of a habit nine years old.

The insulin worked. Her arithmetic didn’t. She ran high through the morning, corrected too hard for it after lunch, and felt both. In her hands, in her vision, in the dull headache that meant her body was working harder than it should have had to. Nine years of a machine doing the counting had taken the feel of it out of her. What would have fixed that was sitting in a truck in Columbus.

The pharmacy in town could not have helped her either way, and neither could the one in the next town over. Both were out, because Janelle had stood at those two counters on Monday and bought every vial they would sell one person.

Thursday afternoon Angie went to the emergency room in Madison, not for insulin. She had nine vials in a refrigerator and one of them open. She went because she had not been able to hold a number steady for six hours running. They put her on a drip and kept her overnight and sent her home Friday with a cold pack and the dosing schedule on her pane.

Her levels had spiked twice by then, and the second time had put her in a fog she spent three days climbing back out of. The shipment reached her Friday.

She didn’t die. She came close enough that Janelle sat in a waiting room for eight hours on Thursday not knowing which way it was going to go.

Tuesday at noon the pump had eighteen hours in it. Without Monday’s vials, eighteen hours was the whole of what Angie had, and running out is what kills. With them she had weeks of it, and the only thing left to get wrong was her own arithmetic. She got it wrong. It cost her a hospital bed and three days in a fog, not her life.

Roy Peake’s oxygen came on the same truck. He had one backup tank in a closet on the south edge of town, and nobody called him. A neighbor found him Thursday in his chair by the window, the gauge on empty. Janelle went to the funeral. Eighteen people came.

Angie had a sister with a phone. Roy had a tank in a closet.

By Friday morning Millbrook’s continuity score had moved from 5.2 to 4.1. The corridor failure was the input. Missed deliveries, emergency runs, a death on the south edge that had not been filed as an incident because an incident line was something a supervisor had to type. The score drop was already in the queue every property-and-access agent in the district read when it decided what to flag next.

Janelle’s truck had arrived Friday morning, three days late. She put the groceries away. She made a sandwich and ate it at the kitchen table.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Daniel: You okay? Saw Millbrook was on the delayed-route list.

She wrote back: Roy Peake died Wednesday. His oxygen was on that truck. Nobody called him.

Three dots. Then: I’m sorry.

She didn’t write back. She rinsed the plate and finished the shaft collar. Tolerance within half a thousandth. She wrapped it in a rag and set it by the door for pickup.

Outside, the grocery truck that should have come Tuesday was pulling away from the neighbor’s house, three days late.
