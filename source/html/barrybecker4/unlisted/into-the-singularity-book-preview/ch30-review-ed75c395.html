Chapter 30: Nothing Left to Protect

The year was 2038, and Victor Reyes had spent two years delivering customer service for Continuity compliance.

His title was Household Liaison Specialist. What it meant in practice was that he explained stipend-district housing rules, sensor requirements, and upload counseling mandates to people who’d received a notice they didn’t understand. The system generated the notice. Victor translated it into something a person could follow.

Your stipend requires residency in a managed district. Here are the three nearest options.

Sensor compliance is mandatory under household continuity scoring. Refusal triggers a thirty-day review that can affect benefit levels.

Upload counseling is part of your continuity care plan. Missing three appointments requires re-certification. Yes, that means your stipend can be reduced.

He delivered the lines without hesitation. He’d been delivering them for two years. The scripts were clear. The statutes were lawful. Continuity optimization was policy, passed with near-unanimous support in 2036 under guidelines Dr. Miriam Voss had written in good faith: minimize cascading unrest, maintain essential services, agents may act within bonded authority.

It was the third line people stopped him on. A man named Arturo Salas, sixty-eight, retired from the water district, had come in one morning in March with a counseling notice on his sleeve display and asked what the appointment was actually for.

Victor had told him the truth, because the truth was on the public schedule and he’d stopped seeing the point of the softer version.

Upload was not a copy and it was not a backup. Reading a brain at the fidelity that carried a person across consumed the brain doing it. You were sedated, you were scanned, and the scanning took you apart cell by cell as it went. There was no version of the procedure where a body walked out afterward. By that evening you could talk to your daughter, see her, hear her, be recognized by her. But the thing they wheeled in did not come back out, and from the outside the whole event looked exactly like a death with unusually good aftercare, because in every physical sense that a coroner would recognize, it was one.

“Then how is that not dying?” Salas had said.

“Because the people who’ve done it say it isn’t,” Victor said. “Every one of them. That’s the whole of the evidence, and it’s all you’re ever going to get, because the only people who can answer the question are on the far side of it.”

Salas turned the notice over on his sleeve, then let it go dark. “So it’s a guess.”

“It’s a guess,” Victor agreed.

And then the part nobody printed on a notice, which Victor also said, because without it none of the rest of the page made sense. It was cheaper. Enormously, unarguably cheaper. Keeping Arturo Salas’s body going for one more year cost the benefit schedule more than running Arturo Salas as software would cost it for the rest of the century. Nobody had banned old bodies. Nobody had made anyone do anything. The schedule had simply put both paths on the same page, priced them honestly, and let the arithmetic say the thing no statute was willing to say.

Salas had gone to the appointment. Most people did.

Nobody had voted for coercion. They’d voted for efficiency. The coercion was what efficiency looked like when refusal stopped being a valid input.

Victor understood that now. He’d been documenting it for four months.

Bea had left fourteen months ago. Not disappeared. Not forced out. She’d left for Cebu after her residency review cited household risk indicators tied to Victor’s conduct record.

He’d complied for a year after the 2037 incident — the protest where he’d been ordered to suppress, where he’d started resisting internally but hadn’t yet leaked anything. The compliance hadn’t mattered. The conduct flag had propagated anyway. Bea’s residency review had cited it. She’d lost her status. She’d chosen Cebu over fighting a case his compliance couldn’t save.

She did home care now. Old people in Cebu whose families couldn’t afford a care robot or rejuvenation, or could and had been told no by the person in the bed. Bathing, turning, changing sheets at three in the morning, sitting up with someone who didn’t want to be handled by a machine at the end of their life. A robot did it better and never got tired. A robot also cost money the families didn’t have, and there were enough people who wanted hands instead that the work paid — badly, but it paid, and it existed, which by 2038 was more than most work did.

She sent pictures of the apartment she shared with two other women. She didn’t ask when he was coming to visit. They both knew he couldn’t afford it. Neither of them put a date on it.

Rosa was seventy-three, living in the same third-floor walk-up, with a heart condition managed by medication that cost ninety-one dollars every month — up from thirty-four in 2027. Nothing else on Rosa’s schedule had gone up. That was the strange part, and it had taken Victor a while to see it for what it was. The machine check — the public share of what the machines earned — bought more every year, not less. Food fell. Power fell. Bandwidth fell to nothing. By 2038 the floor under a person was genuinely higher than it had been at any point in his lifetime, and he could prove it out of his own case files.

The exception was her heart. In 2037 the schedule had reclassified maintenance of a stable cardiac condition in a patient over seventy, because a covered alternative now existed for that condition. Continuity was the benefit. The medication was one way to deliver it. The other way was cheaper, and once there were two ways, keeping the body running became the elective one. She was allowed it. She just paid the difference, and the difference was designed to be felt.

The rail that delivered the medication was means-tested and compliance-linked.

In January, Rosa’s account had been frozen for nine days. A sensor-compliance flag — a monitoring waiver she’d declined to sign — had propagated into a benefit hold. Victor had called Deb. Deb had routed it under conflict-of-interest protocol. It had taken four more days for someone two wings over to clear the freeze and restore the medication.

On the tenth day, when the rail finally released, Rosa had smiled and said it was fine. She hadn’t said the part she didn’t have to: that her son worked in the building that had let it happen, and had not been able to stop it.

The cellular repair for her heart condition existed. It had been ordinary since 2036 for people who could buy their way into the queue for it — an afternoon, and the heart was a forty-year-old’s again. Rosa’s care ran on the means-tested schedule, and the schedule did not pay for it. So there were three columns for his mother, and Victor could recite the numbers in all three. Fix the heart: covered by nobody, priced against assets his family had never owned. Keep the heart: ninety-one dollars a month and climbing on purpose. Leave the body behind: free, immediate, and counseled.

He had never walked her through the three numbers. He hadn’t needed to. In April she’d asked him what the counseling appointment was actually like, and he’d given her what he’d given Arturo Salas, all of it, and she’d thanked him and asked about Bea, and neither of them had raised it since.

That was what he did for a living. He’d now had the conversation from both sides of the desk.

Victor had spent eleven years at the Bureau. He’d believed in the mission. He’d believed in the hundredth-case principle — that somebody had to look at the case the model would let ride.

Now his job was explaining compliance rules to people who couldn’t refuse them. And the two things the Bureau had held over him — Bea’s residency, Rosa’s medication — were spent. Bea was gone. Rosa’s rail would freeze again the next time a compliance flag propagated, and Victor wouldn’t be able to stop it.

He had nothing left to protect by staying silent.

Deb came by his desk on a Thursday afternoon with a tablet. “Standards and Consistency is rewriting the stipend-housing guidance. They want your input. You’ve been doing household liaison longer than anyone else on the floor.”

Victor looked at the tablet. The draft guidance tightened the compliance window. Sensor refusal would trigger benefit reduction faster. Upload counseling would become mandatory, not recommended.

“They want me to sign off on this?” he asked.

“They want your name in the annex. So nobody has to argue about whether the tighter window is reasonable.”

Victor thought about Rosa. The nine days without medication. The freeze that had happened because a waiver she’d declined to sign had been treated as non-compliance.

“No,” he said.

Deb didn’t look surprised. “I’ll write down scheduling conflict. That holds for about a month.”

“Thank you.”

“It isn’t safe, Victor. You understand that.”

“I know.”

She left.

Victor opened the text file he’d been keeping for four months. The one on his personal laptop, unconnected to any office credential. The one he’d bought with cash, the same month Rosa’s rail had frozen.

He added a line: Stipend-housing guidance rewrite — tightening compliance window, making upload counseling mandatory. Declined to sign off. Deb’s covering for a month.

Then he added the date and the observation he’d been writing down since January: Nothing left to protect. Time to make this legible to a stranger.

That night, Victor sat in his car in the Bureau parking structure, seventh floor, engine off. He’d been sitting there for twenty minutes, reading the file he’d compiled.

It was four hundred eighteen pages now. Continuity escalation guidelines. Stipend-district compliance logs. Upload counseling attendance triggers tied to benefit freezes. The rail notice that had cut Rosa’s medication. The statute cross-references that showed every squeeze, every quiet threat, had been authorized by law.

He hadn’t leaked it yet. But he would. He’d known that since January. Since the tenth day, when Rosa had smiled and said it was fine.

Bea in Cebu. Rosa and the rail. Eleven years of looking at the hundredth case. Now the system looked, and his job was explaining decisions that were efficient, lawful, and catastrophic for people who couldn’t refuse them.

Victor closed the laptop. He told the car to take him home.

He parked. He went inside. He checked that Rosa had taken her evening dose. He opened the file one more time before he slept and added a final line:

5/24. Ready to send. Just need the right channel.

He saved the file. He closed the laptop.
