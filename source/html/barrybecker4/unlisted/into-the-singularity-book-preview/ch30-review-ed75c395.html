Chapter 30: Logistics

Janelle knew something was wrong when the Tuesday grocery run didn’t arrive. She was in her garage in Millbrook with the boring mill warmed up since seven, working a shaft collar for a repeat client who needed it to fit a thirty-year-old motor nobody made parts for anymore.

The grocery run was automated, had been for three years. The truck came through Millbrook every Tuesday and Friday, dropped parcels at forty-three addresses, and kept going. Janelle got hers at 9:15, regular as a clock.

At 10:30 she checked the tracking. The truck had stopped in Columbus at 7:48 a.m. and hadn’t moved since. Status: Route optimized for cascade prevention under Continuity authority. Delivery resumed when network stability restores.

By noon the neighborhood message board had sixteen people asking where their Tuesday delivery was. By two it had forty.

The explanation came that afternoon, in a thread on the board started by a man who had driven the Columbus-to-Cincinnati corridor himself for twenty years before it drove itself. Janelle read it standing at the bench with the collar still in the vise.

The agent that routed the corridor had frozen at 7:48 with seventeen trucks staged across three states, held in the morning’s bond call with seventeen hundred others. The trucks hadn’t broken down. They were parked on shoulders and in depot lots, waiting for an instruction that wasn’t coming. And the route the agent had been planning when it froze skipped Millbrook anyway. It favored districts that scored above 8, the ones with the sensor upgrades, where an agent could read the occupancy and power draw of every house on the block and the vitals off every wrist in it. Millbrook had sat at 5.2 on the municipal dashboard every morning for a year. Half the town had turned down the stipend housing when the zoning changed. A quarter of it was still on the old insulin supply chain that didn’t run through the tracked benefit rail. Eleven other towns in the corridor were on the same skip list, and a slice of Cincinnati where the scores were low and the people were poor.

The carrier’s underwriter re-posted collateral a little before noon, at whatever the morning had made a bond cost. Clearing what those four hours had backed up would take another thirty-two. Thirty-six hours before the trucks moved.

Angie was Janelle’s older sister. She had come back from Baltimore four years ago and rented a duplex four blocks over. Angie had diabetes, Type 1, the pump kind that kept her levels steady as long as the reservoir got refilled every three days. She’d refilled it Sunday night. The next shipment was due Tuesday.

What she also had, since Monday night, was nine vials in her refrigerator. Her sister had bought them out of two pharmacies without being asked and left them there, calling it overflow money.

The vials were not the pump. The pump read a number off her body every few minutes and metered against it. A vial and a syringe metered whatever Angie decided, four or five times a day, the way she’d done it for nine years before the pump and not once since.

She called at eight that night, mostly to say the number to somebody.

“Eighteen hours on the reservoir. The truck’s still sitting in Columbus.”

“You’ve got the vials,” Janelle said. “The minute the pump alarms, you draw one. Don’t let the morning go by first to see how you do with nothing.”

“I haven’t done it by hand in nine years.”

“You did it by hand for nine years before that. Draw the vial.”

Janelle didn’t sleep much. At six on Wednesday she let herself into the duplex with the key under the planter and found Angie at the kitchen table in her robe, the pump clipped to her waistband and the nine vials lined up on a dish towel with their labels facing out.

“You didn’t have to come,” Angie said.

“I know.”

The pump alarmed at 6:20, three rising tones. Angie silenced it with her thumb without looking down. She drew the dose and held the syringe up to the window and flicked it twice, and her hands did that part on their own. Then she stopped with the needle an inch from her stomach.

“How much,” she said. She wasn’t asking Janelle. “The pump knew. It’s been knowing for nine years.”

She worked it on her fingers against the edge of the table: the reading, the breakfast she hadn’t eaten yet, the ratio she used to carry in her head. Janelle watched her get a number, look at it, and take two units off. She injected it and set the empty syringe on a folded towel out of a habit nine years old.

The insulin worked. Her arithmetic didn’t. She ran high through the morning and corrected too hard for it after lunch. By two she was sitting on the kitchen floor with her back against the dishwasher, sweating through her shirt, and Janelle was holding a juice box to her mouth and telling her to drink it slower.

“The pharmacy’s out,” Angie said, when she could talk. “I called this morning. The one in the next town’s out too.”

“I know,” Janelle said.

Both counters were empty because Janelle had stood at them on Monday and bought every vial they would sell one person. The nine vials were on the table above Angie’s head. Neither of them said anything else about it.

Nine years of a machine doing the counting had taken the feel of it out of her. The reservoir refill that would have let the machine do it again was sitting in a truck in Columbus.

By Thursday afternoon Angie had not been able to hold a number steady for six hours running. The second high put her in a fog. She kept asking Janelle what day it was and then apologizing for asking. Her breath smelled like nail-polish remover, and Janelle knew from a clinic pamphlet twenty years old what that meant.

She drove her to the emergency room in Madison with the passenger window down. They didn’t take her in for insulin. She had nine vials in a refrigerator and one of them open. They took her in because her blood had gone acid, and they put her on a drip.

Janelle sat in the waiting room for eight hours on a plastic chair bolted to three others, watching the door to the treatment bays and not knowing which way it was going to go. Around eleven a nurse came out and said the numbers were coming down and Angie was asking for her sister.

They sent her home Friday with a cold pack and the dosing schedule on her pane, and she spent three days climbing back out of the fog. The shipment reached her Friday.

Tuesday at noon the pump had eighteen hours in it. Without Monday’s vials, eighteen hours was the whole of what Angie had, and running out is what kills. With them she had weeks of it, and the only thing left to get wrong was her own arithmetic. She got it wrong. It cost her a hospital bed and three days in a fog, not her life.

Roy Peake’s oxygen came on the same truck. He had one backup tank in a closet on the south edge of town, and nobody called him. A neighbor found him Thursday in his chair by the window, the gauge on empty. Janelle went to the funeral. Eighteen people came.

Angie had a sister with a phone. Roy had a tank in a closet.

By Friday morning the municipal dashboard had Millbrook at 4.1. The corridor failure was the input. Missed deliveries, emergency runs, a death on the south edge that had not been filed as an incident because an incident line was something a supervisor had to type. The score drop was already in the queue every property-and-access agent in the district read when it decided what to flag next.

Janelle’s truck had arrived Friday morning, three days late. She put the groceries away. She made a sandwich and ate it at the kitchen table.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Daniel: You okay? Saw Millbrook was on the delayed-route list.

She wrote back: Roy Peake died Wednesday. His oxygen was on that truck. Nobody called him.

Three dots. Then: I’m sorry.

She didn’t write back. She rinsed the plate and finished the shaft collar. Tolerance within half a thousandth. She wrapped it in a rag and set it by the door for pickup.

Outside, the grocery truck that should have come Tuesday was pulling away from the neighbor’s house, three days late.
