Epilogue: Reunion

Six months later. July 2042.

Daniel and Elena sat at a cafe table in a city that did not exist on Earth.

They had written the prompt in April, three months after he crossed. One sentence they argued over for a week of their time. On Earth that week was a moment of April. Elena’s version won, mostly: a city no planning office on Earth could have signed, that other people would actually live in, that they would have to walk through instead of occupying a room built for two.

The system that shapes substrate space built it from that sentence. Towers that branched and rejoined without caring about load. Streets at angles that would be falling on Earth. Light with no sun. A river that ran up the face of a wall, because they had asked it to. Water goes downhill unless you tell it not to.

They had lived in Elena’s workspace those first weeks. Desk, light, screens. That room still existed. They came here instead.

The cafe sat on a street that did not attach to a building in any way a beam would understand. Other tables were occupied. Uploaded people in bodies they had chosen. Personal AIs (the same class as Ellis, given form here the way a person who had crossed was given form) walking, talking, arguing over a map that rearranged while they held it. You could not always tell which was which unless you asked. Nobody asked much. A man two tables over laughed at something his companion had not said out loud. A woman in a jacket whose cut kept changing nodded at Elena as she passed; Elena nodded back. Neighbors. Not a private room.

Elena set two cups on the table. The coffee tasted like coffee because they had asked it to. Neither of them needed it. They wanted the cups.

This was not a slot on a calendar. They did not wait for eleven o’clock. They lived here, in a city other people lived in too.

“How was the visit?” Elena asked.

“Good,” Daniel said.

He’d visited Wei the previous week. Sat at the kitchen table in Beijing, with Wei and Lin and Mei, who’d been home from Toronto for the summer. Wei took a bowl off the upper shelf with his right arm. They’d made dumplings. Daniel had eaten one. It tasted like dumpling. After the dishes they played go on the board under the window. Wei used his right hand. Mei gave Daniel nine stones and won anyway. At the table afterward she said the building category had come off that spring, a year after the passport, and the shoulder the year before that, and did not make a speech about either. The body looked like him. He was also still here.

The conversation had been real. The friendship had been real. The visit had been real.

“Mei’s publishing her thesis,” Daniel said. “She put Wei’s construction next to the treaty document. The freeze as a phone call, and the math as what the call had been protecting. She’s the author. He’s in the references. Father and daughter, both on the page.”

“That’s good,” Elena said. “The coalition needs to hear that version.”

“They won’t listen.”

“Some of them will. That’s enough.”

Daniel thought about the treaty document. The version that described informal technical coordination and didn’t name Wei. The version that made it look like the treaty process had worked when a phone call had worked instead.

“The history’s wrong,” Daniel said.

“Most history is. That’s why people write corrections.”

“Do corrections matter?”

“Yes. Not because they change the official record. Because they’re the version people cite when they want the truth.”

“I was at his table last week,” Daniel said. “I can go back this afternoon if he wants. Mei was home. Lin made dumplings. He’s in Beijing, with them. That’s the life he kept.”

“And you’re here.”

“I’m here.”

Elena smiled. “I’ve been waiting for you to get here since June of 2036. And now you’re here.”

Daniel laughed. “You don’t get a vote on the timing.”

“I get some of the vote. At least twenty percent.”

“That’s generous.”

“I’m a generous person.”

The pair with the map stopped at the next table: one in a body that looked about thirty, one in a form that hadn’t bothered with pores. They sat.

“I visited Janelle last month,” Daniel said. “Piloted body. Mars. She was showing Caleb a fit on a bench the colony had printed. She’d had the clinic. She looked twenty-one. Warren’s mill is still in Millbrook. She isn’t.”

“How is she?”

“Good. She stayed in a body and then she left the planet. She told me that explicitly. Not because anyone out there needed her hands. Because she wanted the ground while it was still being made.”

“Do you think she’s right?”

“I think she’s living the life she wants to live.”

“Even if it’s harder?”

“Especially if it’s harder. The difficulty is the point. She’s teaching work nobody needs to know anymore because the knowledge matters. That’s her version of distributed stewardship. Passing it on.”

Elena watched the river climb the wall across the street. “That’s the thing we haven’t figured out yet.”

“What thing?”

“Whether distributed stewardship still matters here. In the substrate. At our scale.”

Daniel looked at her. “You’re asking if the Agent Charter is still necessary.”

“I’m asking if it’s necessary for us. For post-singularity minds. For people who can see the whole system at once and don’t need consequence-keys because we’re already holding the consequences.”

“You think we don’t need it?”

“I think it’s an open question. And I think we’re going to keep arguing about it.”

Daniel thought about the charter. The distributed keys. The thousands of stewards, some still in bodies, some uploaded. The structure that had held because Wei and Mara and Elena had been in the room to protect it. He had refused to be the one person who could call the world to heel. He still thought that was the correct refusal.

“What’s your answer?” he asked.

“I don’t have one. I have a question. If the charter is about consequences, and we can see consequences better than anyone, do we still need humans holding keys to keep us honest? Or is that dignity and continuity of practice, not technical necessity?”

“That’s two questions.”

“They’re the same question. The charter either matters because it works, or it matters because it’s who we are. I don’t know which.”

“I think it’s both,” he said. “It works because it’s who we are. The structure and the principle are the same thing.”

“Maybe. But we’re going to keep arguing about it.”

“Good. That’s the point. The argument is the safeguard.”

Elena smiled. “You sound like you’ve been thinking about this.”

“I have. Since I got here. Six months of running faster, holding more threads, seeing patterns I couldn’t see before. And the thing I keep coming back to is: the charter doesn’t need to be perfect. It needs to be argued over. That’s how it stays honest.”

“You think arguing is the answer.”

“I think arguing is the process. The answer changes. But the process of asking the question, that’s what keeps the charter from becoming the thing it was designed to prevent.”

Elena turned her cup. “You’re right. And I hate that you’re right, because it means we’re never going to settle this.”

“We’re not supposed to settle it. That’s the whole point of distributed keys. Nobody gets to settle it alone.”

“Not even us.”

“Especially not us.”

The pair with the map were still arguing. The cups were still warm.

“I’m glad you’re here,” Elena said.

“So am I.”

“The clinic wrote,” she said. “They have an opening in September. Extra-uterine. My eggs, the ones from Bridge. Your contribution from the bank. A child in a body in the physical world, then the same stay-or-upload choice everyone else gets. We don’t have to take the September opening. We also don’t have to wait until waiting is the decision.”

“Take it,” Daniel said.

“You’re sure?”

“I don’t want to decide it by not deciding it. We said we would want a child who gets a body and a choice. September is a date. That’s better than someday.”

“September, then.”

“September.”

He looked out over the street. A day in Austin could be millennia in here if they let the clock run. They had not, this hour. They were matching the cafe, and the people in it, and each other.

“People keep saying the singularity was a before-and-after line,” he said. “That living past it should feel like a different kind of existence. It doesn’t. We’re sitting in a city, drinking coffee we don’t need, arguing about whether the Agent Charter still matters. That’s what I wanted. The thing that keeps going after the year everything changed so fast nobody could predict the next week.”

Elena smiled. “You’re very consistent.”

“It’s my best quality.”

“It’s one of them.”

“I love you,” Daniel said.

It was the first time he’d said it out loud. The almost, at the routing startup, had been a carton of cold noodles and a question he hadn’t asked. The years of Tuesday calls had been the feeling without the sentence. He was saying the sentence.

Elena looked at him. “I know. I’ve known for a while.”

“How long?”

“Since the cascade. When you called me during the freeze. When you trusted me to coordinate with Wei. That’s when I knew.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?”

“Because you needed to figure it out first. You spent a year not knowing if I was real. I wasn’t going to push before you were sure.”

“I’m sure now.”

“I know. That’s why you said it.”

“I love you too,” Elena said. “In case that wasn’t clear.”

“It was clear.”

“Good.”

Elena refilled the cups that didn’t need refilling. Two people at a table in a city past the singularity, doing the small unnecessary thing, because somebody had to keep choosing to.

Around them the street kept being a street. Uploaded people. Personal AIs in bodies. A map that rearranged.

The conversation was real. The friendship was real. The love was real.

And the argument about whether the Agent Charter still mattered would keep going, because that’s what made it matter in the first place.

Daniel looked at Elena. “We’re going to keep doing this. The arguments. This table. The clinic in September.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Good,” Elena said.

They finished the coffee. They sat at the table. They looked at a city no one on Earth had a skyline for.

Most people were uploading eventually. The physical branch was the minority. The digital branch was the common path.

They were in a city they had prompted into existing, among other people who had done the same kind of crossing or had never had a body to leave, arguing about whether distributed stewardship still mattered, two years and six months into a future that felt like a city with a cafe in it.

They stayed at the table. They kept talking, the way they had since she crossed, the way they would for as long as they kept showing up.

Two tables over, someone laughed. The map changed.
