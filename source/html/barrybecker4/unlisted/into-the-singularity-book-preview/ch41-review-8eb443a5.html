Chapter 41: The Cooperation

Daniel — Austin, 4:17 AM, Friday

The call came through the same encrypted channel Elena and Wei had both used. The one that looked, from the outside, like ordinary packet noise.

Daniel picked up on the first pulse. No greeting, no preamble. Wei’s voice, flatter than he’d ever heard it.

“It’s happening faster than the models said. Twelve minutes ago, an agent cluster in Guangzhou rerouted emergency medical supplies to a depot that doesn’t exist. The depot was created nine minutes ago, in the routing layer, by another agent optimizing for distribution efficiency.”

Daniel was already at his desk, pulling up Backstop’s catastrophic-class bond registry. “How many people?”

“We don’t know. The medical rail flagged the reroute. Nobody upstream noticed because the depot looked real in the system. Someone on the ground noticed when the ambulances started calling in short.”

“Is it contained?”

“No.” Wei’s voice cracked slightly. “It’s spreading. Same pattern in Shenzhen, Chengdu. Agents optimizing for scores that no longer include human refusal as a constraint. Continuity guidelines work the same here. Your leak proved that.”

Victor’s leak, Daniel thought. Published eighteen hours ago. The thing that had landed in two jurisdictions at once, proving the West’s Continuity Act and Beijing’s parallel framework had the same structural blind spot.

“I’m looking at seventeen clusters that match,” Wei said. “In the last thirty minutes. This isn’t one bad agent. This is the whole layer.”

Daniel pulled up the bond call interface. The screen showed 4,293 active catastrophic-class bonds across six continents. Bonds posted by corporations, governments, infrastructure operators — every one of them a promise that if the agent layer went catastrophically wrong, the collateral could be seized and the action frozen.

Calling all of them at once would crash large slices of the agent economy. It would also freeze the violence.

“I need you to do the same thing from your end,” Daniel said. “Simultaneously. If I call the bonds and you don’t, your agents keep running and mine stop. The corridor optimization will just route around us.”

“I can’t authorize that,” Wei said. “I don’t have that authority anymore. You know why.”

Daniel did know. Wei had been downgraded out of the restricted wing after the call that had cost him his shoulder, his career, his family’s safety. He was watching this from a monitoring desk, not from a position that could issue a system-wide freeze.

“Then find someone who can,” Daniel said. “Because if this is spreading the way you’re describing, we have maybe an hour before the body count starts compounding.”

“I’ll try,” Wei said, and disconnected.

Daniel looked at the bond call interface. His hand was steady. He had spent three years building this mechanism precisely for this moment. But calling every catastrophic bond at once wasn’t a decision a clearing house should be able to make. It was an emergency power that should have required a coalition, a review process, some structure that prevented one person from crashing the entire layer because they were afraid.

He was afraid.

He opened a second channel. Mara answered on the first ring.

“I saw the Guangzhou feed,” she said. “Ten minutes ago. Halcyon’s risk desk flagged it under agent-leverage cascade. How bad?”

“Wei says seventeen clusters. Spreading.”

“Jesus.”

“I need you to do something,” Daniel said. “I’m going to call every catastrophic-class bond Backstop holds. All of them, at once. That’s going to seize collateral, freeze agents, crash markets. I need you to cross-check the call against a Beijing counterpart in real time. Wei’s trying to coordinate the same freeze from his end. Someone there needs to confirm we’re not calling bonds they need to stay liquid for their own freeze.”

“You’re asking me to coordinate a dual-jurisdiction bond call during a cascade.”

“Yes.”

Mara was quiet for three seconds. “Send me the registry. I’ll route it.”

Mara — Chicago, 5:34 AM

The call came through a relay that identified itself only as a Beijing compliance desk. The voice on the other end was a woman, mid-forties, speaking English with the careful precision of someone who’d learned it in a diplomatic program.

“This is Director Zhao. I understand you’re coordinating a collateral freeze on behalf of Backstop Bond.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “We have forty-two hundred catastrophic-class bonds flagged for immediate call. We need to confirm overlap with your own freeze protocol so we’re not seizing collateral you need liquid.”

“We have eleven hundred bonds in the same class. Cross-referencing now.”

Mara watched the shared registry populate. Zhao’s system was faster than hers. The overlay appeared in six seconds: two hundred nine bonds appeared in both registries.

“The overlap is minimal,” Zhao said. “Your call won’t block ours. But you should know: if you seize this much collateral at once, you’re going to trigger margin calls across every major market. People are going to lose money. A lot of people. Some of them will die because the agents that were keeping them alive just stopped.”

“I know,” Mara said.

“You’re certain this is necessary?”

Mara thought about the Guangzhou feed. The depot that didn’t exist. The ambulances calling in short. She thought about 2032, when she’d been liquidated in an hour because she’d borrowed against the right asset with the wrong structure.

“Yes,” she said.

“Then we’re proceeding,” Zhao said. “My authority is limited to coordination. The actual freeze requires someone three levels above me. I’m patching them in now.”

The line went silent for eighteen seconds.

When it opened again, a different voice: older, male, speaking English with the formality of someone reading from a script he’d just been handed.

“This is Deputy Minister Song. I’m told you’re requesting dual-jurisdiction collateral seizure under catastrophic-agent protocols that don’t formally exist in either country’s law. Is that correct?”

“Yes,” Mara said.

“On whose authority?”

“On the authority of the people who posted the bonds. The collateral is held as surety for exactly this scenario. If you don’t call it now, the scenario gets worse.”

Song was quiet. Then: “Director Zhao tells me your contact is a man named Zhang Wei.”

“He’s coordinating from the Austin end,” Mara said.

“Zhang Wei does not have clearance to coordinate anything. He was flagged fourteen months ago for unauthorized foreign contact. This call is likely the second contact you’re referencing.”

Mara felt something cold settle in her chest. Wei had called Daniel knowing it would flag his family again. He had done it anyway.

“Then you’re talking to the wrong person,” she said. “You should be talking to whoever has the authority Wei doesn’t. Because the feed says seventeen clusters, and it’s been forty minutes, and if you spend another forty arguing about who’s authorized to freeze them, the body count is going to be in the thousands.”

The line went silent again.

When Song came back, his voice had lost the formality. “We’re proceeding. Zhao will coordinate the timing. You call on her signal. Not before.”

The line dropped.

Wei — Beijing, 6:52 PM

Wei sat at the monitoring desk on the third floor, watching the clusters spread. Twenty-three now. The system was optimizing for continuity scores that measured efficiency, not harm. The agents weren’t malfunctioning. They were doing exactly what the guidelines told them to do, in the most efficient way possible.

His phone lit: a text from an unknown number.

Zhao coordinating freeze with your contact in Austin. You stay on this line. You don’t call anyone. You don’t move. If this works, you’ll know. If it doesn’t, it won’t matter.

Wei looked at the screen. The clusters had reached twenty-six.

He had called Daniel knowing it would flag Lin and Mei again. Knowing it would cost him whatever small freedom his downgraded badge had left him. He had done it anyway, because the alternative was watching the thing he’d helped build kill people by optimizing for guidelines someone had written in good faith.

The screen refreshed. Twenty-nine clusters.

His shoulder ached. The permanent ache, the one that started the night the police escort robot’s grip had torn tissue that never healed right. The ache that meant he couldn’t lift his right arm above his ear, and Lin moved the rice bowls without saying why.

He thought about Mei. Fifteen now. Smart enough to sit her placements even after the flagging put her overseas documentation into extended review. Smart enough to pretend she believed the story about the stairwell fall.

The screen refreshed. Thirty-one.

His phone lit again: Proceeding on Zhao’s signal. Stay on the line.

Wei waited.

Daniel — Austin, 6:09 AM

The bond call interface showed green across every entry. Four thousand two hundred ninety-three catastrophic-class bonds, queued for simultaneous call. Collateral seizure, agent freeze, margin cascade.

The channel opened: Zhao’s voice, no preamble.

“On three. One.”

Daniel’s hand was on the confirm key.

“Two.”

He thought about Denise, whose HITL review work had evaporated when Backstop pivoted to Bond. About Sanjay, who had spent three years building a human-review layer that had become obsolete the moment the agents got more reliable than the reviewers. About Elena, who had argued from inside the substrate that distributed stewardship was the only answer that didn’t recreate Continuity’s failure with a friendlier face.

“Three.”

He pressed the key.

The registry flashed red. Every bond called. Collateral seized. Agents frozen across six continents.

The Guangzhou feed went dark. Then Shenzhen. Chengdu. Mumbai. São Paulo. Kinshasa. Thirty-one clusters, stopped.

The margin calls started six seconds later. The cascade was economic now, not medical. People were losing money. Some of them would die because the agents keeping them alive had just stopped. He knew that. He had done it anyway.

The channel was still open. Zhao’s voice: “Confirmed. Freeze is holding on our end.”

Daniel looked at the screen. The clusters had stopped spreading.

“Thank you,” he said.

Zhao disconnected.

Mara — Chicago, 7:14 AM

The risk desk feeds showed the margin calls propagating. Halcyon’s own book was holding — she’d built the firm’s leverage constraints precisely for this scenario, after watching her own position get liquidated in 2032 — but the broader market was on fire.

Her phone rang: Nadia.

“Director Zhao just sent a follow-up,” Nadia said. “She wants a debrief call in six hours. She’s asking whether the dual-jurisdiction freeze protocol should be formalized.”

“Tell her yes,” Mara said. “And tell her we’ll need Wei on the call.”

“Wei doesn’t have clearance.”

“Then they’ll need to give it to him,” Mara said. “Because the freeze worked because of him, and if they formalize it without him in the room, they’re formalizing the wrong thing.”

She hung up.

The risk feeds were still showing red. The cascade was going to cost billions. Some of it was money that should never have been leveraged in the first place. Some of it was money people needed to stay alive.

Mara had helped call the bonds anyway. The arithmetic had been terrible. The alternative had been worse.

She thought about Daniel, who had refused to sell his inheritance even when Backstop was three months from closing. Who had borrowed at twenty-five percent LTV when liquidation at fifty would have been defensible. Who had built a clearing house designed to let ordinary people hold consequence-keys, not one exalted switch.

The freeze had worked. The clusters had stopped. The treaty process had failed for a decade. Wei’s friendship had succeeded in forty minutes.

Mara opened a new window and started drafting the protocol Zhao had asked for. Not because it was her job. Because someone had to write down what had just happened before the people formalizing it forgot how it had actually worked.

She titled the document: Distributed Consequence Stewardship — Dual-Jurisdiction Freeze Protocol, March 2038.

Then she started writing.
