Chapter 15: Still a Believer

The dashboard had a new color on it by the time Victor got to his desk that morning, a pale amber wedge in the corner pie chart that hadn’t existed in any training material he’d ever sat through: HOUSEHOLD RISK INDICATOR - ELEVATED REGION. Underneath it, in the smaller font the Bureau used for things it wanted read but not dwelt on, a single line: Flag volume up 340% month-over-month, correlated with regional layoff announcements.

Victor had been at the Bureau eight years now, deep enough into 2029 that most days felt like 2028 with worse traffic. He had never seen a color get added to that chart before this year. He had seen categories quietly retired, merged, renamed into something less alarming. Never the opposite.

His personal brief, the same class of assistant half the floor now kept open beside the official dashboard, had already summarized the amber wedge, ranked three plausible task-force compositions, and drafted a reply to Deb he hadn’t asked for. He closed it without reading the reply. The discomfort was small and specific: the second brain on his desk was the same species as the thing he regulated.

“You’ve seen the memo,” Deb said, appearing at his cubicle the way she always appeared, without any of the throat-clearing other managers used.

“I’ve seen a chart. Nobody’s sent me a memo yet.”

“You will by ten.” She leaned against the partition, arms crossed, in the posture she used for conversations she’d rather not be having standing up. “Three plants in the Midwest went to skeleton crews inside a month. Two more announced this week. People who just lost a paycheck are exactly the people a certain kind of scam artist has always gone looking for.

“Fake relief funds. Fake retraining grants. A convincing-looking nonprofit that’s actually somebody’s cousin’s shell account. We’re standing up a task force. I want you on it.”

“Why me specifically?”

“Because you’re good at telling the difference between somebody trying to help and somebody trying to take.” She paused, and something in her face did the thing it did when she was about to say a sentence she’d already rehearsed.

“Also because of your mother. I’d rather have somebody who feels it than somebody who’s only ever read a case file.”

He said yes. It was the same decision he made most Mondays, just wearing a bigger uniform.

The unrest itself, when he let himself actually watch the footage instead of just reading the summary above it, was smaller and sadder than the word suggested. Not the burning-city footage from a decade of other countries’ news cycles. Mostly just people standing in parking lots outside plants that weren’t hiring anyone back, holding signs somebody had clearly made in a hurry, blocking a road until a municipal drone told them, in a voice recorded once and reused everywhere, that the gathering was unpermitted.

Nobody in the footage looked dangerous. Everybody looked like they’d run out of other ideas.

The task force turned out to be four analysts and a shared folder, which was smaller than the word task force had led him to expect. The model doing the actual flagging was new, trained fast, tuned aggressive on purpose. Better to over-flag and let a human sort it out than let real fraud through while everyone was still calibrating.

Victor’s queue, by Wednesday, had gone from eleven cases a day to forty. The ratio of real problems to ordinary people doing an ordinary generous thing had not changed at all.

A woman named Alicia Byun had set up a fund for seven former coworkers from a shuttered auto-parts plant outside Toledo, three weeks’ rent apiece, transparent, itemized, exactly the kind of thing a decent person organized because nobody else was going to. The model had flagged it for “rapid disbursement to unverified recipients, pattern consistent with structured fraud.” Victor cleared it in four minutes. A payroll list from the plant itself matched every name.

He thought four minutes was the fast version of this job, the version where the human still got there in time. He didn’t know how many of the other thirty-nine cases had waited longer. He knew, because he’d done the math on his own hours against the corner chart, that the answer for the Bureau as a whole was not four minutes.

Case thirty-one that same afternoon was the other kind, the kind the task force had actually been stood up to catch. Four different relief funds, different names, different plant towns, different stock photos of different exhausted men, all routing two layers down to the same beneficiary account. The pattern would have taken a person a week to notice by hand. The model had taken six seconds.

Victor read it three times before he let himself believe it. He’d gotten so used to reading a flag as a mistake waiting to be found that a true one, when it actually showed up, felt like it needed extra checking just to be trusted.

There wasn’t a mistake in it. He wrote up the pattern, attached the four pages and the shared account number, and routed it to Financial Crimes. Not his own desk, not once it stopped being merely suspicious and became confirmed.

Somebody in a different building with actual subpoena power would take it from there. His part ended with a case number and a timestamp. It felt almost like relief. One confirmed catch, out of a week’s worth of wrongful freezes, proof the apparatus wasn’t only ever wrong in the same direction.

He thought, clearing case after case, of a sentence he’d said once: a model that’s right ninety-nine times out of a hundred is, at scale, wrong for millions of people a year, and somebody has to be the one who looks at the hundredth case.

Somewhere past case twenty that Wednesday, doing the arithmetic on his own queue against the queue backing up behind Marcus’s name on the shared board, Victor understood for the first time that the sentence had a second half he hadn’t said out loud: and if the hundredth cases come in faster than there are people to read them, promising somebody will look is a promise nobody can keep.

The clearance paperwork for the task force came with a form he hadn’t seen before: HOUSEHOLD CONFLICT AFFIRMATION. It asked him to reconfirm, in writing, that no member of his own household currently had an open flag.

He thought of Rosa’s file before he’d finished reading the sentence. Related party, disclosed. Still there, two years later, exactly where Deb had filed it the day he’d walked it down the hall.

“It’s not a problem,” Deb said, when he brought it to her instead of just signing it, because he’d learned a long time ago that the version of honesty that protected him was the version that happened before anyone asked. “Her file’s clean. Recert went through fine last cycle. I checked before I put your name forward. Didn’t want you finding out during onboarding that there was a wrinkle.”

“You checked my mother’s benefit file before you put me on this.”

“I checked whether putting you on this would create a problem for you. There’s a difference.” She said it plainly, and Victor believed her. He was aware, believing her, of how much easier it was to believe a kindness when the kindness also happened to keep him exactly where the Bureau needed him to be.

“That file being clean is part of why I trust you on this. I fought for you over two more senior names.”

He signed the form. Walking back to his desk, he told himself there was a real difference between being trusted because of what could go wrong for his mother and being controlled by it. He wanted that to be true. He just wasn’t sure anymore that wanting it was the same thing as it being so.

Rosa called that evening, which by now had stopped being unusual. She called most evenings, since the recert scare two years back, a habit neither of them had named but both of them kept.

“They’re saying things on the news about the protests getting bigger. Ohio, factory towns.” Her voice had the careful lightness of a woman trying not to sound as worried as she was. “You’re not near any of that.”

“I’m not near any of that, Ma. I sit at a desk.” He didn’t tell her the queue had four times as many names in it as a month ago. A fair number of those names belonged to people whose situation looked, on paper, close enough to hers that he sometimes had to sit for a second before opening the file. “How’s the new prescription? Pharmacy give you any trouble?”

“No trouble. You ask me that every time.”

“I like hearing no trouble.”

They hung up the way they always did.

He caught the fusion story on the break-room television two days later and stood there through the whole segment. It ran under a ticker carrying its usual rotation.

A ceasefire line somewhere in the Middle East holding for now, or not. A war in Ukraine so far past whichever anniversary people had stopped counting that the ticker just called it the front. Europe’s digital euro live in every member state: government money the government could freeze by category, cash treated as suspicious. Mining concessions in the Kesengo Corridor that the anchors were calling, carefully, a proxy conflict now. A government holding the rare-earth ground with Western security contractors. An insurgency across the ridge line supplied by Beijing-linked interests.

Neither government had used the word war about any of it, in so many words. Victor had come to understand, from two years of reading agency memoranda for exactly this kind of careful non-word, that they were all already treating it like one. He filed the connection away in the same place he filed most things that were somebody else’s war to worry about.

The reactor outside Oak Ridge was still on the loop a week after it broke, the first commercial plant to make more energy than it cost, on a containment design a research system had drawn up in nine unsupervised days. The anchor said people were already talking about desalination and farm calories, not just electricity. Too early for any of that. The reactor had only just crossed zero. But the anchor sounded almost surprised to be reporting something unambiguously good, and Victor guessed that was why the network hadn’t let the story go yet.

Marcus came in behind him, looked up at the screen, and said, “Huh. A nice one, for once,” in the tone of a man who’d learned to treat good news as a category that needed noting when it showed up.

“Enjoy it. We’ll be back to the other kind by lunch.”

“Devouring my optimism early today, I see.”

“I’ve got forty cases and a form that made me look up my own mother’s file. Optimism’s rationed.”

There was a woman on the screen a moment later, testifying in front of a Senate subcommittee about something called a safety treaty, coordinated frontier-lab audits. Her name flashed underneath: Dr. Miriam Voss.

Victor didn’t know who she was. He knew, watching her answer a hostile question without raising her voice, that he recognized the specific competence of someone who had spent a career being the only adult in a series of rooms that badly needed one.

He filed her name away the way he filed most things larger than his own queue, and went back to his desk when the segment ended.

He didn’t text Daniel that week either, though the thought crossed his mind twice. Once reading a case that reminded him of exactly the sentence he’d once tried to say to Daniel and deleted. Once just from tiredness, the kind that made an old friend’s name feel like the only conversation left that wouldn’t cost him anything.

The draft was still sitting where it had been for two years, unsent, hey, saw something, you good?, a sentence that had gotten less true and more true at the same time the longer he left it alone.

He cleared his last case a little after seven, a fund for a laid-off warehouse crew two towns over from Rosa’s, legitimate, itemized, cleared in six minutes. He sat for a moment in the quiet of an office that had mostly emptied out around him.

The amber wedge on the dashboard was smaller than that morning, not because the underlying number had improved, but because someone upstream had adjusted what counted as elevated. A small administrative mercy that made the chart look better without making anyone’s actual week easier. He filed it under temporary, probably, and told himself, turning off his monitor, that he still believed the word meant what it used to mean.
