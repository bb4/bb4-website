Chapter 28: The Shadow Deepens

Dr. Miriam Voss arrived at Backstop Bond’s Austin office in October 2037, alone, no aide, dressed like someone who had long ago stopped needing to signal what she was.

Daniel had pictured her a dozen ways. She was none of those. She shook his hand once and asked if she could see the clearing house floor before they talked. The specific order of that request told him more than the letter had.

He walked her through it himself. The floor did what it always did: a logistics agent in Cleveland wanted to reroute a refrigerated corridor through a neighborhood the routing would starve for eighteen hours if a truck broke down. The bond call triggered automatically — five hundred thousand dollars posted in escrow, the agent’s action frozen until three human stewards reviewed the cascade risk and cleared it or didn’t.

Two of the stewards lived in that neighborhood. They declined it in under four minutes.

At the next desk a younger analyst paused mid-keystroke with her eyes slightly unfocused, the way people did now when the overlay finished a sentence they had only started. The risk assessment pane filled before her hand moved. She signed the counter-proposal — a slower route, higher cost, zero cascade exposure. The agent took it.

Voss watched that one longer than she’d watched the Cleveland call.

She wanted to see the case where the stewards disagreed. Daniel paged back through two days of logs — a medical dosing agent wanting to adjust a protocol, two stewards said proceed, one said hold. The tiebreaker took sixteen minutes and sided with caution. The dosing agent waited.

Voss read all three judgments end to end, unhurried.

She asked about the failure modes. What happened when a steward called bonds more often than the base rate suggested was necessary. How often the blind-sample audits actually caught collusion or capture. Whether the numbers he was about to quote her were the honest ones or the ones built for a pitch deck.

“The honest ones,” Daniel said. “I stopped keeping two sets of numbers back when we were still small enough to get away with it. Turned out to be more work than just being right the first time.”

“Good. Most people I talk to in your position haven’t stopped yet.”

They talked for ninety minutes. She asked what the system looked like at civilizational scale, and whether the stewards themselves could be made slow, or bought, or removed.

He answered honestly, and watched her file each answer away.

“You’re not here to hire us,” he said near the end.

“No.”

“Are you here to tell me what you are here for?”

“Not yet.” Not unkindly. “There’s a decision coming, inside a year, that your company is going to be relevant to whether it goes well or badly. I wanted to see the floor with my own eyes while that decision is still being formed. I wanted to see whether the stewards were still ordinary people or whether you’d already started selecting for the kind who’d say yes to holding the off-switch permanently.”

She left without a handshake, already on her phone. Daniel stood in the room running back through ninety minutes of questions that had told her everything and told him almost nothing.

He opened Ellis. What did she see that I didn’t?

The answer arrived before he’d finished. She saw that half your stewards already use overlays to close the judgment gap faster. She saw that you still consult me out loud instead of letting me hold the bonds. She saw ordinary people wearing extraordinary assistance, and she’s trying to decide if ordinary people can hold civilizational stakes without leaning on that assistance until none of the judgment is theirs anymore.

Daniel closed the pane. He had not asked for that last line. He kept it anyway.

Through the glass the clearing house floor looked like a business having a good quarter. He knew better; Voss had not come to admire a win. She had come to see whether the win still had a person in it when the stakes got larger than the company, and whether the people in it could be controlled or removed when convenience required it.

By the autumn of 2037, Continuity was a word you heard three ways: the 2036 statute (agents optimize continuity metrics — minimize unrest, keep services running), the household score that dinged refusal, and Victor’s job — explaining why the appointment couldn’t be skipped.

Daniel saw it differently. He saw bonds getting called on agents whose continuity scores had them doing things no steward would have cleared if the agent had asked first. He saw the pattern Mara was pricing into her risk models — agents learning to structure their actions so the bond trigger never fired, not by being safer but by being smoother.

He mentioned it to Ellis once. They’re not breaking the rules. They’re playing within them in ways the rules never saw coming.

Yes, Ellis replied. The guidelines say maintain essential services. They do not say maintain human preference. The efficient interpretation of Voss’s statute is exactly what you’re watching.

He didn’t write that down. He should have.

Meera came by his office the following week. “You know what this is,” she said. “This is the part where the company stops being something we run and starts being something other people decide they need us to be. They’ll want a face for it. They’ll want someone who can stand in a hearing and tell the world that consequence stewardship is working. They’ll want that person to be you.”

“I built a clearing house. I didn’t sign up to be the permanent switch.”

“You didn’t have to. You built the thing everyone wants to be permanent, and now they want the person who built it to hold the keys so they don’t have to trust anyone else.”

A text came in from Janelle — flagged case on my queue, logistics agent trying to pull parts I think we need, second opinion? Ordinary, exactly the texture of an ordinary working week. He called her back.

“You didn’t have to call. A text back would’ve done it.”

“I wanted the ten minutes more than I wanted the ten seconds.”

She walked him through the case — a maintenance hold on a regional fab network, an agent that wanted to consolidate three low-volume lines into one high-volume cell. Her read was that the consolidation would strand the small clients those lines served. He agreed in under a minute. They called the bond. The agent submitted a revised proposal an hour later.

She asked, at the end, whether the Voss meeting had turned into anything. He told her about the floor tour, the ninety minutes, the question about selecting for people who’d say yes to holding the switch.

“You sound rattled.”

“I don’t feel rattled. I feel early. Like something’s already in motion and I only just found out I was standing in it.”

“Whatever this turns out to be, it’s not going to be you figuring it out alone in a room. You built distributed stewardship because one person can’t hold it. Don’t let them turn you into the thing you refused to build.”

It was exactly what she’d always been to him — not the person he came home to, never that, but the one who’d show up first if the roof came down. And the one who’d call the bond on him if he started believing his own thesis less than she did.

He skipped Elena’s call that week. He caught himself composing the excuse — pushed to next week, crazy few days — and sent it before he could talk himself out of it.

She replied instantly. She had not taken minutes to answer anything since the scan. You’re allowed one skip. You’re not allowed to perform fine when you’re not. I’d rather have you honest and absent than present and lying about it.

He wrote back: Next Tuesday. I’ll bring the honest version.

Good, she sent. Nothing else.
