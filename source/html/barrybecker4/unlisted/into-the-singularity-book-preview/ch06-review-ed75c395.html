Chapter 6: Unfinished Sentence

The call connected at 9 p.m. Beijing time, which made it seven in the morning for Daniel in Austin.

It had been four years since Wei left the university lab for a government research institute in Beijing and put half a planet between them.

He still felt a small private satisfaction every time the math worked out to evening for him and morning for someone else, as if he had solved time zones himself.

His wife, Lin, called it his one remaining graduate-school habit: being smug about arithmetic. She wasn’t wrong. She’d said it fondly enough, on her way out to pick up their daughter from her grandmother’s, that Wei hadn’t bothered defending himself.

Daniel’s face resolved out of the pixelation a beat after the audio did, same as always, in the kitchen Wei had never seen in person and had somehow still learned to recognize by the particular angle of light through one window in the mornings. The light looked different to Wei this time, flatter, like a man who’d been home for a funeral and hadn’t entirely made it back yet.

“You’re doing the thing where you smile before you say hello,” Daniel said. “You’ve been doing that since the lab. I’ve never figured out what it’s about.”

“It’s about you being upside down and it not being midnight for you for once.”

“Seven a.m. is not upside down.”

“For you it’s practically noon. You were the only person in that lab who came in after ten and left before the rest of us had eaten dinner.”

“I had a system,” Daniel said, defending a system that had not, in the end, produced a finished dissertation. They both knew it and had long since stopped needing to say it out loud.

Wei had finished. Daniel had taken the master’s and the job offer and never quite apologized for it. Wei had never needed him to. Daniel built software that kept real warehouses running. That seemed at least as respectable as the seven years Wei had spent proving an ever-narrower fact about optimization problems almost nobody had heard of.

They talked for a while about nothing that would survive being written down. A professor of theirs, Lindqvist, had retired somewhere in Portugal and sent Wei a postcard every few months with increasingly implausible claims about the local wine. They also had a running argument about whether a grad-school bar in the city where they’d met had closed or merely changed its name for tax reasons.

Wei’s daughter was four now. She had recently decided that the correct response to any instruction was a single devastating “why?” She delivered it with the confidence of someone who knew that many instructions stopped making sense if you questioned them long enough.

“She’s going to run a country someday,” Daniel said. “Or overthrow one.”

“At this rate, by next week.” Wei let himself laugh, properly, the kind that came out before he could shape it into something more presentable.

“She asked me last week why the sky. Not why the sky was blue. Why the sky, as a concept. I told Lin we’d raised a philosopher and Lin said no, we’d raised someone who was going to be exhausting at every dinner party for the rest of her life. I said those were the same thing, and we argued about it the way we argue about everything, which is to say happily and pointlessly until one of us gets hungry.”

“That tracks. You argued with Lindqvist for forty minutes once about whether a proof was elegant or just short. I still don’t know who won.”

“Nobody won. That’s what made it a good argument.” Wei smiled at the memory of it. Lindqvist at the whiteboard, delighted, refusing to referee, letting two exhausted grad students burn an entire afternoon on a question with no correct answer. He’d understood, even then, that the arguing was the actual education and the proof itself was almost beside the point.

“How’ve you been?” Wei said, when the easy part of the conversation thinned — not small talk this time, an actual question.

Daniel felt the whole Ohio week rise in his throat: the mill, the church, Carol Weiss reading an inheritance he still couldn’t picture as money, the Friday call and efficiency gains landing on his father’s kitchen table like a second kind of death in the same week.

“Complicated,” he said. “My dad died. I got laid off the same week. I’m — handling it.” The last part was true only in the sense that it let the call keep going.

Wei went quiet for a beat. “I’m sorry. About your dad.”

“Yeah.” Daniel didn’t offer the layoff any further than that unless asked. He wasn’t sure he could say it out loud to someone this far outside it without making it real again. “Keep going,” he said. “The stupid part. I don’t want to go back to my own week yet.”

This call was usually the best twenty minutes of Wei’s week. Even on vacation, he acted fine so nobody would worry. With Daniel, who had known him before either of them learned to do that, he didn’t have to.

Because he relaxed, he said more than he meant to. For about four seconds, he forgot there were things he was not supposed to say.

“How’s the new building, by the way?” Daniel asked. “You mentioned you got moved.”

“Bigger. Better cafeteria. Worse parking.” He heard himself trim the answer down before he’d consciously decided to trim it. No mention of which district. No mention of the name stenciled on the lobby glass that he’d stopped saying out loud to anyone outside work sometime in the last few months without quite noticing when the habit had started.

Daniel, who had no reason to expect more than that, took the small answer at face value and moved on to asking whether the cafeteria thing was going to become a whole bit, the way everything with Wei eventually did.

It was such an easy, practiced little omission that Wei didn’t register it as one until later. The bigger one, twenty minutes on, he caught in real time.

“Work’s been strange otherwise,” he said, as he might have said it any of the last hundred times they’d talked. “There’s this whole new review process now for anything that touches the —” He heard himself say it before he’d decided to. The sentence was already halfway out.

He stopped.

Daniel waited, the way he always waited, unbothered, assuming the pause was just Wei hunting for an English word, which it usually was.

“—for anything that touches procurement,” Wei said. Procurement was technically part of it, but it was not what he had meant to say.

“Procurement,” Daniel repeated, mildly, in the tone of a man who had heard worse workplace complaints. “God, I miss having a job where procurement was the worst thing wrong with it.”

“You have no idea,” Wei said, and let a small laugh do the work of closing the subject. Underneath the laugh, something dropped a half degree colder in his chest. Not because Daniel had noticed anything — he was fairly sure Daniel hadn’t. But because he had noticed, in real time, the exact moment his own mouth had swerved around a sentence his mind had not authorized it to say.

That kind of reflex, he understood with the clean, unhappy clarity of someone watching a symptom appear on cue, did not develop in a person by accident. It developed because a person had been practicing it, somewhere below the level where they noticed themselves doing it, for a while now.

He glanced, without meaning to, at the door of the room he was sitting in — a habit he’d have denied having, a week ago, if anyone had suggested he had it — and then back at the screen.

Daniel was now describing, with the enthusiasm of someone who genuinely loved a well-solved technical problem, an incident from his Meridian days in which two programs had tried to update the same shipment at once. It was a story with a beginning, a middle, and an ending: a problem one person was allowed to explain fully to another.

Wei envied him that more than he let himself examine.

He thought, unbidden, of Han, two desks down from his own until three weeks ago. Whose absence nobody in the office had explained and nobody had asked about twice. A badge that stopped working one Monday. A nameplate gone by Wednesday. A story everyone seemed to agree, without ever actually agreeing out loud, was probably nothing. Probably a transfer. Probably the kind of ordinary churn any institute this size produced.

Wei did not know what had happened to Han. He had noticed, with some discomfort, that he had quietly stopped being the kind of person who asked.

“You still there?” Daniel asked, at some point Wei had lost track of. “You went somewhere.”

“Sorry. Long week.” True, technically, same as procurement had been true. He was getting good at answers like that. “Tell me about the batch scheduler again. I like it when your old job stories sound like something with an actual villain in it.”

“The villain was a stale cache. Genuinely evil. I respect it.”

They talked another ten minutes, easier ground the rest of the way. Lin’s mother’s opinions about the daughter’s diet. A mutual friend’s engagement neither of them could quite believe. Daniel’s vague promise to visit Beijing that Wei privately suspected, correctly, would not happen this year either, not with a house in Millbrook still technically for sale and a job search he kept finding reasons to defer.

Then they hung up, the usual way, no ceremony — talk soon, tell Lin I said hi. Wei sat for a while in the quiet after, in the room whose door he had checked once already tonight without deciding to.

He thought about calling Daniel back and finishing the sentence properly, just to see what it would feel like to tell the truth to someone outside the institute.

He did not call.

He told himself, closing the laptop, that there was nothing yet worth finishing the sentence over. A badge system and a review process and a building with better locks than the old one added up, so far, to bureaucracy and not to anything Daniel would recognize as an actual villain.

Even as he told himself that, he heard the same unfinished sentence he’d been living inside all year.

He heard Lin’s key in the door a few minutes later, their daughter already asleep against her shoulder, worn out by whatever four-year-old philosophy had exhausted her that evening. He closed the laptop the rest of the way and got up to take some of the weight off his wife’s arms, and did not, that night or for a good while afterward, tell her about the sentence he hadn’t finished either.
