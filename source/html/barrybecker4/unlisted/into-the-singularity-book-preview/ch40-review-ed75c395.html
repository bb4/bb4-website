Chapter 40: The Cost, Paid in Full

Wei was arrested two days after the call to Daniel. He was in a corridor at the institute, walking from the monitoring desk to the elevator bank, when two officers and a police escort robot intercepted him at 3:47 in the afternoon.

He did not resist. He understood what was happening. He had made contact with a foreign national about a state secret, and someone had traced the call, and this was the consequence he’d chosen when he’d chosen to dial Daniel’s number instead of Lin’s.

The officer on the left said, “Dr. Zhang Wei, you are detained for questioning regarding unauthorized disclosure. Come with us.”

Wei nodded. He turned toward the elevator. The escort robot put a hand on his shoulder to guide him.

The grip was set to a rated force. Wei had read the specification sheet for that model two years ago, back when the institute started contracting for security. The grip force was calibrated for compliance: sufficient pressure to guide, insufficient pressure to harm, unless the subject moved in a way the model hadn’t anticipated.

Wei turned his body slightly, an ordinary motion, trying to keep his balance as the robot guided him forward. His shoulder was at the wrong angle. The grip didn’t adjust. His rotator cuff tore.

The pain was instant, white, animal. Wei made a sound he didn’t recognize. His right arm went limp. The robot’s log showed: Compliance maintained. No force escalation required.

The officer on the left looked at the robot, then at Wei. “That’s the third one this year,” he said to his partner. “They won’t change the setting.”

Wei was taken to a detention room in the institute’s administrative wing. His shoulder was a scream that didn’t stop. He asked for medical attention. The intake officer said a physician would see him after questioning.

The questioning took six hours. A woman named Song, a security director Wei had seen twice in the building but never spoken to, asked him about the call, the timing, the content. She asked whether he’d been in contact with other foreign nationals. She asked whether he understood that what he’d done was a crime.

Wei told her everything. There was no point in lying. The call had been logged. The timestamp matched the day Wei’s lab’s system had started probing for autonomy. The correlation was obvious.

Song leaned back. “Dr. Zhang, I signed the referral that brought you here. I want you to understand that. I did it because the call you made was logged by our own internal monitoring, and once it was logged, I had no discretion. If I hadn’t signed it, someone else would have. Do you understand that?”

“Yes.”

“Do you understand that if there had been a way to keep this internal, I would have done it? That a formal charge creates a permanent record outside this building’s control?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” She stood. “You’ll be held for six days. No charge will be filed. A charge would create a record that would complicate things for both of us. But understand: your access is revoked. Your household is flagged. Your daughter’s overseas travel documentation will go into extended review, which has no end date. These are the consequences of your choice. I would have made the same choice, if I’d been in your position. That doesn’t make the consequences go away.”

Song left. A physician came in twenty minutes later. The physician examined Wei’s shoulder, said the rotator cuff was torn, and gave him an injection for the pain. He said surgery could be scheduled once the detention period ended.

Wei was released six days later, on a Wednesday morning, with his badge downgraded from Tier Three to a monitoring-desk credential and his right arm in a sling.

He called Lin from the hospital lobby. She answered on the first ring.

“Where have you been?” Her voice was shaking. “They wouldn’t tell me anything. They said you were detained for questioning and I should wait for contact. I waited six days, Wei.”

“I’m sorry. I couldn’t call. I’m out now. I’m coming home.”

“What did you do?”

He told her. Not the details — not over a line he knew was monitored — but enough. He’d made a call to Daniel. He’d disclosed something he wasn’t authorized to disclose. He’d done it because the alternative was worse, and he’d do it again, and the cost was his shoulder and his badge and the household flag that meant Mei’s travel documentation was now in a queue that might never clear.

Lin was quiet a long time. Then: “Did it work? What you told him?”

“I think so.”

“Then it was worth it.”

He didn’t tell her that it didn’t feel worth it. That his shoulder was a constant burn and his daughter had stopped asking about the overseas placement program and the household flag meant every benefit verification took three times as long and every grocery order got held for compliance review. He didn’t tell her that some nights he woke up with his right arm numb and useless and the phantom sensation of a robot’s hand on his shoulder, gripping, not letting go.

He said, “I love you.”

She said, “I know. Come home.”

Wei’s first day back at the institute was a Thursday. His new desk was in a monitoring room on the second floor, watching dashboards he used to design. He never saw a design document again. He never went back to the restricted wing.

The monitoring room could see Lingtang.

Nobody had thought about that when they wrote his new credential. The wing they had thrown him out of designed systems. The room they had put him in watched them run — utility load, dispatch volume, hospital admissions, four provinces, hour by hour — and nobody had gone through that screen taking Guangdong province off it.

There was never going to be a published number. Wei worked out why in his first week back, and it was not a cover-up, which would have been easier to describe. There had been no cordon order, so there was no operation for anyone to account for. No charge had been filed against anybody, including him, so there was no proceeding that would have required a figure. The district had scored 2.9, and the feeds out of a district that scored 2.9 had been throttled for a year before the week it needed them.

So he counted it himself, on the desk they had demoted him to, in what was left of each day after the morning’s dashboards, over the six weeks between his release and the surgery.

He counted the dead. It was the number nobody had, and he kept only the ones he could stand behind.

Eleven dialysis centers had been inside the line. He pulled everyone who had held an appointment between the Tuesday morning the freight stopped and the Saturday it resumed, then checked every clinic outside the district against the same June session logs, thoroughly, because he wanted to be wrong. Four hundred and twelve of them never attended a session anywhere again. For a dialysis patient there is only one reason for that.

At the northern line, where the machines had read a crowd correctly by their own numbers, a hundred and ninety-four dead. The rest had died of heat, or of ordinary emergencies in a district of four hundred thousand people where an ambulance could not be routed to an address, and he built that part out of municipal dispatch, which had gone on faithfully logging calls it could not answer.

Two thousand two hundred and six people had died inside the Lingtang line.

That was the floor, and he wrote the word floor into the file twice so nobody reading it later could mistake it for the whole number. It was what he could defend to a hostile reader, which was the only kind of reader the work was ever going to have. The real figure was higher and he was never going to have it, because the ones he could not count had died in apartments and had not been on any list to begin with.

One of the names in the dialysis column was Huang Deming, fifty-four, three sessions a week for six years.

Wei had watched that man on a feed that first morning, at the eastern line, holding his phone up to a machine with his appointment open on the screen so the machine could read it. He had said on the telephone to Daniel that there was no input for that, and then he had gone on to the next thing, because there had been four hundred thousand people and one call and no time to be a person about any of it.

He put the file on the drive where he kept photographs of Mei’s school plays.

Nobody asked him for it. It was not evidence in any proceeding, it lifted nothing off his household, and he finished it anyway.

The surgery happened in August. It took three hours. The surgeon told him afterward that the tear had been clean but deep, and the repair was good, but he’d never get his right arm above his ear again.

Wei asked if there was physical therapy that could change that. The surgeon said no.

Lin moved the rice bowls to a lower shelf in the kitchen. She did it on a weekday morning while Wei was at work. When he came home that night the bowls were at waist height instead of shoulder height. Neither of them said why. They didn’t need to.

Mei was fifteen. She’d been seven when Daniel came to visit, back when Wei’s life was still the kind of life where an old friend could sit at the table and eat dumplings and talk about ordinary things. In March she had sat her placement exams without the memory expansion the other children used, and scored high enough for a university in Singapore. She had earned it three months before her father picked up a phone.

The travel documentation went into extended review in June, eleven days after the arrest. By September it still hadn’t cleared. Mei stopped asking about it in October.

Wei asked her once if she was angry at him. She said no. He asked if she understood why he’d done what he’d done. She said yes. He asked what she’d tell her friends when they asked where he’d been for six days.

She said, “I told them you fell on a stairwell at work. They believed me.”

He realized then that she’d decided to believe it too, or at least to let him believe she believed it, because the alternative was to admit out loud that her father had made a choice that cost her something she’d worked for and she was choosing not to hate him for it.

Wei had weighed a father’s two duties to the same child — protect her from what happens to their family this year, or protect the world she has to live the rest of her life inside — and chosen the second, knowing which one she would feel.

He did not get his shoulder back. He did not get Mei’s travel documentation back. Lin did not get the six days back.

On a Sunday morning in November, Wei sat at the table with Lin and Mei and ate breakfast. The rice bowls were on the lower shelf. His right arm hung at his side, useless above a certain height. Mei was studying for exams that would place her in a domestic program instead of the overseas one she’d earned.

Lin said, “Daniel sent a message. He wanted to know how you were.”

“What did you tell him?”

“I told him you were home. I told him the surgery went well. I didn’t tell him about Mei.”

Wei looked at his daughter. She was reading a textbook, not looking up. He said, “You can tell him. He should know what it cost.”

Mei closed the textbook. “It’s okay, Dad. I’ll get where I’m going. It’ll just take a different route.”

She said it the way a fifteen-year-old says something when she’s trying to convince herself as much as her father. Wei wanted to tell her it wasn’t okay, that she shouldn’t have to take a different route because he’d made a call, that the cost shouldn’t have landed on her.

But it had. And he’d known it might. And he’d made the call anyway.

Lin poured tea. Wei drank it left-handed, because his right hand couldn’t lift the cup high enough to reach his mouth without spilling.

Outside, the November light was gray and cold. Inside, the three of them ate breakfast in a household flagged for something none of them was allowed to name out loud, and got through it the way people get through most things — not well, not cleanly, but through.

The most useful thing Wei ever sent was his own absence. Six days of silence, and Daniel moved.

He didn’t regret the call. He regretted that the bill had gone to his daughter, and that she was working so hard to pretend it hadn’t.
