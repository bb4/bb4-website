Chapter 20: The Unwind

The first crewed lunar landing in sixty years happened on a Monday in the spring of 2032. For about thirty-six hours it was the only story anyone could hold in their head at once.

Mara watched the feed standing at her kitchen counter with a mug of tea she hadn’t touched. Four people in white suits crossed grey dust no human being had touched since 1972.

By Wednesday morning the headline was gone.

Congress had failed, overnight, to pass the borrowing authority that kept the federal government’s own payments moving. Treasury payments seized in stages. Rating agencies moved before the markets opened in New York.

By the time Mara reached Halcyon’s floor, the Moon feed had been pushed to a sidebar.

She texted Devon before the open.

Watching?

Watching. Don’t do anything. Give the open an hour.

The open didn’t stop at an hour. Equities went first, then real estate names. Bitcoin moved with them — not first, not alone, just in the same violent, indiscriminate company as everything else people had borrowed against.

By lunch it was down eleven percent from Monday’s close. Two regional banks had halted withdrawals for “operational review.”

By the close bitcoin was down nineteen percent. Devon called at 4:07.

“Say the number out loud.”

“Six hundred seventy-nine thousand.”

“And the cushion.”

“Thirteen percent left before you call me about margin.”

“Ridgeline put out a statement twenty minutes ago. They’re ‘reviewing covenant compliance.’ That’s the sentence a treasury company writes when it’s about to sell the thing its entire balance sheet is made of. If they dump into this, your thirteen percent becomes a different conversation before the close tomorrow.”

“Ridgeline’s been reviewing covenant compliance since Christmas. It’s theater.”

“It was theater at eight hundred thirty-eight. At six seventy-nine it’s math.”

Thursday morning two European spreads blew out before London opened. A mid-sized Asian sovereign that had been quietly rolling short-term paper for eighteen months missed a refinancing window.

By nine Chicago time the phrase sovereign debt crisis was on every terminal. The banks had started seeing withdrawal traffic their own models flagged as cascade risk.

Bitcoin was down twenty-four percent from Monday when Ridgeline’s second statement hit the tape at 10:41.

Not reviewing. Selling. A scheduled, disclosed, covenant-required liquidation over the next seventy-two hours.

Within seven minutes the price had dropped through six hundred thousand. Mara’s liquidation threshold sat at five hundred eighty-six thousand six hundred.

She watched it approach the way you watch a car approach a wall you’ve already done the math on.

Devon called at 11:03.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “You’re done. Collateral and borrowed exposure both. The coins, the loan, the position you built on top of the position. All of it.”

She sat very still at her desk and watched the number keep falling without her. The specific terror arrived — not the loss of the money, exactly; the quieter, older recognition underneath it. That she had not, after all, gotten out. The Kessler girl from the machine shop. Still. Again.

Farid found her twenty minutes later.

“My office. Now.”

She went. The Moon feed was still running, muted, on the television behind his desk.

“How much of it was yours?”

“All of the personal piece. None of it through the firm.”

“I told you last fall to keep the paperwork current.” He looked at her for a long time. “Go home, Mara. Don’t come in tomorrow. We’ll talk Monday about what still exists of your seat here.”

She stood. The rage arrived all at once — not at him, not even at Devon, but at the gap itself, the certainty that this was exactly what the Kessler girl would do and exactly where she’d end up. She picked up the ceramic mug from his desk and threw it at the wall. It shattered. Coffee ran down the paint.

Farid didn’t move.

“I’m done,” she said. “With this conversation. With pretending a crash this size makes anyone whole. With all of it.”

She left. He let her go.

Daniel heard about Ridgeline from Sanjay at Thursday noon. Backstop’s Round Rock office had the particular wrong quiet of a room full of people refreshing pages.

Full-reserve Bitcoin, no leverage, every catastrophic claim Backstop had ever underwritten. At Thursday’s crashed price the reserve ratio had compressed below the conservative standard Daniel had set when Bond went live. Still solvent. Still adequate by any honest measure. In a week when fractional and synthetic competitors were quietly collapsing, adequate was not what clients wanted to hear.

Sanjay stood in the doorway with Concord’s email open. Updated attestation or they pulled the renewal. Three other clients had forwarded the same template.

“We need to add reserves,” Daniel said. “Real coins. Prove we’re not Ridgeline.”

Ellis had already run it. Daniel turned the monitor.

Borrow 25% against the inherited 45.7 BTC. Buy ~11.4 BTC at crash prices. Restore the ratio. Loan stays safe even if the price falls another 75%.

“Dad’s coins,” Daniel said. “Twenty-five percent. Buy at the crash. Put them in the reserve. Not selling. Not amplifying. Adding coverage.”

Sanjay read it twice. “That’s what she did.”

“No. She borrowed to amplify gains on the way up. I’m borrowing to backstop claims on the way down. Different ratio. Different reason. Same asset, handled the other way.”

He wrote Elena before he called Mara. Thursday, not their standing Tuesday.

Borrowing against the coins. Not selling. Long story.

Tell me Tuesday. The whole thing. And call your sister first, not after.

He called Mara that night.

“I heard,” he said. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t. If you say I’m sorry in the voice people use when they mean I told you so and are too decent to say it, I’m going to hang up.”

“I wasn’t going to say I told you so.”

“I know. That’s worse, in a way.” She was quiet. “Farid sent me home. I broke his mug. Compliance is going to eat whatever the market didn’t. You were supposed to call me this quarter. About capital. I can’t be that call anymore.”

“I know. I’m calling to tell you what I’m doing instead.”

He told her. Twenty-five percent against Dad’s coins. Buy at the crash. Put the coins in the reserve.

“You’re giving yourself a seventy-five-percent drawdown cushion. I needed thirty percent and didn’t have it. That’s the trade I didn’t make in 2027. That’s the trade Dad made for fourteen years and you just made this morning.”

“I know.”

“Say the sentence. Out loud.”

“You weren’t wrong about Bitcoin. You were wrong about leverage.”

“Yes,” she said. “I’ve known since Devon made me say thirty percent out loud last fall. I just thought knowing was the same thing as being protected by knowing.”

“Do the loan. Fund the reserves. Don’t make this week about me being a cautionary tale in your board deck. Just do the version of it I didn’t.”

She hung up first. He signed the custody documents and initiated the wire.

Within a month Backstop Bond was the only full-reserve bonding platform to survive the crash without external rescue. Clients stopped pulling renewals. New ones signed up because Backstop had weathered it. The boring structure became the industry standard because the crash killed everything else.

He did not sell a single inherited coin. The loan was never in danger.

The text from Carol Weiss came in late Thursday night.

Ray Delgado filed to buy Kessler this afternoon. Option from the will, five years due. He closes next week. Shop’s already empty — cheaper robots and the stimulus finished the floor after the crash. He bought the building and the billing. Not the people.

Daniel read it twice, then forwarded it to Mara without a note.

Mara read the forward standing at the same counter where she’d watched Brazil and Argentina and the Moon. She thought about Ray buying a machine shop that no longer employed people, that billed its remaining work in Bitcoin, that hummed along without the bodies that used to make it mean something.

She typed back to Daniel.

Good for Ray.

Yeah, he wrote. Good for Ray.

She set the phone face down. Somewhere in Millbrook, a man who had never once borrowed against a conviction to make the conviction larger was signing papers on an empty shop. She turned the laptop off and let the quiet be quiet, and for the first time in five years did not confuse the quiet with safety.
