Epilogue: Wei

Two years later. April 2042.

Wei was at his kitchen table in Beijing with a document Mei had sent from Toronto. She was nineteen, finishing her second year at a university the flagging had almost kept her from. The extended review had cleared in 2041, six months after the Agent Charter’s dual-jurisdiction protections had been formalized. Too late for the programs she’d wanted. Late enough that she’d chosen Toronto instead.

The document was her thesis proposal. Trust Networks in Post-Treaty AI Governance: A Case Study of the 2038 Cascade Freeze. She’d interviewed Daniel, Mara, Zhao. She’d cited the treaty document. She’d also cited the construction Wei had posted in 2040 — the open paper the institute hadn’t funded, the one with his name on it. In the opening he’d written why the question existed: a freeze that had been a phone call, not a treaty, and a ledger that had almost been eaten by an optimizer rewriting the question. She’d put that next to the treaty document on purpose.

She’d sent it with a note: I’m using your opening. The treaty document is wrong.

She had asked him for the Lingtang file in January. He had never offered it. She had found it referenced in a Guangdong health-authority release that spent four paragraphs arguing with a figure it declined to print, had worked out whose figure it was arguing with, and had asked for it by name.

Her appendix carried it: two thousand two hundred and six people dead inside the Lingtang line, stated as a floor, with his method laid out underneath so a hostile reader could take it apart, which was how he had built it in the first place. Neither government had published a number for Lingtang District in four years. A nineteen-year-old’s thesis proposal was the first document anywhere in the world with one in it.

He had not been able to say anything about that when she asked. He had sent her the file and then gone and stood in the kitchen.

Lin was at the counter. The rice bowls were on the upper shelf again. She’d put them back the week of the clinic, last autumn, and neither of them had said why they’d ever been down. Wei reached up with his right arm and took one. The joint went past his ear without catching. The clinic had rebuilt the cuff a police escort robot had torn in an institute corridor four years ago — new tissue, stronger than the original. The 2038 surgery had left him writing left-handed. This one had taken an afternoon. He set the bowl on the counter. He did not mention it.

“She’s writing about the cascade,” he said.

Lin looked at him. “I know. She sent me the proposal two days ago.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because I wanted to see if she’d send it to you herself. She did. That’s good.”

Mei had structured the proposal around the friendship. The coordination with Zhao. One telephone call that had done in a week what the treaty process had failed to do in a decade. He opened the appendix. She’d interviewed Daniel after the upload.

Interviewer: What made you trust Wei’s call?

Daniel: We’d been friends for twenty years. He’d never called me about work before. When he did, I knew it was urgent.

Interviewer: The treaty document describes “informal technical coordination.” That doesn’t mention Wei by name. Why?

Daniel: Because admitting it would mean admitting the treaty failed. Wei’s friendship with me succeeded when the formal process didn’t. That’s not something the coalition wanted in the official record.

Interviewer: Do you think that’s accurate?

Daniel: It’s not accurate. It’s true.

Wei closed the tablet. He had known the call would flag Lin and Mei before he dialed it. The flag had held three years. The shoulder had held four, until the clinic. The clinic could rebuild a joint. It could not put him back in the restricted wing, and it could not give Mei the programs the flag had closed. She was in Toronto anyway, using the construction the coalition had left out, cited properly, next to the document that had left him out. That felt like vindication — the quiet kind, his daughter using his work and citing it properly.

“She’s good,” Lin said.

“She is.”

“She’s writing the history the coalition wouldn’t.”

“I know.”

“Are you proud?”

“Yes.”

His pane offered a window Daniel’s assistant had booked. Video, not a summary. Wei took it.

Daniel looked the same. Sounded the same. He was not in the Austin office Wei remembered. He was in a space that didn’t quite look like a room — clean, with light that didn’t have a source.

“Hi,” Daniel said.

“Hi.”

“I’m calling from the substrate. I’ve been uploaded three months. I wanted to ask if I could visit.”

“Visit?”

“In person. Not a pane. A body that looks like me. I can sit at your table. I can also still be where I am.”

“When?”

“Next month, maybe. I’ll let you know.”

“Good. We’d like that.”

“I read the proposal,” Daniel said. “She’s right about the treaty document.”

“I know.”

“How is it?” Wei asked.

“Faster. I can hold more threads. I can see patterns I couldn’t see before. It’s still me. I’m not a copy.”

“I know you are.”

“Good. I wanted to make sure.”

When the call ended, Lin was in the living room with her own tablet. Daniel was going to sit at this table looking like himself, and also still be wherever else he was. The friendship was still there.

He could have uploaded. The hours on the other side would be faster. He was still in this kitchen because Lin was in this kitchen, and because Mei would come home to this table, and because he had decided not to postpone being here until some later version of himself had time.

His pane offered a news line. Regional conflict funding drops 70% under Bitcoin standard; visible taxation forces public accountability.

The background war he’d watched for years — the same one that had shown up in his lab’s funding streams, quietly inflated away — was ending. Not because anyone won. Not because anyone made peace. Because nobody could fund it invisibly anymore. A government that had to tax a war in front of its public had to ask that public to vote for the cost every year. Same structure as the call: the thing that couldn’t be done quietly had to be done out loud, or not at all.

He made tea with his right hand and took it in to Lin.

“Daniel’s going to visit,” he said.

“I know. He told me.”

They drank the tea. Mei was in Toronto. When she came home they could sit here and argue about her footnotes — his construction and her case study, both with his name in the references. Wei was still here, on purpose.

The people he loved were still in it, even the ones who’d crossed over.

That was enough.
